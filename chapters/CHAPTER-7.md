I'm standing in a library, in front of an elderly woman at a desk who guards, like it's the last clean water in the city, the rights to a bank of twenty-some desktop computers the public is allowed to touch.

About four of them sat open. The rest were filled, and I took the room in the way you take in any room you walk into broke and desperate — fast, and looking for the angle. Most of the chairs held men who looked the way I looked, or close to it: down to their last good idea, wearing the week on their clothes. A couple of elderly folks pecked away one finger at a time, leaning into the screen like it might whisper back. Nobody talked. It wasn't the polite hush of a library, that *shh* you grew up under. It was the silence of a waiting room where everybody's there for a different bad reason and nobody's fool enough to ask. A whole row of people the city had quietly stopped keeping a seat for, clinging to the one free thing it still handed out, and not one of them making a sound about it. After years of Vegas floors — bells, transfers, money getting made out loud — the quiet of that place landed on me like weather.

Funny thing is, she never even asked me for an ID. Never asked. All that — the eleven miles, the napkin maps, the DMV, a woman folding a twenty into my paperwork so the State of Alaska would agree in writing that I was a person — and the librarian didn't want to see a lick of it. Just let me know there was a 45-minute limit. I already knew 45 wasn't going to be near enough. I also knew there was nothing to do about it standing there, so I filed it with everything else and sat down.

First task: check my bank account. See how much of me was left out there in some stranger's pocket. No dice. The bank wanted to text a code to the phone I no longer owned. Of course it did. Fuck. That would have to wait — and anyway the damage was long done by now. Four days is a lifetime with a man's debit card. I wasn't going to claw any of it back from a library chair. I let it go.

Next: Facebook. Which, for the uninitiated, is its own small miracle to get into without your phone, because the second you try to log in from a strange machine the whole thing slams shut like a bank vault and demands to text you a code — and it'll almost never just email you instead. Google's no better; same trick, same trap. Lose your phone and you're more or less locked out of your own life until you can get that number back in your hand. I knew the drill cold. This was maybe the hundredth time I'd run it — years of living with Alisha, plus my own private talent for losing a phone somewhere between the first drink and the last. I knew exactly the kind of wall I was about to put my shoulder into.

And then, through some genuine back-alley miracle, I got in. Facebook, for reasons known only to God and its algorithm, decided this once to email me the code instead of text it. Google, asked to confirm it was really me, let me verify through another email I could still reach. One little door swung that should've stayed bolted, and I was through. It wasn't even hard in the end. It just cost me — twenty of my forty-five minutes, gone, watching the wheel spin. But I was in.

I fired off a message to the only person I had left to fire it to: the oldest of my three sisters, Niki.

Niki was always the one. The one who showed up. Out of everybody I came up with, she's the only one who was really *there* for any of it — at my games, in the stands, recording the whole thing on a VHS camcorder so I could come home and watch exactly how bad I was. I wasn't bad. I just also wasn't filling those bleachers with scouts. Didn't matter to Niki. She taped it like it was the state final every time.

So she was the first call. She'd always been the first call — for a loan, for a ride, for the kind of trouble you can only confess to one person. Over the years that number got rung more than a handful of times, and almost every time, if she had it, Niki came through.

She wrote back almost the second I hit send. And I was in luck, because she'd just gotten paid. Niki's a courtroom transcriber — has been forever — which means she never quite knows when the money's landing or how much it'll be. There's always a pile of it owed to her somewhere out in the world, trickling in a little at a time, on nobody's schedule but its own. And she carries the same curse I do, the same hole in the pocket: money shows up, money leaves. Catch her the wrong week and she's as broke as I am. Catch her on payday and she's an open hand. This time I needed more than the fifty bucks that usually patched me — and this time, thank God, I'd caught her on payday.

She wasn't surprised. Not by a single word of it. Niki had seen this exact movie too many times, with me in the starring role and her cast, again, as the one who wires the money in the third act. No lecture, no *how did you* — just straight to logistics. How to get it to me. Where I could stand to pick it up. We're heads-down in the details, the two of us hammering out the mechanics of saving my life one message at a time, when —

A tap on my shoulder.

Not the old woman. An old man. Her muscle, I figured — the enforcer she sends when the clock runs out and the desperate don't move. My time was up, he says. I tell him I'm sorry, I just need a few more minutes. He says he's sorry too, but that's not how it works. I can come back tomorrow.

Tomorrow. I had no tonight. There was no tomorrow-me with a roof to sleep under and a fresh forty-five minutes to spend.

So I stood up. And I looked that old man dead in the eye, and I gave it to him flat, no heat, just the facts laid down one on top of the other like bricks. Look. I was beaten and robbed of everything I own. Then I got taken to jail for breaking into the Airbnb I was paying to rent — because they took my key too. I sat in a cell four days while somebody out there ran free with my debit card. I have no money. I just walked here from the DMV with this paper ID — which, turns out, I didn't even need to use this computer. And right now, this minute, I am working out the only way I have left to get money sent to me by my family. I need a few more minutes.

Then I sat back down and kept typing, like he'd already left.

And I felt it — that thing where a person's presence just lifts off your back. He was gone. I kept my eyes on the screen and braced for the next move, for security, for a hand on my arm. It never came. Nobody came. The clock turned into a suggestion, and I closed my deal.

Niki found it: a Harris Teeter inside two miles of the library that ran Western Union. Perfect. All they'd need was an ID — and I had one, the paper kind, but one all the same. Check. She sent five hundred dollars. Five hundred. Said it was ready to pick up.

Thank fucking God.

I walked out of that library lighter than I'd walked into it, and of course — *of course* — it was raining now. But I decided to take even that as a gift. A free shower. A chance to rinse four days of jail off my skin and maybe sluice a little of this rotten luck down the gutter with it. Twenty, thirty minutes of walking and I'm at the Harris Teeter.

Western Union lives at the customer-service counter, which means it shares a window with every other small catastrophe in the store. And in front of me is a man trying to return a lamp. No receipt. The lamp is snapped clean in two — broken right where the socket you screw the bulb into meets the pole, just *gone*, hanging by a wire — and this man is not giving up. Not a chance. He's going to win this. The clerk has to go find her manager. I'm standing there soaked, with five hundred dollars and the rest of my life thirty feet away behind a register, watching a grown man litigate a corpse of a lamp, and it eats up another thirty minutes I'll never see again.

Finally. The counter. I give them my name and Niki's name and lay my paper ID faceup, right next to the broken lamp the man before me had abandoned, along with the outcome he'd hoped for — exactly what she'd told me I'd need. And the clerk says, oh, no — we need a confirmation number, or some such thing.

Fuck my life.

No confirmation number, no money. And there was no arguing my way around this one — this wasn't a desk clerk I could outlast with a story. This was a number that either existed in my hand or didn't, and it didn't. How in the hell had neither of us caught it? Niki's got the slip in front of her, I'm standing right at the window, and between the two of us we'd built the whole bridge and forgot to nail down the last plank.

So. Back out into the rain. The thirty-minute walk back to the library — to beg, again, for a computer. Except now I'm walking straight back into the same elderly woman at the same desk. The one who just watched me get told my time was up and answer by sitting back down and typing through it. The second she sees me she's already shaking her head: come back tomorrow.

And here's the thing — this time I genuinely needed her. The first round I could bull my way through; there was an open machine and an old man who blinked first. But the library was full now, every chair taken, and you don't get on one of those computers without a fresh code, a unique passcode she generates from behind that desk. I couldn't exactly rip a logged-in stranger out of his seat. I needed *her*, on my side, by choice.

So I pled it. Straight, and small. Look — I am going to be sleeping outside tonight if I don't get on there for the passcode to my own money. Two minutes. That's all. And then, God help me, I reached into my pocket and offered her everything in it: two dollars. Two soft dollar bills, the change from the DMV woman's twenty, held out across the desk like I was tipping her — like she could take it down to bingo later, put it on a card, have herself a little night. I tried to bribe a librarian with two dollars.

She looked at me. And she gave in — but she didn't take the two bucks. Wouldn't touch it. Just generated the code and waved me through.

I got back on, got the confirmation number off Niki, who said — oh, yeah, they did give me one of those — like it had been sitting there in plain sight the whole time. And it had. Couldn't be mad at her if I tried. She was footing the entire bill; she could forget a hundred numbers and I'd thank her for each one.

I tucked that confirmation number away like it was made of gold and walked it back through the rain to the Harris Teeter one more time, to a customer-service line that was, finally, blessedly empty. No lamp. No manager. Nobody between me and the window.

Things were looking up.

And that, right there, is exactly the moment to be careful — because in my experience, *things looking up* is the precise sound the house makes right before it reaches across the table to collect.

I laid the confirmation number flat on the counter and waited on the heavyset woman behind it — the same one from before, thirty-something, the soft, shut-in look of someone who'd found a register to hide behind — to start counting hundreds into my hand. She took the number. Then she said: ID.

And I actually felt good about it. *See,* I thought, *the DMV wasn't a waste after all.* Eleven miles and a stranger's twenty and a paper slip I'd cried over — all of it about to earn out, right here. I dug it out of my pocket and slapped it on the counter like I was laying down a winning hand.

She looked at it and said: oh — we can't take temporary IDs. It has to be an official one. I'm sorry.

Sorry my ass.

That same paper had been sitting on that same counter the round before. Maybe she hadn't gotten far enough into it last time to ask — the confirmation number had killed it before we got here — but it had been *right there*, faceup, in plain sight. You couldn't have told me that the last time I was standing right here? I said. I was coming apart now, I could feel it climbing my throat, and I clamped down on it and kept my voice level, because volume never helps. The wall doesn't hear you better if you yell.

I'm sorry, sir. There's nothing I can do.

Then she offered me the one thing she had: you could transfer the money into someone else's name, she said. Somebody with a proper ID. They could pull it out for you.

I don't know anyone, I said.

And it was the truth, the flat whole truth of it. I knew Steven, hours south and unreachable. I knew a sister a continent away who'd already done her part. I knew a man named Mike who thought he owned a treatment center. In this entire city, at this counter, in the rain, I did not know one single soul with a wallet and a name on a card.

I felt the old routine load itself up in my chest — *I was robbed, I was beaten, four days in a cell* — the speech that had moved an old man off me at the library a couple hours back. And I swallowed it. Just swallowed it whole. Because I knew this clerk, and I knew this counter, and I knew the speech would buy me exactly nothing here but the same soft *I'm sorry, sir* with my own desperation stink on it. You don't waste a closer's energy on a door that doesn't open. You find the door that does.

So I walked back out into the rain to think, and somewhere between the automatic doors and the curb, the idea arrived.

I'd stand right here, outside the Harris Teeter, and I'd offer anybody with a valid ID fifty dollars — fifty of Niki's five hundred — to let me wire the money into *their* name and walk it back out for me. Clean. Simple. Fifty bucks for ninety seconds and a flash of plastic. Hell, in Vegas I'd have had ten takers before I finished the pitch.

Except.

Except I need you to picture the man making that offer. Picture him standing under the overhang of a grocery store, soaked to the bone, four days of jail ground into a torn shirt, filthy shorts, a head wound gone the color of bad fruit, asking strangers — strangers loading bags into dry cars, strangers with somewhere to be — to please, just for a second, attach their name and their ID to five hundred dollars on his say-so. Everything the eye takes in screams the exact opposite of *trust me.* I didn't look like a man who needed an ID to claim five hundred dollars. I looked like the reason that woman behind the counter had a rule against men like me in the first place.

It was, as far as I could see, my only option. And it was about to be the hardest sale of my life.

The rain had let up into a drizzle, and I took even that as a gift, because it was the only kind I was being handed. I set up under the overhang and got to work on the first and hardest part of any sale — not looking like exactly what you are. I was trying not to look homeless. I was failing at it the way you fail at holding your breath: there's a clock on it, and the clock always wins.

So I did the one thing I know how to do. I worked the floor. Except the floor was a grocery-store parking lot and the marks were strangers in the rain, and I stood there reading every one of them as they came and went — who looked soft, who looked kind, who looked like the type to stop for a man who'd plainly had a week.

I started with my own kind. There were other men out front running some version of what I was running — parked on the curb, fishing the same crowd for the spare-change brand of mercy. Figured they'd understand the assignment. I walked up to the first one and opened.

"Hey — you got an ID on you? Because if you do, I've got a way for you to make fifty bucks in about two minutes. I got robbed, they took my ID. I've got money wired to me, Western Union, sitting right inside — but all I've got to show is this paper ID and they won't take it. You've got a real one, I put the money in your name, you walk in and pull it out, you keep fifty. Fair?"

I ran it exactly the way I'd run a thousand vacation packages down a phone line — easy, warm, the price tucked into the back half where it lands soft, the close built right into the ask so all he had to do was hand me the word *fair* back.

He didn't have an ID. But — and this is the part where a desperate man starts to believe again — he had a friend who did. The friend was right there, pulling himself toward us across the lot in a wheelchair. Yeah, he's got one, the first guy says. It's at his place, couple blocks up. The man in the chair nods along, says he'll go grab it, be right back, and the two of them set off — one walking, one wheeling — to fetch the little plastic card that was going to save my night.

One open, one close. I sat down to wait on my money like a man who'd just booked a deal.

Fifteen minutes. I sat there fifteen whole minutes dead certain that a man was about to wheel himself two blocks each way, in the rain, for fifty dollars. Thirty minutes. No walker, no chair, no ID, no nothing. And it came down on me slow and stupid: he was never coming back. Of course he wasn't. The closer had just gotten closed — stood up clean by a man in a wheelchair, out in a parking lot, at the bottom of the worst week of my life. You almost have to tip your cap.

So. Run it back. Next man — no ID. And somewhere in the middle of striking out I finally tripped over the real hole in my whole operation: it wasn't just an ID I needed. It was a phone. The money was wired to me, in my name, and to shove it into somebody else's I'd have to get Niki back on the line to cancel it and send it fresh — and not one of these curb-sitters had a phone any more than I did. We were all of us out there equally erased.

That moved the target. No more of my own kind. I needed somebody established — a real ID and a real phone in a real pocket — so I turned my back on the curb and waded out into the lot proper, in among the civilians.

I had a theory, and I'll admit up front it was held together with spit: catch them at the car, loading bags, right after they'd emptied their wallets inside — figured a person goes a little soft right after they've already spent, some kind of momentum I could lean on. I don't know that it made a lick of sense. But it was a plan, and a plan was more than I usually walked around with.

It went about how you'd guess. Twenty-some people, and most of them all but broke into a run. One man just backed away going *get away from me, get away from me, get away from me,* scared half out of his skin, like I'd reached for his throat instead of his goodwill. Others looked straight through me and shut their doors and I heard the locks go *thunk* down the row. A few were kind about it and still said no. And the whole time I kept grabbing for the thing that had never once not been in my hand on a sales floor — something to sweeten the deal with. A free night. Show tickets. A bonus week somewhere warm. I'd spent my whole life closing people by sliding a little extra across the table, and now I *was* the package, soaked and bleeding, and there wasn't one thing left to bundle. Just me, in the rain, asking.

I drifted out toward the middle of the lot, out by the pumps, where the store kept a little glassed-in hut — chips and smokes and scratch-offs, the window where you pay for your gas. And wandering around out there I found a couple. Young, strung out, that loose-jointed nowhere-to-be drift I knew on sight. What the hell. I gave them the whole story, and they said yes — they actually said yes. They had an ID.

They did not have a phone.

Halfway home. Always exactly halfway.

But I was standing twenty feet from a phone, and it was attached to the woman inside that hut. She'd looked friendly enough through the glass. I told the couple to hold tight and ducked in to ask if I could borrow it — and before I could get the first word of the pitch out, she beat me to the punch.

Why don't you just use *my* ID? she said. I get off in an hour. You can use my phone.

Yahtzee.

It was better than my own plan by a mile, and not just for the obvious reason. There was a fear I'd been carrying the whole afternoon without ever letting it fully surface — that whoever I recruited could just as easily take that five hundred dollars and stroll out the far end of the store, and what in God's name was I going to do about it, call a cop, me? But she wasn't some drifter off the lot. She was an employee, behind a counter, with a name tag and a shift and a till to answer for. For once in that whole endless day, the house's own walls were standing on my side of the table.

Her name was Paralee, and she was about to be my savior. Late forties. Born and raised in Alaska — Eyak, she told me, full-blooded, a word I didn't know that day and had to look up later: the name of one of the smallest Native peoples this country has ever had. Long black hair, gone to a little gray. Thin. A lifetime of cigarettes written into her face the way weather writes itself into anything left standing out in it long enough. She had decided, in the space of one sentence through a gas-station window, to throw me a line, and she was not going to let go of it.

I got Niki back on the phone and she moved the money into Paralee's name. And Paralee — a woman I'd known all of four minutes — fronted me a pack of cigarettes and a cold Coke against money I didn't have yet, to be squared up the second it came out. So I posted up outside that store for the next hour with a smoke and a Coke and a bag of chips, the closest thing to comfort I'd touched in five days, and I watched the lot and wondered, the way you wonder down at the bottom, what in the hell was coming for me next.

And there was Niki again, working the far end of the wire without being asked. While I sat there smoking, she got on the phone with the little Japanese-style pod hostel where I had a bed reserved for the week and headed off the next thing I didn't even know was lined up to sink me — that the front desk was going to take one look at a paper ID and a beat-to-hell stranger and balk, same as everybody else had all day. She told them the story. She got me cleared to check in before I ever walked through the door. God bless that woman. I keep saying it because I keep meaning it more.

An hour later the money came out without a hitch — clean, the whole five hundred, real cash folded into my own hand for the first time since a stranger emptied my pockets in the dark. I went into the store and bought a couple of shirts and a pair of pants and carried them straight back to the fitting room, and I peeled off the t-shirt I had bled through and slept in and walked eleven miles in and begged in and come apart in, and I left it in a heap on that fitting-room floor like the shed skin of the week I was finally crawling out of. Walked out a different-looking man. Paid Paralee her sixty — fifty for the favor, ten over for the smokes and the Coke and the simple decency, though there is no bill you can settle that last part on.

And then Paralee, having already done more for me than the entire city of Anchorage put together, offered me one thing more. Come by the house, she said. Meet my husband. Have a couple beers, take a hot shower, I'll cook us all a real dinner.

Now. I had a bed. An actual reserved bed in an actual room with my name already cleared at the desk — the first roof in longer than I could stand to count that was genuinely, only mine — and every grain of sense I had left was pointing me dead at it. Go to your pod. Lock the door. Sleep for a year.

But she had saved me. And I was so swollen up with gratitude, so unused to anybody being plainly, uncomplicatedly good to me — the woman at the DMV that morning aside — that turning her down felt like spitting in one of the only two open hands the whole day had held out. So I said yes. Of course I said yes. *Why the fuck not* — the same three words, in the same order, that had walked me into a madman's group home that very morning, the same reasoning that has loaded me into the back of every wrong car I have ever climbed into.

And that yes — that one polite, grateful, can't-make-myself-be-rude yes — is right where the next thing starts. Because if there's one thing I have proven myself world-class at, past closing a stranger and surviving a beating and walking out of a cell with nothing to my name, it is this: saying yes to precisely the wrong invitation, and then finding out why the long way around.
