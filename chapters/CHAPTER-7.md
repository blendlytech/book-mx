# Chapter 7

I am standing in a library.

An elderly woman sits at a desk. She guards the rights to a bank of twenty desktop computers like they are the last clean water in the city. They are rationed out in forty-five-minute slices.

Four are open. I take the room in the way you take in any room when you are broke and desperate. Fast. Looking for the angle.

The chairs hold men who look exactly like me. Down to their last good idea. Wearing the week on their clothes. Old folks peck at keyboards one finger at a time, leaning in like the screen might whisper back. Nobody talks. It isn't the polite hush of a library. It is the silence of a waiting room where everybody is there for a different bad reason and nobody is stupid enough to ask. A whole row of people the city quietly stopped keeping a seat for.

After years of Vegas floors—bells, transfers, money made out loud—the quiet of this place lands on me like weather.

The funny thing is, the librarian never asks for an ID. All those miles of walking and a stranger's twenty-dollar bill so the state would certify my existence, and she doesn't want to see a lick of it. Just tells me the forty-five-minute limit.

I already know forty-five minutes isn't enough. I file it away and sit down.

First task: the bank account. See how much of my ghost is still bleeding out in a stranger's pocket. No dice. The bank wants to text a code to the phone I no longer own. Four days is a lifetime with a stolen debit card. The damage is done. I let it go.

Next task: Facebook. Which, without a phone, is its own miracle. Log in from a strange machine and the vault slams shut. It demands to text you a code. Lose your phone, and you are locked out of your own life. I know the drill cold.

Then, a genuine back-alley miracle. Facebook decides to email the code instead. Google lets me verify through a backup address. One little door swings open that should have stayed bolted. I am in.

It costs me twenty of my forty-five minutes. But I am in.

I fire off a message to the only person left to fire it to. My oldest sister, Niki.

Niki is always the one. The one who shows up. She sat in the bleachers with a VHS camcorder and taped my average games like they were the state final. She was the first call for a loan. A ride. The kind of trouble you only confess to one person.

She writes back the second I hit send. I am in luck. She just got paid. Niki is a courtroom transcriber. The money trickles in on nobody's schedule but its own. She carries the same curse I do. Money shows up, money leaves. Catch her the wrong week, she is broke. Catch her on payday, she is an open hand.

I need more than the usual fifty bucks today. I caught her on payday.

No surprise. No lecture. She has seen this exact movie too many times. Me in the starring role, her cast again as the one who wires the money in the third act. We go straight to logistics.

We are hammering out the mechanics of saving my life, and then—

A tap on my shoulder.

Not the old woman. An old man. Her muscle. The enforcer sent when the clock runs out and the desperate refuse to move.

Time is up, he says. I tell him I'm sorry. I just need a few more minutes. He says he's sorry too, but that's not how it works. Come back tomorrow.

Tomorrow. I have no tonight.

I stand up. I look the old man dead in the eye. I give it to him flat. No heat. Just facts laid down like bricks. I was beaten and robbed. I went to jail for breaking into the house I was paying to rent because they stole my key. I sat in a cell four days while somebody drained my account. I have no money. I walked here from the DMV with a paper ID. And right now, this minute, I am working out the only way I have left to get money sent to me by my family. I need a few more minutes.

I sit back down and keep typing, like he has already left.

I feel it. The weight of a person's presence lifting off your back. He is gone. I keep my eyes on the screen and brace for security. For a hand on my arm. Nobody comes. The clock turns into a suggestion. I close my deal.

Niki finds a Harris Teeter less than two miles away. Western Union. They just need an ID. I have the paper one. She sends five hundred dollars.

I walk out of the library lighter. And of course, it is raining. I take it as a gift. A free shower. A chance to rinse four days of jail off my skin.

Thirty minutes later, the Harris Teeter.

Western Union lives at the customer-service counter. It shares a window with every small catastrophe in the store. In front of me is a man returning a broken lamp with no receipt. He will not give up. He is going to win this one. I stand there soaked, five hundred dollars and the rest of my life thirty feet away behind a register, watching a grown man litigate a dead lamp for thirty minutes I will never get back.

Finally. The counter. I give them my name and Niki's name. I lay my paper ID faceup next to the broken lamp abandoned by the man who didn't win.

The clerk says: "We need a confirmation number."

Fuck my life.

No confirmation number, no money. There is no arguing. This isn't a desk clerk I can outlast with a story. A number exists or it doesn't. Between Niki and me, we built the whole bridge and forgot the last plank.

Back out into the rain. A thirty-minute walk back to the library. To beg for a computer.

I walk straight back to the same elderly woman. She shakes her head before I speak. Come back tomorrow.

I need her to generate a unique passcode. I need her on my side. By choice. I plead it small. Straight. I am going to be sleeping outside tonight if I don't get the passcode to my own money. Two minutes. Then I reach into my pocket. I offer her everything I own. Two soft dollar bills. The change from McDonald's.

I try to bribe a librarian with two dollars. I figured she could maybe hit the bingo hall after work. Have herself a little night.

She looks at me. She gives in. She doesn't touch the money. She generates the code and waves me through.

I get back on. I get the confirmation number from Niki. Oh yeah, they did give me one of those. I could not be mad if I tried. She is footing the bill.

I tuck the number away like gold. Walk it back through the rain. Harris Teeter. One more time.

The line is empty. No lamp. No manager. Things are looking up.

That is exactly the moment to be careful. Things looking up is the precise sound the house makes right before it reaches across the table to collect.

I lay the confirmation number on the counter. I wait on the clerk. The same heavyset, thirty-something woman. She takes the number. Then she says: "ID."

I feel good about it. The DMV wasn't a waste. I slap the paper ID on the counter like a winning hand.

She looks at it. "We can't take temporary IDs. It has to be an official one. I'm sorry."

Sorry my ass.

That paper ID had been sitting on this same counter an hour ago. She just hadn't gotten far enough into the transaction to ask. "You couldn't have told me that the last time I was standing right here?" I say. I am coming apart. I feel it climbing my throat. I clamp down. Volume never helps. The wall doesn't hear you better if you yell.

"I'm sorry, sir. There's nothing I can do."

She offers the one alternative: transfer the money into someone else's name. Someone with a proper ID. They pull it out for you. "I don't know anyone," I say.

The flat truth. I know Steven, unreachable in Kenai. Niki, a continent away. I know Mike, who thinks he owns a treatment center and five trucks. In this entire raining city, I do not know one single soul with a wallet.

The old routine loads up in my chest. I was robbed. Beaten. Four days in a cell. The speech that moved the enforcer at the library. I swallow it whole. Because I know this clerk. I know this counter. The speech buys me exactly nothing but another soft I'm sorry, sir coated in my own desperation. You don't waste a closer's energy on a door that doesn't open. You find the door that does.

I walk back out into the rain.

New plan. I will stand under the grocery-store overhang and offer any stranger fifty dollars to use their ID. Fifty bucks for ninety seconds of their time. In Vegas, I would have ten takers before I finished the pitch.

Except. Picture the man making the offer. Soaked to the bone. Filthy shorts. A torn shirt grinding four days of jail sweat. A head wound the color of bad fruit. I don't look like a man who needs an ID to claim five hundred dollars. I look like the reason the clerk has a rule against men like me in the first place. Everything about me screams the exact opposite of trust me.

It is my only option. It is about to be the hardest sale of my life.

I start with my own kind. Men parked on the curb running the spare-change hustle. I pitch the first one. Easy. Warm. Exactly like a vacation package. He doesn't have an ID, but his friend in a wheelchair does. Two blocks up. They promise to go get it. Thirty minutes later, in the rain, it hits me. The closer just got stood up by a man in a wheelchair. You have to tip your cap.

Next man. No ID. And then I trip over the real hole in my operation. I don't just need an ID. I need a phone. The money is wired to my name. I have to call Niki to change it. None of these curb-sitters have a phone. We are equally erased.

The target moves. I need someone established. I wade into the lot, in among the civilians. Catch them at the car. Loading bags. Wallets empty. Twenty-some people. Most break into a run. One man backs away like I am reaching for his throat. Locks go thunk down the row. I have no free nights to offer. No show tickets. I am the package. Soaked and bleeding.

I drift out by the gas pumps. A little glassed-in hut. I find a young, strung-out couple. The drift I know on sight. I give them the story. They say yes. They have an ID. They do not have a phone. Always exactly halfway home.

But I am standing twenty feet from a phone. It is attached to the woman inside the hut. I tell the couple to hold tight. I duck in to ask to borrow it. Before I get the first word out, she beats me to the punch.

"Why don't you just use my ID?" she says. "I get off in an hour. You can use my phone."

Yahtzee.

It is better than my plan. The fear I had been carrying all afternoon—that the stranger would just walk away with the five hundred dollars—vanishes. She is an employee. A name tag. A shift. A till to answer for. The house's walls are finally standing on my side of the table.

Her name is Paralee. My savior. Late forties. Eyak—a Native people I have to look up later. Long black hair gone gray. A lifetime of cigarettes written into her face like weather. She decides, in one sentence, to throw me a line.

I get Niki on the phone. Move the money into Paralee's name. Paralee fronts me a pack of cigarettes and a Coke. A woman I have known for four minutes extending trust against money I don't have yet.

I post up outside the store with a smoke and a Coke and a bag of chips. The closest thing to comfort I have touched in five days. I watch the lot and wonder what the hell is coming for me next.

Niki works the far end of the wire. She calls the Japanese-style pod hostel. Heads off the next disaster before I even know it is coming. The front desk was going to take one look at my paper ID and turn me away. Niki tells the story. Clears me to check in. God bless that woman.

An hour later, the money comes out clean. Five hundred real dollars folded into my hand.

I walk into Harris Teeter. I buy two shirts and a pair of pants. I walk straight to the fitting room. I peel off the t-shirt I have bled through, slept in, walked countless miles in, and come apart in. I leave it in a heap on the floor. The shed skin of the week I am finally crawling out of.

I walk out a different man. I pay Paralee her sixty dollars. Fifty for the favor, ten for the smokes and the Coke and the simple decency.

Though there is no bill you can settle that last part on.

Then she asks if I want to come home with her for dinner.

It is an invitation I should refuse. I have clean clothes, a pocket full of cash, and a pod waiting. I should say thank you and walk away.

But the reflex is incurable. The same instinct that has loaded me into every wrong car of my life.

I say yes.

Why the fuck not.
