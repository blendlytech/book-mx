# Ginger

In my family, the word for it was *talking.*

My father got caught talking to a woman. That's how it was said, and that's how it stayed said, for thirty-odd years, at every table where it ever came up. Not an affair. Not cheating. Talking. As if the man had been overheard having a nice conversation and the whole house came down over a misunderstanding about the length of it.

I was five years old.

And the person who caught him was my mother's mother.

I want you to hold that one up to the light for a second. Not a friend. Not a neighbor. Not some anonymous phone call in the night. His wife's mother — the one woman on this earth with a permanent, unblinking, personal interest in exactly what my father did with his evenings. That's who found it. So it didn't arrive at my mother the way these things usually arrive, sideways and deniable and easy to talk your way out of. It arrived from her own mother, all the way proven, and there was no version of it my father could sell.

He'd have sold it if there had been one. I want to be fair to the man and to the truth at the same time: my father could sell anything, and I know that because I got it from him, and I have spent an entire adult life proving what that particular inheritance is worth. But there wasn't a play to run on his mother-in-law. So the thing that never happened in that family happened. It got named.

I was five. Nobody explained one word of it to me and nobody was ever going to, because you already know how that house worked — the thing occurs, the ledger closes, everybody goes in to dinner.

---

Here's the part I've never written down and I'm only now sitting still long enough to look at.

I was five when David died.

I was five when my father got caught.

I don't know which came first. I have never asked. There is not a single person left I could ask who'd give me a straight answer about the order of it, and I'm not sure I'd want it. But somewhere in that one year, in the same twelve months, in the same small collection of houses on the same few streets, a garage door came down on a five-year-old boy and a marriage came apart, and the woman standing in the middle of both of those was my mother.

I've written that my mother was never the same after David. That's true. I've believed it my whole life and I still do.

But I was five years old, and I only ever got handed the one explanation, and I've been carrying it around for forty years like it was the whole account. It wasn't. It might not even have been the biggest thing in the room. A woman can be broken by a dead child and by a husband caught out by her own mother in the same season, and there is no line down the middle of that where you get to sort out which piece did which damage.

I don't have an answer. This is the first time in my life I've even had the question.

---

So then there was Ginger.

She had been in love with my father a long time. I don't know exactly how long and I don't much care to do that arithmetic either, but she was, and everybody knew she was, and when the thing finally broke open she was the one standing there.

And here's what nobody expected, my mother's family least of all: she stayed. She married him and she stayed, and she took on the whole disaster he came with — a man with a whole life already behind him, three girls, and me.

Two daughters of her own — Holly, about three years up on me, and Natalie, about five, though I'd put money on neither of those numbers and I wouldn't swear to either one on a Bible. And a son, Jimmy, who I've written about elsewhere and who is the single best thing anybody ever handed me. That's on her ledger too, and she'd never in her life think to put it there. She brought me my brother. She probably thinks of that as an accident of the paperwork. It is the only piece of good luck I have ever been dealt in my entire life and it came in with her.

---

Now here's the number that matters, and it's the reason this chapter exists.

From twelve years old to eighteen, that woman was my mother.

Not a stepmother. Not my dad's wife. My mother, in every way the job actually gets done — the daily, unglamorous, thankless grind of getting a boy from twelve to grown. Six years. The six that make you.

And I did not notice until I sat down to write this what sits right underneath that number.

Eleanor died when I was eleven.

The one woman on this earth who ever took a passed-along kid and made him feel like the whole point of the weekend was dead in the middle of my sixth-grade year, and I sat at her table waiting for her to come through the door, and she never did.

And then I was twelve, and Ginger was my mom.

I don't know whether those two things have anything to do with each other. Nobody sat me down about that either. But I've got a hole where a grandmother was, and one year later a woman who wasn't obligated to me in any way that a court would recognize stepped into the exact size and shape of it and did the whole job for the next six years.

She did it right. That's the part I want in print. She didn't do it adequately, she didn't do it grudgingly, she didn't do it the way you look after somebody else's kid because you married the father and the boy came with the house.

She did it right.

---

She's funny. I should say that first because it's the first thing anybody says about her and it's the first thing anybody notices. Ginger is genuinely, effortlessly funny, and I grew up in a house where the currency was making people laugh, and she could hold her own at that table against my father, which is not a small claim.

She can cook. Not fancy — I don't mean she's got some technique. I mean the woman knows how to make a meal, the way people knew how to make a meal before it was a hobby, and there was always one, and it was always going to be there.

And she made me shower.

I want to sit on that one, because I know exactly how it reads on the page and I know exactly what it means.

She was the one who told me I had to take a shower. Somebody in that house had to be the person who noticed that a twelve-year-old boy had not washed, and had to be willing to say so, out loud, to a kid who was going to get an attitude about it — and then say it again the next day, and the next week, and for six years. That was her. That was not anybody else. Nobody else in my life was tracking whether I was clean.

That's motherhood. That's the actual thing, and it's got nothing to do with birthdays or hugs or the speech at the graduation. It's somebody deciding they are on the hook for the boring maintenance of you. Every single thing I know about what a parent is I learned from a woman telling me to go get in the shower.

And she did it through the worst of it. That's the other half. Those years covered the deep end of my father's drinking — the real end, the part of his run that took the paint off everything — and she stood in that house for all of it, and kept the meals coming, and kept a kid who wasn't hers on the rails, and did not put any of it down on me.

She was my rock. That's her word from me and I'm not going to dress it up any further, because that's what a rock is. It's the thing that's there when you come back.

---

Now here's what I owe her, and it's the reason I've been circling this chapter for weeks instead of writing it.

I've got six years of that woman raising me and almost no film on any of it.

I can give you my grandmother's kitchen down to the color of the light over the table. I can give you the sound of a car locking up its brakes in the middle of a street in 1985. I can give you the exact weight of a handful of my sister's hair.

Six years of the person who actually did the job, and what comes back is a warmth, a certainty, a plate on a table, a voice telling me to go wash.

I know why. You don't store the ordinary. You store the ruptures. Everything I can see in high definition is a thing that broke — and Ginger never broke anything. Ginger was the part that held, and the parts that hold don't get photographed. They just quietly do the entire job while the rest of the house is busy being memorable.

Except there is one. One scene, and it comes back whole, in focus, every detail, and I would give a great deal to trade it for any six ordinary suppers.

It's a parking lot. And I'm in the car.

---

The last time I was back in the States I was skinny.

That's the word I use for it and everybody who has ever been where I was knows exactly what that word covers. Skinny. Gaunt. The face gone in at the sides, the clothes hanging off, the look a man gets when the thing has taken over the accounting and started spending the body. You can't hide it. You can't dress for it. It's in your face and your neck and your hands and it tells the entire story to anybody who's ever seen it before.

And I knew where she worked. I'd hear about it — where Ginger was these days, how she was doing, the ordinary traffic of information about a person you love that comes to you sideways because you haven't picked up a phone.

And I would go there. I want to be exact about this: I went. I got in the vehicle and I rode over and I was in the parking lot. That's how close I got. That's how many times I got that close.

And my father would get out, or Josh would get out, and go inside and see her.

And I'd sit in the car.

They'd tell her. Of course they would — it's the natural thing on earth to say. *Clay's outside.* And I don't know what she did with that. I've never asked and I don't want to know, because I have run every version of it in my head and there isn't a single one I can stand.

I was mortified.

That's the whole of it. That's the entire reason. I was so ashamed of what I looked like that I sat in a parked car twenty yards from the woman who raised me, more than once, and did not get out.

---

So here's what I want said, and this is the part I actually sat down to write. The rest of it was just me working up to it.

Ginger — it was never you.

Not one second of it. Not because I didn't care and not because I forgot and not because I got out into the world and decided the people back there didn't matter. There was never a version of it where I didn't think about you.

It was that I could not let you see me like that.

I had exactly one thing left that was worth anything to me and it was that somewhere out there was a woman who remembered me before all this. Who knew me at twelve, and at sixteen, and had a whole picture of me that hadn't been ruined yet. And I could not make myself walk in that door and put the other one on top of it. I wanted you to keep the one you had. I thought I was protecting something.

I know better now. I know exactly what I did.

Because we all have clocks. Every one of us. And I sat in a parking lot doing arithmetic about my own vanity while a clock ran on a woman in her seventies, and I told myself I was doing it for her.

None of it should have mattered. Not the weight, not the face, not what she'd have thought. There is not one chance in this world that Ginger would have looked at what I'd turned into and loved me any less, and I knew that then as well as I know it now, and I stayed in the car anyway.

That's not shame protecting somebody. That's shame protecting itself, and it will cost you the people who'd have had you at your worst, and it costs you them for good, because clocks don't care what your reasons were.

I love that woman, all the way through, and I never forgot one day of it. I want her to know what she did. From twelve to eighteen she was my mother, and she did it right, and nobody made her, and nobody thanked her, and she never once handed me a bill for any of it.

You deserve three chapters. I'm sorry I only gave you one.
