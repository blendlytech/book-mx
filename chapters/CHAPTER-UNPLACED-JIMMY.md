# Jimmy

Jimmy was two years older than me and he never once used it on me.

If you didn't grow up the smallest thing in a loud house you might not know what that sentence is worth. Two years, at that age, is everything. Two years is height and reach and knowing what things mean. Two years is a boy who can end any argument in the yard whenever he feels like ending it. Every other person in my life who ever had two years on me spent them — on me, at me, over the top of me. That was the going rate.

Jimmy had it and never spent it. Not one time that I can remember.

He was Ginger's son. Which made him my stepbrother, technically, on paper, in the way the county would write it down. I have never once thought the word *step* about him and I'm not going to start here.

He was my big brother. That's all. I never had one. I had three sisters who were older and one of them I couldn't be in a room with for eleven minutes, and then a woman married my father and walked her boy into my house, and I got the thing I'd have traded any two of the others for.

---

He didn't come alone, though. There were two more, and I'd be telling this crooked if I left them out.

Holly and Natalie. Ginger's daughters — three years up on me and five, or near enough that I'm not going to argue about it.

They were beautiful.

Not pretty. Not nice-looking. Beautiful, the way that a boy of a certain age recognizes instantly and permanently as a separate species from anything he is ever going to get near. The kind of beautiful that makes a man in a driveway lose his place in his own sentence.

And I was twelve. Thirteen. Dead center of the worst of it — right in the stretch where a boy has just found out women exist and has started the long humiliating business of working out where he ranks among the men who'll be competing for them.

So somebody went and put two of them in my house.

I've turned that over my whole life and I still say it was one of the crueler tricks ever played on me, and here's what makes it funny instead of sad: not one person meant anything by it. There was no malice anywhere in the vicinity. It was paperwork. Two families got stapled together, and one of the things I got out of the deal was a daily, permanent, over-the-breakfast-table demonstration of the exact category of woman I had already privately decided I would never in my life have one single chance at.

And they were my sisters. That's the other half of it and it's the half that settles it. That is what they were and it's what they've always been, and there was never one second of anything else in it — that's not the point and it never was.

The point is what a house like that does to a kid who was already keeping a running tally of everything he wasn't.

Because look at what was under that roof. Jimmy, who was everything I wanted to be and could not manage to be. Two girls who were walking proof of the league I wasn't in. And me. Small, scared, not a fighter, planning my routes through the school hallways, a boy whose stepmother had to tell him to go take a shower.

That's the thing nobody explains about where a man's self-esteem goes. It doesn't get taken off him in one bad afternoon. It gets *measured* out of him, a little at a time, at a kitchen table, by people who love him and have not the first idea they're doing it.

---

Here's what I want to say about Jimmy and it's going to sound like nothing until you put it against the rest of this book.

He couldn't be bought.

There is an offer that gets made to every kid at a certain age, and it doesn't come with a voice or a face, it just comes. It says: the shortcut to being somebody is to do the bad thing where people can see you. It says the drink and the dope and the stealing and the not-caring are a currency, and if you spend enough of it you get to stop being the kid nobody's afraid of. It says popularity is a thing you can buy with your own life on installment.

I bought it. I bought the whole package, and then I bought it again, and I made the payments on it for thirty-five years, and this entire book is the receipt.

Jimmy looked at that offer and it did not move him one inch.

I watched him do it, that's the thing. I was right there. It wasn't that nobody ever put it in front of him — everybody gets it put in front of them — it's that he'd look at it and it just wasn't worth anything to him. Same offer, same age, same house, same town. He couldn't be bought with the illusion, and I got sold on the first pitch and stayed sold my entire adult life.

I've spent a lot of years and a lot of money on people trying to explain to me why I'm like this. Nature, they'll say. Nurture, they'll say. And then there's Jimmy — same roof, same table, same everything that supposedly makes a man — who took a look at the exact same thing that ate my whole life and said no thanks and went and did something else.

I've written it before in this book and I'll write it here where it actually comes from: it's not nature and it's not nurture. It's me.

I know that because of Jimmy.

---

He was strong.

I don't mean he could lift a thing, though he could. I mean the other one. He was solid the way you want a man to be solid — you knew what he was going to do, and then he went and did it, and it was the same on Tuesday as it had been on Friday.

I was not strong. I was not solid. I was quick, which is a different animal entirely and usually a worse one.

I was never a fighter. That's the plainest way I know to put it and it's the thing I was most ashamed of for most of my childhood. I was afraid of the guys in the grades above me — genuinely afraid, the kind where you plan a route through a building — and I got very good at the arithmetic of who would be where and when. Some boys learn to fight. I learned the hallways.

And I want to be honest about what that shame actually did to me, because it's not the small thing it sounds like. It wasn't about losing a fight. I never got hurt bad enough for it to be about that. It was that I knew what I was. I knew, at nine and eleven and thirteen years old, that if it ever came down to it I would not stand, and I carried that knowledge around inside me like something rotten in a coat pocket, and no amount of anything I ever accomplished afterward got the smell out.

Jimmy knew. He never said it to shame me. He tried to fix it.

He worked on me. He'd get after me to stand up, to answer for myself, to quit doing the thing I did — which was to go around a problem sideways with my mouth instead of walking up to it. He was trying to make me stronger and he was trying to make me answer, and those are two different jobs and he took both of them on for a kid who wasn't even his blood, for no reason at all except that he'd decided I was his to look after.

It didn't take. I want that on the record too, because I'm not going to write a chapter where the good older brother fixes the broken kid and everybody learns something. He put in the work and it did not take. I went right on being quick instead of strong. I went right on going around instead of through. Thirty-five years later I was on a phone in Las Vegas going around a stranger sideways for money, which is the same move I was running at eleven, just monetized.

But he tried. Nobody else did. That's not nothing — that might be the only thing in this whole book that isn't nothing.

---

He was what I wanted to be.

Not what I wanted to *have.* I've wanted plenty of what other men have and I've taken a fair amount of it. This was different. Jimmy was the first time I ever looked at another human being and understood that there was a way to be a man that I was not on track for, and that some people just are it, without effort, without a pitch, without a single angle running underneath.

A man. A good man. Those are two separate words and I've only ever managed the first one, and some days not that.

I've measured myself against him my entire life. Every job, every woman, every border, every jail cell, every one of the four thousand mornings I woke up and did the arithmetic on whether I could get through the day without a drink — somewhere underneath all of it there is a two-years-older boy who never used those two years on me, who couldn't be bought, and who is the yardstick I have been failing against since I was eight years old.

That's not his fault. He never asked to be anybody's yardstick. He was just a decent kid who got a stepbrother and decided to do the job right.

---

Jimmy deserves his own book and he's getting a chapter, and I'm running out of chapters, and I'm aware of exactly how that sounds coming from a man who found room in here for every bar he ever got thrown out of.

That's the arithmetic of a life like mine. The wreckage writes itself. Somebody hands me a crisis and I can give you the weather and the smell of the room and what everybody was wearing. The people who were just steadily, boringly good to me for years on end — Ben, who I loved and never told; Ginger, who raised me and never billed me; Jimmy, who tried to make a man out of me and didn't quit when it obviously wasn't taking — those are the ones I've got the least film on and the most owing to.

So here it is, Jimmy, in the only language I've ever actually been any good at.

You were the only big brother I ever had, and you didn't have to be, and I have never once said thank you for it. I'd tell you what I turned into but you can read it — it's the rest of this book. I'd just want you to know that the whole time I was becoming it, there was a part of me measuring the distance back to you, and that part never once got quiet.
