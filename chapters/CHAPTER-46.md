# Chapter 46 — Always a Tuesday

It was another two weeks before Omar surfaced.

I'd about given up on him coming at all — figured I was just another body on a bunk to him now, another strung-out story he'd heard a thousand versions of. Then on a Tuesday — it's always a Tuesday — a staffer told me Omar wanted me in his office. I walked over there thinking *finally*, rehearsing my pitch the way I'd rehearsed a thousand pitches in my life: a few weeks in one of his apartments, I'd make it right, I had money coming. I had it all timed out, the ask buried three sentences deep so it wouldn't spook him. That is what I do. That is what I had done walking into every room I ever entered.

I opened the door and damn near fell the fuck over.

Glenda was sitting in a chair directly across from him.

Glenda. In a chair. Breathing. Whole. Looking right at me.

She came up out of that seat and we crashed into each other and I lost it entirely. I want to be honest: it wasn't relief. Relief has a shape to it; relief is a weight coming down. This was my whole chest coming apart in a stranger's office. I had buried this woman. I had grieved her in a ditch in the desert with men pissing ten feet from my head. I'd stood in front of an empty call center, laid her in the ground in my mind, and made my peace with it. I had walked four days out of the mountains carrying that death on my back, and here she was — solid, warm, crying into my neck. Not one single gear in my head knew what to do with it.

Omar got up, stepped out, and shut the door behind him. I didn't even notice. He told me later he stood in the hallway a good ten minutes.

“You're *dead*,” I stammered into her hair. I didn't have a better word. “Where did you go? How... what happened? How did you get out?”

The questions came tumbling out in a pile, tripping over each other, not one of them finishing. She just held on tighter, repeating the only words she had:

*“I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.”*

And right there, as fast as the shock and joy had hit, something sharp came up behind it. Because *sorry* is a word you say when you made a choice. A dead woman doesn't have anything to apologize for.

“What the fuck?” I said, pulling back to look at her. “You just *left* me up there? You were here this whole time? Your mom, your sister — I reached out to them. More than once. They told me they didn't know where you were. Nobody knew where you were! I thought you were *dead*, Glenda. I walked four days through the desert thinking you were in the ground. I *grieved* you. Do you understand that? And — Christ — did you know Zaul's here? He's *here*, he's been here the whole—”

*“Sit down, baby.”*

She took both my hands and pulled me down into the folding chair beside her, looking at me with those eyes I'd buried and never thought I'd see open again.

*“Let me explain what happened.”*

***

“You remember the nice couple?” she said. “The ones who hauled the feed in? Who started bringing food for Clayita?”

“Of course I do,” I said. “I'll remember them the rest of my life.”

“Well. I confided in them.”

And then she told me how she got off that mountain, and it was so simple it made me sick.

She'd worked the one angle nobody else up there could work. She had the language, she had the face that belonged, and she had the one man on that hill whose guard dropped completely around her. She got Omar drunk. *Our* Omar. The mountain one — the one whose name I will never be able to hear clean again. She got him good and drunk one morning, and while he was passed out, that couple put her in the bed of their truck under a heavy tarp and drove her all the way to Mexicali. Flat on her back over the wheel well, holding her breath through every checkpoint and every slow turn, listening to men she couldn't see ask the driver questions in the dark.

She'd run a full month before I did.

A month. She'd been off that mountain and down in this city, safe and breathing, for four weeks before I ever put a broken piece of wood to the screw in that bus window. And during that month, and in all the weeks after, she had been carrying the exact same weight I was.

“I thought you were dead too, baby,” she whispered. “I really did.”

***

And then she told me the rest of it, and this is the part I've had the hardest time carrying, because there is no villain in it to hate.

“My mom, my sister — they never told me you reached out.”

She said it flat. Not defending them, not accusing them. Just stating the fact.

She'd reached out to my family the same way I'd reached out to hers. Thrown messages into the exact same silence from the opposite end of the wire. Different names, burner accounts, the same black hole. And out of every single soul she contacted, exactly one answered.

Zach.

“Did he tell you I reached out?” she asked.

“No,” I said, stunned. “He never said a word.”

I sat with that for a second. My nephew — the last believer, the kid who had sent me the last sixty dollars in his pocket on the word of an uncle telling a story nobody had a right to believe — had been holding both ends of the thread the entire time, and never realized it.

“The only person I got through to was Brandy,” I said. “And that was a week ago, when they finally let me touch the laptop.”

*“Your* laptop,” she said, and a ghost of her old smile surfaced — the first expression that wasn't pure grief. “You mean my sister's laptop. You still have that thing?”

“Yeah,” I said. “I still have it.”

She laughed. A real, genuine laugh — the first one in what felt like a year, cracking out of her rusty and dry, like an engine turning over after sitting in a barn.

“Only you,” she said, shaking her head. “Only you would crawl down off a cartel mountain and trek four days through the desert with Jo's laptop still strapped to your back.”

“It was seven days total,” I corrected her instinctively, the words out before I could stop them. I couldn't help myself.

***

I don't have anything profound to say about it. I've tried to find the wisdom in it, and there isn't any.

Two people spent five months locked in a hollowed-out bus four feet apart. Then they spent two months in the exact same city, twenty minutes away from each other, each holding a private funeral for the other. Every cry for help went out into the dark from opposite sides, and not one message crossed the gap. Nobody was trying to be cruel. Nobody lied out of malice. It was just scared families, broken phones, and terrified relatives keeping their mouths shut to protect their own. That was all it took.

I never did get a straight answer about why her mother and sister told me they hadn't heard from her. I asked once, later on, got an evasive non-answer, and let it drop. I decided I didn't need it. Those women had watched a daughter and sister come home half-dead off a cartel compound, followed by a frantic gringo blowing up their phones from a country he wasn't supposed to be in. I can imagine half a dozen versions of that conversation where no one is an evil person. I'd rather carry those versions than dig up a bitter one.

Leaving a rock unturned is a new habit for me. I'd call it personal growth if I hadn't learned it in my forties, sitting on a folding chair in an intake office, with my entire life in pieces.

***

There was one more thing, something I'd been carrying since Michigan.

I'd never asked her. Not across three years through a glowing phone screen, not across two years living in Mexico, not during five months trapped in a bus with blacked-out windows. When I thought she was dead, not asking that question was the regret that ate at me the most. It sounds trivial now, written down, but sitting there broken in that office, it slipped out like the climax of a movie nobody would believe.

“What was wrong?” I asked. “Back then. You'd get on video at night, your eyes would fill up with tears, and you'd just stare off past the camera. I asked you a hundred times what was wrong, and you never told me. Why did you cry looking at me?”

She stared at the wood grain on the corner of Omar's desk.

“I was watching you die,” she said.

The plainest sentence anyone has ever spoken to me. I had waited two years to hear it, and it took her four seconds to lay it bare.

She told me she would sit in that kitchen in Mexicali with the tequila bottle right where it always sat, staring at that lit phone screen, watching a man two thousand miles away shrink week by week. She said by November she wasn't looking at a living man anymore. She said she had already started mourning me in the past tense and hating herself for doing it. When I'd ask what was wrong, the only real answer was: *You're going to die before I ever get the chance to touch you.* And there is no way to say that into a telephone at midnight to a man who already has a glass in his hand.

So she said nothing. She looked away, swallowed the tears, and I poured another drink. We danced that steps for months.

Then she gave me the answer I never saw coming, and was too selfish to ever ask for.

“That's why I quit,” she said.

I actually made her repeat it. I sat there in that chair and made her say it a second time. Understand: I was staring at a woman returned from the dead, answering every question I thought had been lost forever.

She told me she checked into this facility — *this exact building* — two years ago because she decided someone had to be sober enough to stand on their own two feet when I finally arrived. She knew she couldn't fix me from two thousand miles away. The only piece on the board she had the power to move was herself, so she moved it.

It wasn't poetic or polished. That’s just the raw gist of what she said, and it cut straight through me.

I have turned that over in my mind more than almost anything else in this story.

Because I had built my entire escape to Mexico on the foundation of her getting sober. I wrote it down on page one: she put down the bottle, and that was the crack the rest of my life slipped through. That was the myth I lived by — the miracle that turned a drunken, long-distance phone affair into a reason to liquidate everything I owned and run south. I flew two thousand miles on the back of that woman's recovery.

And not once did I ever ask her why she did it.

Not once in two years. I hijacked her miracle and turned it into leverage on myself: *If she can do it, I can do it, and I'll do it by sheer proximity without ever checking into a program.* I never once stopped to ask the woman who paid the price what it cost her, or who it was for.

She got sober because she was watching me commit slow-motion suicide.

And my grand arrival was putting a cold Tecate in her hand on a dirt road in Puerto Peñasco. Eight months after that, she was doing cartel cocaine out of a plastic baggie in a dingy Rosarito apartment. Five months after that, we were both face-down in the gravel being kicked by men in tactical boots.

I started rambling, trying to say all of that at once. It was long, desperate, loaded with apologies, and right in the middle of it I could hear my own voice doing what it always does: shaping the story, working the angles, trying to control the room even here, even now.

She let me babble for about thirty seconds.

Then she let out that flat, sharp little snort through her nose — the sound she makes whenever a man says something breathtakingly stupid with absolute conviction.

“Don't,” she said sharply. “I picked the bottle back up myself. Nobody pried my mouth open.”

And that was it. That was all she would give me. She handed me the answer I’d been chasing for two years, and then flatly refused to let me turn her choices into a tragedy about myself. It was the most merciful thing anyone had ever done for me, and she delivered it in ten words.

***

The door clicked open and Omar walked back in. The good one.

“You two have been through hell,” he said, looking between us. “I think you should stay here for a while. Nobody is going to touch you inside these walls. You're safe here.”

“I don't feel safe here, Omar,” I admitted, my voice shaking. “I don't feel safe anywhere. Do you have somewhere else we can go? An apartment, a house outside town?”

Glenda looked at me and gently shook her head. I knew the answer before Omar even spoke. There was nowhere else.

He looked at Glenda. “You should check back in too. The women's annex.” He turned back to both of us. “You both need serious psychological help after what you survived. Heavy counseling. I'll find an English-speaking therapist for you, Clay. I'll handle it personally.”

I couldn't say no. I'd love to tell you I weighed the pros and cons, that I acted like a man with cards to play. The truth is I had run out of road. A decent man was standing in front of me holding a door open, and for the first time in my life I was too exhausted, too beaten, and too grateful to look for the catch before walking through it.

“I have a room with air conditioning,” Omar added. “It'll just be you and Freddie. Freddie does the maintenance and construction around here — he built my house. He works seven to five every day and stays away on the weekends. He just lives here to keep himself clean. You can rest in there. Recover. Get your head right.” He glanced at Glenda. “You too.”

In a Mexican rehab, that offer was like handing someone the keys to a penthouse. In general population, you sleep stacked thirty men to a sweltering concrete room with barred windows, waiting for a relative to sign a release form. Having an air-conditioned room with one roommate was a miracle.

We both agreed. A six-month commitment. I signed the intake papers — discharge date set for sometime in October.

Sitting back in that cheap chair, the full, absurd circle of my life hit me square in the chest. The rehab clinic I had flown to Mexico to exploit. The facility where Zaul and I had set up ten duct-taped Dell computers to fleece American solar clients, using recovering addicts as cheap labor. *That exact building* was now the sanctuary keeping me alive. And the woman I had come south to rescue was checking into a dormitory sixty feet away, neither of us holding a single dollar, a passport, or a plan.

You couldn't make it up. It’s the recurring curse of writing an honest memoir: the parts that sound most like bad fiction are the exact parts you can't leave out. It might not be a clean love story, but it’s an intensely human one.

What Glenda and I were going to be to each other, I had no clue. We had both been ghosts to each other for months. You don't just resurrect a relationship on an intake day. But that was a problem for later. I was finished pretending I knew what was coming next.

***

I didn't care about the missing ID. I didn't care about the frozen bank accounts or the warrants back in Ohio. 

That night, I lay flat on a clean mattress with cold air blowing across my face, safely tucked inside the compound I had once tried to con, four weeks off a cartel hit list, with the woman I thought was dead sleeping across the courtyard.

I want to tell you that was the end of the bad news.

Except they handed my laptop back that same week. And the moment I got the TextNow app reconnected to the Wi-Fi, an unread message popped up on the screen from Ace.

It had been sitting there waiting for almost a month.
