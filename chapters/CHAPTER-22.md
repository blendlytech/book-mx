# Chapter 22

We were just walking to the store.

That's the part I keep landing on. Not running anything, not holding anything, not doing one single thing but walking up the road on the poor side of Mexicali in that August heat that comes up off the pavement and lays itself across your chest like a hand. Me and Glenda, on foot, going to buy groceries the way two people who live together go buy groceries. Rose was finally past the worst of it — propped up, mending, hollering at us from the back room about what to get — and for the first time in months the house didn't feel like a hospital. We'd walked maybe three blocks when the truck slid up beside us on the sidewalk, and a cop got out, and told us to get against it for a pat down.

It's nothing, down there. You learn that fast. A gringo on the wrong side of a border town, a head taller than everybody, the kind of pale that doesn't belong on that street — you get stopped. They run a brisk business catching guys who ran from the law on the U.S. side, but they're hunting felony fugitives, the ones with real federal weight on their names, the ones who light up a system. My two warrants weren't that. A Vegas bench warrant and an Ohio DUI don't show up anywhere but the states that hold them — not in NICS, not in anything a cop in Baja can pull. I knew that the way you know a thing you've leaned on too many times. So I put my hands on the hot metal of that truck and I wasn't scared. I'd done this dance before.

---

They went through me first, the cop rattling off Spanish I didn't have a word of, Glenda stepping in to tell him I didn't speak it. Pockets, waistband, the pat down the world over. Nothing on me, because that was the one law we never broke — you do not leave that house carrying. No paraphernalia, no pot, no Yeti of beer for the walk. You go out clean because the whole town is a tripwire and you know it.

Then they searched her.

And there it was. Some spilled weed down in the bottom of her purse, the way it gets — a busted bud, a little scatter of it in the seams that you'd never think to clean out because who cleans out the bottom of a purse. The cop pinched it up between two fingers and held it where we could both see it, and the air changed.

Here's the thing about a stop like that. They are not the law, not really, not in that moment. They're looking for a payday. That's the whole machine — they find a reason, they let the reason get heavy, and then they let you make it light again with money. We told them we didn't have any. Which was the truth, the way I'd built my life to make it the truth. I kept nothing in my own account; American money in a Mexican bank is just a number that taunts you. What we had, I'd moved over to Glenda — about four hundred dollars sitting in her name. Not nothing. But standing on that sidewalk, broke as far as our pockets went, we played it the only way you can: empty hands, empty story, sorry, officer, we've got nothing.

They didn't believe us. So they leaned on the soft spot, which is always the woman. They told Glenda they'd take her in, that she'd sit the whole weekend in a cell, and they'd test the gringo, and if I came up dirty for marijuana I'd be deported on the spot. No phone call, no court, no nothing. Gone.

And we held. We figured they were bluffing — the threat is the product, the threat is what they're selling — and we were bluffing right back, two broke-looking nobodies with four hundred dollars one transfer away that we'd decided they were never going to see. I thought I had it read. Glenda thought they'd get bored and let us walk. We were both so sure.

They loaded us into the truck.

---

The station wasn't much. They tested me almost the second we were through the door — and I want you to notice what I didn't do, because I've had a long time to notice it. I didn't ask for the card. There's a thing where you can demand to see the documentation, slow the whole machine down, make them do it by the book, and a man thinking clearly asks for the card. They separated me from Glenda before the thought ever surfaced. By the time I'd have wanted it she was gone to some other room and I was being walked into a little office in the back where a judge sat behind a desk with my test results already laid out in front of him like a dinner he'd ordered.

Small room. Him at the desk, a couple of cops leaning in the doorway, all of them talking low and easy and laughing about something while I sat just outside it waiting to hear what was going to happen to me. I still wasn't scared, and that's the part that kills me looking back. I had my wallet. No passport — never had one, that was the whole problem of my life down there — but a wallet, an ID, the look of a man who'd be released. Because every other time, in Peñasco, it had gone exactly that way. Some cop with broken English would stop me, question the gringo, pat me down, find no money and no dope, and wave me off. I sat in that office running the same tape, certain it would end the same.

But they'd found weed on Glenda. And that had already changed everything, and I was the only one in the room who hadn't caught up to it yet.

They had one guy there who spoke English. He listened to the judge, then turned to me and gave it to me flat. *You're being deported. Dirty test. Drugs.* And that was the whole of it. No pass go, no go back to the house, no collect your laptop and your clothes and your life. Just up, out, into a truck, and pointed at the border. I had my cell phone and wallet in my pocket and that was the entire inventory of everything I got to keep.

---

They held me a while first. Hours in a cell, and that's where the fear finally came and found me, late, the way it always does — sitting on a concrete bench with nothing to do but turn the warrants over in my hands. If those two ever stood up and showed themselves, this stopped being a deportation and became something with bars and years on it. But they didn't. They couldn't. A Vegas bench warrant and an Ohio DUI live in Nevada and Ohio and nowhere else; they don't ride the wire down to a Mexicali holding cell or up to a U.S. port of entry. So when they finally walked me to the line and handed me to the Americans, secondary inspection ran my name, verified I was who my ID said I was, and confirmed the one fact that mattered: a U.S. citizen, and they have to let a citizen back into his own country no matter how he turns up at the door.

Then they opened it and put me out into the streets of Calexico with a phone, an ID, and a debit card.

So much better than Alaska. That's the first thought I had, standing there blinking in that border-town light, and God help me it was true. In Anchorage they'd turned me loose at two in the morning into the cold with nothing — no phone, no money, no name anybody would honor — and I'd walked miles to prove I existed. This time I had a phone in my hand and a card in my pocket and a country under my feet. Better in every measurable way.

Just as scary, though. Exactly as scary. Because better-equipped is not the same as found, and I was standing on the wrong side of a line from everything I owned and everyone I loved, with no idea on earth what came next.

---

So I went to the deep well. My father.

He's the big-money one — always has been, the man you call when the number's too big for Niki, the DUI lawyer, the thousand here and five thousand there that pulls you off the rocks one more time. And he was not happy to hear from me. You could hear it land on him, the weariness of it, *this again,* before I'd even finished the sentence. I asked for six or seven hundred — enough for a hotel room and a minute to think — and I genuinely did not know what I was going to do with it. Sneak back across. Stay on this side and figure it out. Both at once. I didn't even have my laptop, which meant I didn't have the one tool that let me make a dollar, and that was its own quiet emergency stacked under the loud one.

He balked. Of course he balked. He let me hang there a beat, made me feel the full weight of the ask, and then — pissed off the whole time, doing it the way he did everything, with his disappointment fully itemized — he sent it. Troy had wired me a hundred right before all this, out of nowhere, the way Troy does. So I had about eight hundred dollars to my name and a weekend to make it mean something. I got a cheap room. I shut the door. And for the first time in a day I had four walls that were mine.

---

The text came in right behind the money.

I've read some hard things off a phone screen standing in a parking lot. This one had its own shelf. It was a deadbeat-son letter, the long-form kind, every grievance of a lifetime itemized the way he itemized everything — that I was the worst son a man could be cursed with, that I'd run off every last person in this family one at a time, that I'd cost him thousands across the years and handed back nothing, that I was a loser and had always been one. I'd heard versions of all of it before, drunk, in the heat of some specific disaster. What I'd never gotten was the button he put on the end of it: *There. That was worth the money I just sent you.*

That was new. That wasn't a father chewing out a son. That was a man closing an account. He'd bought the right to say it and now he'd said it, and some sober corner of me read it clean, understood exactly what it was, and decided — standing right there — to set it down and let it go. I had a plan to build. I couldn't afford to feel it yet.

Then the room was secured and the door was shut and the liquor was on the table, and this was never going to be a two-beer night. The dial I was forever bragging about was nowhere in that room. It was shot after shot chased with beer after beer, and somewhere down in the bottom of it I picked the phone back up and read the whole thing again, and again, until the words stopped being words and turned into a fire — and then I let him have it.

I told him what kind of father he'd actually been. I told him it was his fault I was the drunk typing those words, that he'd poured the foundation for this long before I was old enough to pour my own. I told him he'd wrecked this family before I ever got my turn at it — that he'd cheated on every woman he ever walked through our door, that money had always meant more to him than his own kids, that he'd never once in his life gotten all four of us on speaking terms with him at the same time, and there was a reason for that, and the reason was him. It was a beating. I swung at every soft place I'd spent forty years learning the location of.

I regretted it before the hangover even cleared the next morning. I didn't mean a word of it — not the way I'd meant it at one in the morning, not as a verdict — but there's no taking that kind of thing back. You don't unsay the truest cruel things to a man coming up on eighty. In one long text message, drunk in a border-town room, I'd taken the one well that had pulled me out of every hole I ever dug and put a bullet in it. I felt sick about it for about as long as I let myself feel anything in those days. And then I did what I always did with the things I couldn't undo. I set it down where I didn't have to look at it. I had a plan now, and the plan needed me.

---

Then I built it, because building the plan is the one thing I've never not been able to do.

I got Jo on the phone — Glenda's sister, the same Jo who'd run me across that border the first time, two years and a whole life ago. She'd cross back over and get my laptop and bring it to me on this side within a couple of days. Glenda would get out that same night; with Rose finally healed up enough to be left, Glenda was more than ready to be gone from that house and that city. She had a place lined up in Rosarito — cheap, no deposit, the kind of spot you can land in when you've got nothing and need it yesterday — and that's where she was headed, to set it up then send for America. The plan was that Jo would run me up to San Diego that weekend, and I'd hole up there while Glenda got Rosarito sorted, find a room of my own, and — finally, after all these years of doing everything the crooked way — get an actual passport so I could cross like a human being. Legally. Through the front door.

Jo wouldn't take a dime for the ride. I found a room to rent up there for five hundred a month and talked the guy down to letting me pay it a hundred and fifty a week instead, once I laid my whole situation out for him the way I lay everything out for everybody, so it cost a little more monthly, but in small installments, which is what I needed...time. So I had a little room to breathe in. A little money to play with. All I had to do was find an online job inside the week, and the laptop would be in my hands, and Glenda would be down in Rosarito waiting, and I'd be on my way to her clean and legal and aboveboard for once in my life.

That was the plan. And I remember exactly how good it felt to have one — the old relief of a man who's always been able to draw himself a map out of any room, sitting down to draw one again. The dial right there in my hand where it had always been, pointed south toward a cheap house on a coast I'd never seen, toward a town called Rosarito. I was sure of it. I was sure of all of it.

---

San Diego ate the six hundred I had left in about two weeks.

The room took its bite a hundred and fifty at a time, The passport took its $150, and the rest went to food and the small constant bleed of staying alive in an expensive city, and then it was just gone. Jo had brought the laptop up like she promised, and I sat in front of it day after day and could not turn it into a single one. There was no online job. Not for me, not that month, not for the life of me, however I worked the angles. And the normal kind, the in-person kind a man my age can walk in and land, I couldn't take either — because somewhere along the years I'd lost track of the plain fact that you need a Social Security card to fill out the paperwork, and I didn't have one, and it had been so long since a living soul had asked me to show one that I'd half-forgotten the thing existed. Just one more door I never thought to check until I was standing in front of it with no key. One more piece of paper proving I wasn't quite a real person.

The two weeks came and went, and then I was homeless in San Diego.

I want to say it that plainly, because I spent a long time saying it every way but.  I didn't tell a soul.  I didn't reach out to anyone in the family. I slept on the beach for about two weeks. I panhandled. I collected cans and scrap metal and walked them to the recycling center for the few dollars they handed back. The man renting me the room didn't put me out with any meanness in it — he didn't dislike me, he just needed the rent the way everybody needs the rent, and he couldn't carry a stranger for free, and I never blamed him for it. The one thing he could do, he did: my passport was still being mailed to his address, and he told me he'd hold it for me whenever it came. So I had that much. A couple more weeks of sleeping on sand a few miles from the one document I was waiting on.

Then I got into a shelter, which is not a thing that happens fast. There's a wait, and there are rules, and the biggest rule is that you have to be sober — which, it turns out, is no challenge at all when you don't have a dollar to your name to get unsober with. So I was sober. Forced clean by simple broke, the cheapest sobriety there is and the only kind I'd managed in a while. And the shelter was a sad place. A lonely place. Quiet in the way that gets down into you. I'd been broke in a few countries by then, but being poor in my own — seeing how this country handles its poor up close, from inside the line instead of tossing a dollar across it — was its own particular education. I lay on a cot in there at night with nothing to think about and therefore everything, no drink to blur it and no plan left loud enough to talk over it.

---

And still — God help me, still — the only direction I knew how to face was south.

The passport was the whole spine of it. The one clean, legal, aboveboard thing I'd tried to do in years: wait on that little blue book, carry it up to the crossing, hand it across, and walk to Glenda through the front door like a man who belonged somewhere. I waited on it on a cot, and then I waited on it on the sand. And then it came.

Denied.

Not the warrants — that's the joke of the whole thing, the warrants never made a sound. It was a date. My divorce date. I'd dug it up online and written down the day the thing got *filed* instead of the day it was *finalized*, the day the state actually called it real, and those two dates sat a few weeks and one entire denial apart. I could resubmit. Fix the one date, send it back, wait the weeks out again, hope. I wasn't going to. Something in me had already quietly walked over and closed that office down.

I'd scraped maybe thirty dollars together that day, panhandling and hauling cans, and I looked at it and decided it was a joint-and-a-bottle kind of day — the kind that ends with me out on the beach, figuring the rest of my life out from there. Another one of my great ideas. I ran it back two days straight.

The second night, it happened again. I got robbed in my sleep — my bookbag this time, my laptop and my clothes, lifted right off me on the sand while I was out cold, the exact same way Alaska had peeled a phone off my body four years and a whole life ago. The one mercy was that my wallet and my phone were in my front pocket, up against me, so I still had the two small things that let me pass for a person. I sat up in the gray morning with sand in my teeth and stared at the dent where the bag had been, and I could not believe it — and in the very same breath I believed it completely, the way you believe weather. I didn't call anybody. Not Niki, not Brandy, not a soul. I'd burned the father to the ground, I was too embarrassed to lower a bucket into anyone else's well, and somewhere in there I'd decided I would just take this one on the chin and never say a word to a living person about how far down I'd actually gotten.

And right there on the sand, with nothing left to lose and nothing left to guard, I made the only decision that felt like movement. I was going to cross back into Mexico the way I'd come the first time — illegally, on foot, through the cracks — and make my way down the coast to Rosarito. To Glenda.

Who was, by then, selling weed for the cartel to get by.

Let me say that one again, because I had to say it to myself a few times before it would sit down. Glenda was selling weed for the cartel. And the worst part is it wasn't even getting her by — she'd gotten the one month's rent covered and that was the whole reach of it. Here's how it runs down there: they front you twenty bags that belong to them and ten that are yours to sell or smoke yourself, eighths, a hundred pesos a bag, about six American dollars apiece. Nobody is getting rich off thirty bags of weed. It isn't a money deal, it's a leash. But Rosarito will feed you if you let it. A family that grew fruit and vegetables and sold them off a stand all day was handing her the bruised stuff at close — the soft, dinged, perfectly good produce they'd have thrown out otherwise, still fine to put back on the table by the next afternoon — and between the few pesos and the free food, she was getting by.

I wasn't getting by. I wasn't getting anything. I was just coming — no laptop, no passport, no plan that had survived contact with a single day, nothing in my two hands but a phone and a wallet and the oldest reflex I owned.

Another chapter. Another fresh start. Another geographic cure, pointed the wrong way down the only road I had left.
