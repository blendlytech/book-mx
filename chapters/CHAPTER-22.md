# Chapter 22 — Carrie

I once pulled a whole handful of hair out of my sister's head.

Not a few strands. Not a tug. I got my fist up in there behind her ear and I turned it and I pulled the way you'd pull on a rope, and it came out — all of it, at once, roots and everything, and the sound she made was not a sound I had ever heard a person make before.

And then I was just standing there in the living room holding it.

That's the part I've never been able to put down. Not the pulling. The standing there afterward with my sister's hair in my hand, lighter than I expected, still moving a little like it hadn't been told yet, and Carrie with her hand up on her head and her mouth open and no sound coming out yet, and both of us understanding in the same half second that something had happened that neither one of us knew how to undo.

I don't remember what started it. That's the truth and it's a stupid truth. Sixty seconds before that I'd have told you it was the most important thing in the world.

Then the screen door banged, and I heard my mother coming, and I knew exactly what was coming behind it, because it was always the same thing coming behind it — that yellow plastic fly swatter off the nail by the door, and my backside, and the whole thing over in about nine seconds. And I stood there and took it and my sister stood there and watched me take it, and that was the transaction. That was how it worked in our house. You did a thing, you got the swatter, and the ledger was closed.

Nobody ever asked me why I did it. Nobody ever asked her what it felt like. The swatter came out and everybody went back to their day and we ate dinner.

---

Carrie was the youngest of my three sisters, which made her the closest one to me in age, which is a polite way of saying she was the one I had to go through. Niki was the oldest and was practically an adult by the time I was aware there were other people in the house. Brandy was six years up and had her own weather system entirely. Carrie was right there. Carrie was in arm's reach.

So we went at each other. For years. Not the cute kind of sibling thing you tell at a Thanksgiving table — the real kind, the kind with hair in it. We could not be in a room together for eleven minutes.

And here's what I want on the page about her, because I've never said one word about it anywhere else in my life.

Carrie pinched every penny she ever touched.

That's the thing everybody in the family said about her, usually with a little laugh in it, and it always got said the wrong way. She wasn't cheap. She wasn't greedy — Lord, she was the least greedy person that family ever produced. She was *smart.* That's all it ever was. She knew what a thing cost. She knew what she had. She could tell you to the dollar what was in her account on a Tuesday in March, and I have never in my entire life been able to tell you what was in mine on any day of any month, and that right there is the whole difference between my sister and me laid out in one sentence.

She had money when nobody else had money, and it wasn't luck and it wasn't help, it was arithmetic, done quietly, every single day, for thirty years, by a woman nobody in the family ever once thought to ask for advice.

I was the other kind. I've had a hundred thousand dollars come through my hands in a year and not one dollar of it stay. I've been the guy buying the table a round on Friday and calling his sister for rent on Monday. My whole life is a story about a man who never once did the arithmetic.

She had every right to look at me sideways. She never did. Not once. She counted everything on this earth except what her little brother owed her.

---

I need to tell you what I did on Facebook.

Somewhere in there — years before Mexico, years before I ever heard the word *ayahuasca,* back in the Vegas-and-after stretch when I was drinking like it was a job I was good at — I got up around two in the morning and I found David's sisters online.

A grown man, drunk in the dark, with a phone.

And I typed it all out to them. All of it. That it was my fault. That I did it. That I'd been carrying it my whole life and they deserved to hear it from me. I don't know how long that message was. I know it was long. I know I hit send more than once. And I know that whatever I thought I was doing — confessing, unburdening, coming clean, whatever noble word I had loaded up in me at two in the morning with a bottle beside the bed — what I was actually doing was taking the worst day of their lives. Walking up to their door thirty-some years later, and knocking on it because *I* needed something.

That wasn't an amends. I know what an amends is now. That was a drunk man handing his suffering to the people on earth least obligated to hold it.

Back in Sinaloa I wrote that I never spoke of David again sober. That's true and that's exactly the shape of it. Sober, for thirty-five years, not one word. Drunk, one time, to the wrong people, at the wrong hour, at their expense.

---

And then Carrie told me she was out there too.

I don't remember how it came up. I remember exactly where the floor was and I don't remember one word of how we got there. She said it plain, the way she says everything plain.

She'd been out in that driveway with David. And she had shown him the button too.

I want to be honest about what happened in me, because if I clean this up the whole chapter is worthless.

The first thing I felt was not relief. It wasn't gratitude, and God knows it wasn't love, and it wasn't anything a decent man would feel when his sister opens her chest up and shows him she has been carrying the identical stone for the identical thirty-five years and never once set it down in front of anybody either.

The first thing I felt was that she was taking something from me.

That's it. That's the sentence. There is a sick little room in the back of me and that's what was in it — *she's trying to steal my thunder.* Like it was mine. Like I had the deed to that driveway. Because if she was out there too, then the thing I'd built my whole interior life on top of — the reason I drank, the reason I ran, the reason I was the way I was, my *reason,* the one I'd been paying rent on for four decades — didn't belong to me alone anymore. And I would rather have kept the guilt than share it. That's what I'm telling you. I would rather have been the boy who killed his cousin than one of two kids who showed a five-year-old a button.

That's the disease. Not the drinking. That. The drinking is just what the disease drives.

So I did the only thing our family ever taught either of us to do with a thing like that.

I buried it. I said something. I don't know what I said. I moved us on to something else and I put it down in the cellar with everything else, and I did not pick it up again for years, and Carrie — who had just handed her little brother the single heaviest thing she owned — watched me set it on the floor and walk away from it.

And I got it, eventually. I did get it. It took me an embarrassingly long time and it cost me a night on a concrete floor in Sinaloa with a bucket, but I got it. She wasn't taking anything from me. She was reaching for me. It was the closest that woman ever came to saying *I love you* to me in forty years, and I heard it as competition.

---

Down in that *maloca* the medicine put me back in the driveway and made me press the button, and it showed me the door coming down, and it never once showed me my sister standing there.

I've thought about that a great deal. I think the medicine shows you what you actually believe, not what's true, and what I actually believe — under everything, under the arithmetic, under thirty-five years and two grown people's separate testimony — is that I was out there alone. Guilt doesn't divide. You can hand a man all the evidence in the world and he'll go right on carrying the whole thing, because the carrying is the point. It's the last thing some of us own outright.

Carrie's been carrying hers alone in a different house in a different state the entire time. And we have never once talked about it since.

---

Here's what she made me, and I've only got it sorted out the last couple of years.

Carrie was the first one who ever fought me back. Not a mother — a mother is different, a mother has the swatter. Carrie hit back. Carrie had a mouth on her and she was quicker than me and she was not one bit afraid of me, and I could not win that with my hands, so I learned to win it with my mouth. I learned it at seven years old in a yard in front of a woman who wouldn't back down, and I got so good at it that by thirty I could talk a stranger out of a credit card number in four minutes on a phone. That voice they showed me on the medicine, the greased one going out over all those cubicles — that voice was built in a driveway, fighting my sister.

And the other half of it. What I learned in the nine seconds after the swatter came out was that a hard thing is a thing you take, and then you go in and eat dinner, and nobody says one word about it ever again. Feel it later. Feel it never. Put it in the cellar. I have run every catastrophe of my adult life on that exact procedure — the mugging, the jail, the daughter, the border, the mountain — take it, close the ledger, go eat.

It works fine. It works right up until you're forty-some years old on a bus floor in Mexico with a stranger singing over you and the cellar door comes off its hinges.

---

Carrie deserves her own book, not a chapter, and definitely not this one, sitting here in the back of a story about a garage door and a bucket. She has a good heart. She's got a good heart in a family that never made it easy to keep one, and she raised her kids and did her arithmetic and stayed exactly who she was while I was out setting fires.

There's a time she stuck up for me that I'm not going to put in this book. It isn't mine to put here and some of it isn't only about me. She knows the one. I want her to know plainly, in print, where she can hold it in her hand: I know what it cost you, and I have never forgotten it, and I appreciate it more than I have ever managed to say out loud.

I'm sorry I wasn't the big, little brother I should have been. That's the phrase I've always had for it and I know it doesn't parse. I was the youngest and the only boy and I was still supposed to have been bigger than I was, and I wasn't, not once, not for you and not for your kids. I wasn't there. I was in Vegas, or Alaska, or Ohio, or a rehab in Mexicali, or a room I can't remember, and you were where you always were, doing it right, with nobody's hand on your back.

We never got the chance to say it to each other. That's the thing that gets me at three in the morning. Not the hair, not the swatter, not even the driveway. It's that there was never one window in forty years when the two of us were both standing in a place where that sentence could have gotten said. When we were kids we were at each other's throats. When we grew up I was gone. And by the time I had the words, I was the last person on earth she had any reason to open a door for.

The past refused it. It just wasn't in the cards.

So I'll put it here, where it can't be taken back, and where I don't have to survive her face while I say it.

I love you, Carrie. I always did. I was just raised in a house where the fly swatter came out and everybody went back in to dinner, and I never learned how to say a thing like that to a woman who could beat me at it.
