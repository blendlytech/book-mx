# Chapter 13 — In the Flesh

My plane set down in San Diego with about an hour to kill before my ride showed, and that hour is where the whole harebrained thing finally turned real on me. Not real the way a plan is real — a plan you can lay out on a couch and admire, all its clever little parts. Real the way a wall is real, which is to say you don't find out until you've already walked into it. I'd run clean out of road. There was nothing left in front of me to scheme. Just a curb, and a stranger coming to drive me, and a border I had no earthly business crossing waiting somewhere down the highway in the dark.

So I did what I do. I sat outside and I smoked. Cigarette after cigarette, lighting the next off the last, watching the doors for a car I wouldn't recognize driven by a girl I'd never met. And under the nerves, running the whole time like a stock ticker I couldn't shut off, was the math of my own blood. Praying that last vodka had cleared — and it would have, liquor's a gentleman that way, in and out of a man inside an hour or two. Praying the weed had cleared too. I'd quit the weed a week out exactly so it would, the weak stuff, the little bit of nothing I was sure a body finally eating again could flush in seven days. I had it all figured. I always have it all figured. Remember that.

I texted Glenda. *I'm here. Just waiting on your sister.* And then I sat in the California sun feeling, of all the things a grown man can feel at the end of a thing he's chased for three years, *silly.* Out of place. Too big for the moment and too small for it at the same time. Jo was about an hour late, and that was fine. I had nothing but cigarettes and the weight of it, and there was plenty of both.

---

Jo finally rolled up. Young — early twenties — and a genuinely beautiful girl, tall and thin and quick, funny right out of the gate. We hugged like people who already half-knew each other, because in a way we did. And it wasn't just her. She'd brought two friends along for the ride, and one of them was actually the one driving.

It was a quiet car. Two hours of quiet, San Diego falling away behind us and the country going brown and flat and enormous. There wasn't much to say and I wasn't the man to say it — I was nervous, I was somewhere else, I felt out of place in the backseat of a stranger's car heading toward the one country I'd spent two years swearing off. The three girls talked among themselves, mostly in Spanish, a language I'd made exactly no effort to learn for the woman I was crossing a border to be with. Jo sat in the back with me. At some point she leaned in, snapped a picture of the two of us, and fired it off to her sister with a caption I'd have paid money to write myself: *haha, I met him first.*

They smoked weed the whole way. Passed it around the car, easy as gum, and not once offered it my direction — because I wasn't allowed to touch it. I was sober-ish now, you understand. Headed to a rehab to get clean, and not even there yet, and already the joint was going around a circle I'd been cut out of. There's a joke in that somewhere. I was too wound up to laugh at it then. I can now.

---

The plan was a thing of beauty.

I was to be dropped at the front steps late — after ten, on purpose — by which hour Zaul, the director, and anybody else who wore the word *authority* would be home in their own beds. The door would be answered by trusted residents working the night shift, the English-speaking ones, my own people, Glenda's circle. A quiet handoff. No fuss. I'd meet Glenda that night on the men's side, and then we'd slip over to the women's side, where Zaul had set aside a room for the two of us — the free side of the cage, the part of the place that didn't lock from the outside — and we'd have it for a week, until my next scheduled check, after which I'd move into a cheap Airbnb and start running the operation I'd flown two thousand miles to build. A soft landing, engineered down to the hour. I am very good at engineering soft landings. It's the touching down I've never once gotten the hang of.

---

We made Calexico, where the plan called for us to drop Jo's two friends before we approached the border, and then there it was in the windshield. The crossing. The exact thing I'd fought with Glenda about for weeks — *they are not just going to let me into a country without a passport, that is not how borders work* — and her telling me, flat and certain, *they won't even check.* I didn't believe her. I want that on the record. I thought she was a sweet optimist about to feed me to the federales. But I didn't have the luxury of my own opinion, because if they turned me around at that line I had maybe two hundred dollars to my name once Jo was paid, and two hundred dollars gets a man exactly nowhere — not a flight back, barely a couple nights in a motel before the floor opened up again. So I had no choice but to take her read over mine and bet the whole pot on it.

And then we were in Mexico.

That's it. That's the whole crossing. I didn't even know it had happened. One second we were rolling up on the thing I'd lost sleep over for a month, and the next Jo was grinning at me going *see? That was easy.* No stop. No check. No man with a gun and a clipboard asking me the one question I had no answer for. It was like driving from one town into the next, and the only thing on God's earth that changed was the signs — the language gone over to one I couldn't read, the speed limits counting up in kilometers instead of miles, the numbers suddenly bigger and meaning less. I'd bet everything I had left on a stranger's say-so, and the house had let me walk. It always let me walk. That's the thing about a streak like mine: it doesn't feel like luck while it's running. It feels like you've got the place figured.

---
She pulled up to the rehab and I grabbed my bags, and Jo sent me off with the smile and the line I'd carry a long way: *good luck.* And then, half under her breath, that laugh — the one that means *you are completely out of your fucking mind* — sized me up and let it go.

I banged on the metal door. It opened on a wall of Spanish I didn't have a single word for. *Hola* — and then a run of it, faster than I could catch.

"I'm Clay," I said. "Glenda."

A beat. Then the whole face changed. *Ohhh. Entra, entra.*

I entered. And that is precisely where what was supposed to happen stopped happening.

---

First thing. Piss test. First thing through the door, a cup.

Positive. Marijuana.

And I want you to appreciate the comedy of it, because I've had years to and I still can't quite get over the workmanship. I'd lain awake sweating the vodka — and the vodka came back clean, gone like a gentleman, just like I'd figured. The thing that lit up that cup was the weed. The weak stuff. The little bit of nothing I'd quit a week out *specifically so this exact moment would go smooth.* I had timed my poisons down to the day, hedged every bet I could see — and the house reached right past the hand I'd covered and collected on the one I'd called nothing. A whole life believing I had the place figured, and I couldn't even read my own blood.

That cup changed everything. I was walked into a back room and told to strip. All the way. And then a stranger went through every crease and crevice a man's got, looking for whatever a man in my shape might be smuggling into a place like that. This was not in the agreement. Nobody on that night shift spoke a word of English — or at least nobody in charge of getting you naked and running his hands along your every seam did — so there was no arguing it, no name to drop, no angle to work. I'd come down to run a clever little operation inside this building, the broker, the man with the plan. And the building's first order of business was to strip the plan off me and have a look at what was underneath.

I'd also, somewhere in that doorway, signed a document. Officially checked myself into rehab. I'd come to use the place. The place had checked me in first.

---

Detox was about four beds and far, far too many bodies for them. I came in on a room full of passed-out addicts — men who'd hit that door their own selves a night or two before me and gone straight down into the deep sleep of the newly stopped. One of them stirred enough to make a sliver of room, and I slid in between two strangers onto a dirty pillow and lay there flat on my back, not moving, taking inventory of every single thing I had just signed my name to. I did not sleep. Not a minute. I lay there and let the night go by underneath me like the road had gone by under the car.

Morning came, and with it a parade. New faces drifting in to look at me and drift back out — the gringo, word getting around. I'd hear it travel: a run of Spanish, then *gringo,* then *Glenda,* then the long *ohhhh* of a thing clicking into place. Hours of that. And then, sometime in it, a man crossed the room and handed me a teddy bear. Said the only word of the transaction I understood. *"Glenda."* From her. I was mortified — a grown skeleton of a man laid out in a Mexicali detox clutching a stuffed animal in front of a roomful of strangers — and I was also exhausted past the point of pride, so I tucked it under my head and used it for the pillow it turned out to be.

A while after that they brought me food. Chicharrones — chicken skins, fried, the kind of thing that's somebody's whole childhood and somebody else's gag reflex. They were rubbery and they were awful and they slid around my mouth refusing to become food, and I ate every one of them down anyway, because the one thing I was not going to do on my first morning in that place was insult the people feeding me. I damn near brought them back up. I got them down. Chalk up a small win.

---

It was maybe two more hours before I heard *gringo* again, except this time it came with a hand waving me up and toward the door of the detox room. I got to my feet, confused, and walked out into the light.

And there she was.

Three years I'd loved that face through a screen and touched it exactly never, and here it was in the actual air in front of me, every bit as beautiful in the flesh as it had ever been in pixels — the same face, sober and clear and rested, wearing a smile and underneath the smile a wide-eyed worry, both at once, the whole of her looking at the whole of me. We came together careful. A small, almost formal kiss, the kind you give when you've somehow loved a person for years and are meeting them for the first time in the same breath, and then a long real hug that knew better what to do than either of us did.

And the entire rehab clapped.

Clapped for us — a courtyard of drying-out strangers breaking into honest applause for the gringo and his Glenda, this thing they'd all watched come together off a run of Spanish and a teddy bear. It was not how I'd pictured it. Three years, I'd pictured it a thousand ways, and not one of them had me walking out of a detox into an audience to embrace a woman I'd never once been allowed to hold. But standing there in it, I'll tell you — it fit. It fit better than anything I'd have written for myself. The cleanest moment of my adult life, and it came to me dirty, in a cage, with witnesses.

---

Zaul's ruling came down secondhand, the way rulings do in a place like that. Because I'd tested hot, the room he'd set aside for Glenda and me on the free side was off the table — I'd be serving the week on the men's side instead, alone, until I could clear a check and get myself into that Airbnb. I was embarrassed clear through. Ashamed. I'd come all this way and gotten myself flagged at the door like a kid caught at the gate, and the price of it was that the two of us, after three years and a border, still didn't get to be alone in a room together. One more week. The whole thing rich with the kind of irony I've never had to go looking for; it just shows up where I am.

So that was the week we had, and God help me it was a good one. We saw each other every single day. She had a job in the house by then — she was a panhandler for the place, out on the corners with a can collecting whatever the city would give a rehab, and she'd bring it back and stop off at the men's side to drop it, and we'd stand there and smoke cigarettes together and hold hands and look at each other and flat refuse to believe it. It was hard to meet her eye. I'd told you it would be weird and it was weird, the specific weird of a thing you've wanted so long and so hard finally standing in front of you with a pulse. But I was happy. Underneath the shame and the cigarettes and the detox still in my system, I was happy. And I was getting sober — not because I'd finally chosen it, let me be honest about that, but because there was a locked door between me and the nearest bottle for the first time in my grown life. Sometimes that's the only way it ever comes to a man like me. Not a decision. A cage.

---

But the week wasn't all her, and I'd be lying to you if I painted it that way. There was a job underneath it — the actual reason I'd told everyone, myself first, that I'd come down at all. So by day, while Glenda worked her can out on the corners, I built the call center.

Zaul had handed me ten stations and the run of them — *do what you want, they're yours.* And what a fleet it was: a mismatched junk-drawer of old Dell and HP towers, monitors in every brand the last twenty years ever made, a couple of them with their backs held on by nothing but duct tape and optimism. It was perfect. Not because it was any good — it was held together with tape — but because it was *mine,* free and clear, full authority, nobody leaning over the desk. Just a room and a problem and the one part of me that has never once let me down.

And I'll let you in on something I'd never have said out loud down there: I had the whole thing built in my head before I ever crossed the line into the country. The dialer provider, picked. Every scrap of software and hardware it would take to run a room, speced out and ready. The scripts written. The training laid out step by step. I even had the audio — Glenda's own openers, her voice coming down the transfer to me, recordings I'd saved off that first solar job for no reason on this earth except that I was a sentimental man who'd gone and kept the sound of the woman years before I ever had the woman. Now they were training tape. The single most romantic thing I owned, run back through a speaker to teach a room of drying-out strangers how to do to some man in his kitchen the exact thing she'd once done to me.

All that was left was the building of it, and the building of it is the one part I've never had any trouble with. Ten mismatched machines wired and humming, a training plan typed up and sitting on Zaul's desk, inside two days flat. Training would start on the third. I'd come through that door on a dirty cup, stripped and searched and folded into a detox — and seventy-two hours later I was management. The newest inmate in the place, building the house a business out of duct tape and secondhand Dells. You want the whole diagnosis of what's wrong with me, there it is in one clean line: shame can't move me and love can't move me, but sit me down in front of a dead room and ten broken computers and I will have the thing singing by Thursday.

---

Training started on the third day, exactly like I'd drawn it up, and I walked into a room of ten bilingual addicts looking at me like a magic trick they couldn't find the wires on. You could read the questions stacked up behind every set of eyes. *How does this skinny gringo know Glenda. How does a man who can't order a taco in this country wind up running a phone room inside a Mexicali rehab.* And the big one, the one I caught them trading looks over: Glenda is a beautiful woman, and here was this rattling coat-hanger who'd somehow taken her clean off the table. It did not add up for a single one of them. I didn't hold it against them. It barely added up for me.

But a room like that you don't talk your way into — you work your way in, and the respect shows up when the training does. So I went to work. I gave them the whole ugly truth of the trade, every trick I had, the tonality, the mindset, the thing living down under the words that keeps a stranger's phone pressed to his ear when every sane bone in him is telling him to hang up. I played them the calls — Glenda's and mine, front to back, the ones I'd saved — and we ran them into the ground. Hours of role-play. Scripts torn up and built back better. Objections hammered down their throats over and over until the rebuttal came back out of them without a thought to it, which is the only way it ever comes — after you've said the thing five hundred times and stopped having to think about saying it. And inside three days the wide eyes were gone and what I had in their place was a team. A real one, sharp and hungry and ready — the whole machine sitting there fueled and idling, about to be turned loose on a sales floor two thousand miles north.

---

In a week we'd be together. Officially. Out from under the watch, into our own four walls. And I want to tell you that's the part I was looking forward to with a clean heart, but the truth is the closer it got the more it scared me, because of the one thing about us I haven't said plain yet.

For three years, whatever you'd call what Glenda and I had done with each other's bodies, we'd done it through glass. She'd strip down on her end and do her thing, and I'd do mine on mine, two thousand miles and a screen between us, and we'd called that a love life and it had been one. The screen was the whole arrangement. The screen was the safety — the door I could always close, the distance that made it possible to give a woman everything I had as long as I never once had to be in the room for it. And now the screen was gone. In a week there'd be no glass at all. We were going to do it *with* each other, in the same air, with hands — the most ordinary thing in the world for everybody else alive, and for the two of us the single most terrifying frontier left on the map.

I lay on the men's side that night with a teddy bear named after my girlfriend under my head, dead sober for the first time in longer than I could count, the building I'd flown two thousand miles to use having already stripped me down and checked me in and handed me back to myself with nothing in my pockets. I didn't know yet what that place was going to come to mean to me. I never know it yet — that's the whole tired song of my life. I only knew I was finally, after three years, one week away from the thing I'd wanted most and run from hardest.

The real meat of it, like I told you, was just getting started.
