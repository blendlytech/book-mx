# Chapter 18

Rose drove all the way up from Mexicali to Puerto Peñasco just to collect us, and I should have read the size of the thing right there in that — that the trip didn't even properly start until somebody had already burned a day's driving to reach the place we were leaving from.

She loaded the four of us into the car, and the bags, and America in the back with me, and she pointed it south. A thousand miles of Mexico laid out between that beach and the town she still called home. Glenda up front with her mother. The girl beside me. And me — no passport, no papers, a country behind me and not one legal reason to be sitting where I was sitting — going along for the ride into Sinaloa.

You have to understand what Sinaloa is, or at least what it is to everybody who's ever heard the name and nothing else. It is the home office. It is the place the word *cartel* comes from when an American says it without knowing he's saying anything specific. And I was about to be driven straight down into the middle of it with a face like mine and a wallet full of the wrong nothing.

---

The trouble started with a road.

There are two ways through that country. There are the *cuotas*, the toll roads — fast, lit, patrolled, full of ordinary people going ordinary places, and you pay for the privilege of all of it at a booth every so often. And there are the *libres*, the free roads, that wind down through the little towns and the long black empty stretches that belong, once the sun goes, to whoever out there is strong enough to hold them.

Rose got us onto the wrong one. She didn't mean to — a missed sign, a saved few pesos, an honest mistake any tired woman makes on a long drive — but somewhere back there the toll road slid off to one side and we kept straight, and by the time any of us understood what had happened we were too far down into it to turn the car around. Off the safe road. Onto the other one. And the other one had people on it who wanted to meet us.

---

The cops found us first.

Lights, the shoulder, the slow roll to a stop. Two of them. And it took about four seconds at the window for it to stop being about a traffic anything and start being about the gringo in the back with no passport.

They knew. They always know. A paperless American this deep in is not a problem to a cop down there — he is a *payday*, a thing that fell out of the sky into your slow shift — and the only question on the table was the size of it. Rose and Glenda talked low and fast and steady in Spanish I couldn't follow, and I sat still and small and kept my hands where they could see them and my mouth shut, because the one thing I had to offer that situation was nothing, and I offered all of it.

A thousand pesos. That was the number. About fifty American dollars to make me disappear off their afternoon. We paid it, and the lights went off, and they waved us on down the road they all knew we shouldn't have been on — and that, I would learn over the next few hours, was the *friendly* version of getting stopped in that country. The cops just wanted money. The cops were the part of the drive I'd one day look back on as easy.

---

We pulled in for gas somewhere out in the nothing, full dark coming on.

I was standing at the pump — I think I was the one pumping, I want to say I was — when a truck rolled up out of the dusk and stopped too close, and a man leaned out of it. He didn't raise his voice. He didn't have to. He said something short, and Glenda's whole body changed in the front seat, and what it came down to, translated quiet into my ear, was this: give him the money. The gas money. Hand it to him, and get back in the car, and go.

So I did. We did. You don't pay for the gas at the counter, you pay for it to the man in the truck, because the man in the truck is who that gas station answers to and the counter is just a formality — and you hand it over and you do not look at him too long and you get your family back on the road. No menace you could've pointed at in a courtroom. Just a man, and a truck, and the entire understood weight of everything behind him, collecting what was his.

We left. We left fast.

---

The worst of it had no event in it at all. That's the part I can't ever quite make people feel.

It was full night by then, real dark, the kind with no towns in it, and a truck came up behind us and got close. And stayed. Not passing. Not falling back. Just *there*, right on our bumper, its headlights filling the whole back glass and washing the inside of our car white, mile after mile after mile of nothing-road, for the better part of forty-five minutes.

Forty-five minutes is a long time to wait to die. I'll tell you that for free.

Nobody in that car said the thing out loud. We all knew it — Rose's hands at ten and two, Glenda gone still as a held breath, me watching those lights in the side mirror doing the arithmetic of it — every one of us was certain, dead certain, that this was the night we got run off into the brush and robbed and shot and left, the way it happens out there to people who took the wrong road. And we did not say one word of it. Because America was in that back seat. And whatever was about to happen to the rest of us, the adults in that car had made a silent unanimous decision that the girl was not going to spend her last clean minutes being afraid. So we talked about nothing. We kept our voices easy. We performed, the three of us, the most important acting jobs of our lives, for an audience of one eleven-year-old who could not be allowed to see her whole family certain it was the end.

And then, with no more reason than it had come, the truck dropped back. Peeled off. Was gone. I never found out what it was or what it wanted or whether it had wanted anything at all, and you don't get to find out, and the not-finding-out is its own particular flavor of forever.

---

Five miles out from the town, the country itself stopped us.

There was a mountain off to the side with a man on top of it — there's always a man on top of it, a *halcón*, a lookout, a pair of eyes the town keeps up high to watch every road in — and that man had seen our headlights a long way off and made a call, and so before we ever reached the first house, men came out of the dark and stood our car down. Armed. Calm. Not police, nothing like police. The other authority, the real one, the one that actually owned this ground and every soul on it.

And this was the moment the whole drive had been bending toward, the one with the gringo in it. They came to the windows to find out who was coming into their town, and they found a face that did not belong — and then Rose said her family's name, and Glenda said hers, and the names landed.

Because Glenda and Rose were *from* there. Not visiting. From there, blood and bone, generations of them in that dirt, known. And in that country a known name is the only passport that has ever actually meant anything. The men looked at the women, and the women's names did the thing my useless empty wallet never could, and one of them looked back at me — the out-of-place gringo, the question mark in the back seat — and gave a small nod. *He's with them. He's fine. Let them through.*

I was accepted. Just like that. Cleared into a place no piece of paper on earth could have cleared me into, on the strength of two women who happened to love me and happened to be from the right dirt.

We drove the last five miles into town in a quiet I have never felt before or since. And I sat in the back of that car next to a sleeping girl and I did not yet know to be frightened by the right thing — by how good it had felt, getting *let in*. How easy. How much I'd liked being waved through into that world instead of turned away from it.

I'd find out. There was another mountain coming, a long way down the road, with another lookout on it and another set of armed men at the bottom — and at that one, no two women's names were going to be enough to get me back out.

But that was later. That night I was just a man who'd lived through the drive, rolling into a sleeping Sinaloa town for Christmas, grateful in all the wrong ways, breathing again.
