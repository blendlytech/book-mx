# Chapter 18

Nobody ever brought up the passport.

Four people planned that trip for three weeks. What to bring, what to cook, whose house we were staying at, whether America would miss too much school. Not one time did anybody sit down and say out loud that the man riding in the back seat had no passport, no visa, no tourist card, and two warrants waiting on him up north.

It got handled the way that family handled everything. Sideways, in Spanish, in a kitchen. Rose said something to Glenda over the stove one afternoon and Glenda said something back, and as near as I can tell that was the meeting.

I didn't push it. I'd spent two years getting good at not asking questions whose answers might cost me something, and if those two women had decided the gringo was fine, then the gringo was fine, and I was not going to be the one who talked them out of it.

---

We left Peñasco a little after six in the morning.

Rose had driven up from Mexicali the day before to collect us — three hours of desert burned before the trip even started — and she'd showed up tired, and nobody said anything about that either. She was going to drive her people home to her brother's house in Sinaloa and that was the end of the discussion.

She had that car packed like a woman going home. Not suitcases — bags, boxes, a cooler, a case of something, gifts for people I'd never heard of, and a foil pan of food she'd cooked at two in the morning that rode in the footwell where America had to keep her feet on either side of it. The trunk didn't shut on the first try. Glenda knelt on it.

North out of Peñasco on the 8, up to Sonoyta. Then east on the 2 with the border running along on our left. Caborca, and the long empty haul to Santa Ana, and at Santa Ana you get on the 15 and go south, and the 15 runs the whole spine of that country all the way to Mexico City.

The first day was nothing.

We stayed on the *cuota* — the toll road. Four lanes of good pavement, a painted line down the middle, green signs telling you the kilometers to the next town. Every so often it funnels down to a booth and a kid in a vest takes your money and lifts a gate. It runs about three hundred and forty pesos to do the length of Sonora that way. Seventeen dollars, near enough, to keep your family on the road where the rules still work.

Between the booths it was farm trucks and semis and a bus full of people asleep against the glass. Rose drove with her wrists on the wheel. Glenda kept losing radio stations and finding new ones. America had one earbud in and one out.

Hermosillo. Guaymas, with the water coming up close and hard-looking on the right. Then the road cut inland and it was wheat and cotton and irrigation ditches and dust standing in the light. The AC gave up around noon and we drove with the windows down in a roar that made everybody talk louder. We stopped for gas and bathrooms and a bag of *chicharrones* that America went through like a machine. I slept part of the afternoon with my head on the door and woke up with a wet collar.

I was bored.

---

Around five Rose started rubbing at her eye with the back of her wrist.

She'd been driving since before six. She's a woman in her sixties. She'd been trying to make Los Mochis before dark and it had quit being possible somewhere back around Guaymas, and she was going to keep going anyway. I'd had my phone out pricing rooms for the better part of an hour by then. I leaned up between the seats and told her to get off at Obregón and let me get us rooms.

She said no. She said she was fine.

I said it again, and she looked at me in the mirror a second, and then she put her blinker on, and I have wondered since whether she'd been waiting for somebody to hand her a reason.

I could afford it. The Mexicali money was still landing the way it had all fall — Zaul's floor humming, the solar leads converting, never a month under five grand — and two rooms on the outskirts of Obregón came to less than I used to drop in an hour at a video poker machine.

Rose did not like it. She tried twice to put pesos in my hand, once at the desk and once on the stairs, and both times I closed her fingers back over them. After the second time she went stiff and quiet for about ten minutes, and then it passed.

The place was out on the *periférico*. Three stories built in a U around a parking lot, white paint, tile floors, an ice machine that sounded like a rock tumbler, and a pool lit up blue in the middle of all that concrete.

We ate off a grill under a tarp across the road, tacos on paper plates that went transparent, and America ate faster than anybody and then got in that pool and stayed in it until they killed the lights.

Rose went up early. Glenda went in to shower.

So it was me in a white plastic chair at the shallow end at nine at night, and America in the water, and it was the first time in three months the two of us had been alone.

You know how it was between us. Three months in the same apartment and never a hug. I'd start to open up and she'd put a flat hand out and we'd high-five, and that was the whole arrangement. She'd known me for years as a face in her mother's phone and then the face moved into the kitchen and she'd been walking a wide circle around it ever since.

She hung on the edge in the deep end and started talking to me in English.

That girl spoke good English and had nobody on earth to spend it on. Not her mother, not her grandmother, not one kid at that school. So she used it on me. She told me a long story about a girl at school and a chair that I could not follow and that had no ending. She did her English teacher's accent, which was devastating. She asked whether American schools really have lockers like on the shows and I said they do and she said that was stupid.

Then she got quiet. She pushed off the wall and went out to the middle of the pool and turned so she was facing away from me, toward the far end, and she asked it in that direction instead of at me:

Are you going to stay with my mom?

There wasn't a gap between the question and the answer. Nothing got in there.

I told her I was staying. I said it warm and I said it certain — no hedge in it, no *we'll see*, not one of the little seams a true thing usually has in it. That we'd all be going back to Peñasco after Christmas. That she'd be in school in January and I'd be right there. And I heard my own voice doing it, and I knew the voice. It was the one I used on marks to get them into a chair. But I meant it.

She said okay.

She got out and got her towel, and on her way past she put her hand up, and I slapped it, and she went in to her grandmother's room.

I sat out there a while feeling wonderful about myself.

---

Glenda came out later with wet hair and we sat on the concrete stairs at the end of the second floor where you could see the highway going by past the parking lot.

I told her what America had asked me and what I'd said.

She was so happy. That's what I remember. She wasn't suspicious of it and she didn't warn me about anything and she didn't say one wise thing — she just got happy, and grabbed my arm, and told me her daughter had never once asked her real father anything like that. Not in eleven years. Never bothered to.

So there were two of them believing it by the time we went upstairs.

I didn't have a drink on that whole drive. Not day one and not day two. Rose was in the car and I'd promised that woman I'd keep her daughter sober, so we just didn't, and it wasn't hard, and I never even walked down to the OXXO on the corner, though I could see the sign from those stairs.

I filed it away as more proof. Since that beach in Peñasco I'd been telling myself I'd finally gotten hold of the thing, and here were two more days of evidence for the file.

I slept like a baby in a paid-for room with the AC going.

---

We got a late start.

Everybody slept in. America got the pool one more time. Rose wanted a proper breakfast, and something got left in a room and somebody walked back up for it, and the woman at the front desk turned out to know somebody Rose knew, which down there costs you twenty minutes minimum.

It was after eleven before we rolled. Seven o'clock would have put us at her brother's house in daylight.

We crossed into Sinaloa around midday and came down through Los Mochis in the early afternoon with the sun still high on the fields. Then Guasave. Then the long green run south, cane and tomatoes and irrigation water throwing light.

Sometime after dark I noticed we hadn't paid a toll in a while.

---

It isn't a moment, getting off the right road. It's a slow leak.

The pavement changed first. You feel that before you see it — the road noise dropped a note and went grittier and the seams started coming up through the tires in a rhythm. Then the shoulder narrowed and then there wasn't one. The painted line got faint, then intermittent, then gone. The green signs quit.

We came through a little town. One street, a *tope* that near took the oil pan out from under us, a store with its light still on and three men standing out front of it who turned and watched the car the whole way through. Out the far side and back into the black.

Nobody said anything for a good while. All four of us knew, and every one of us was waiting for somebody else to be the one.

Then Glenda, without turning around:

Mamá.

Ya lo sé.

That was the whole conversation. *I know already.* Rose's hands moved on the wheel to a place they hadn't been all day. Glenda held her phone up near the windshield, tipping it, hunting a signal the way you hunt a station. One bar, then no bars, then not even the little x.

I asked what was going on. Glenda said her mother must have missed a sign somewhere back before that town — the *cuota* bent off one way and the *libre* went straight on and we went straight on.

Two roads run down through that country. The *cuota*, where you pay for a road somebody is still responsible for. And the *libre*, free the way a thing is free when nobody has claimed it, running down through the little towns and the long black stretches that belong after dark to whoever out there is able to hold them.

I asked if we could turn around.

Glenda said something to her mother and her mother said something short back, and then Glenda told me the next place to get across was a long way behind us. An hour, she thought. Maybe more. And then that same hour again coming back down.

So that was the decision, and there was nothing dramatic in it. Four tired people at the end of a long day looked at two hours of extra dark and picked the smaller number. Nobody argued. Nobody even really discussed it.

We kept going.

The first thing through me was not the family. It was a fast selfish loop about myself — the warrants, deportation, a Mexican jail, what happens to a man with no papers in a country that has no record of him being in it — and running under that a thin mean thread of *this woman got us onto the wrong road*, which I had the decency not to say and not the decency to not think.

Then I went through what I could contribute and came up with nothing. Couldn't show anybody a document. Couldn't call anybody. Couldn't drive, because a gringo behind the wheel ends the whole thing on sight. Couldn't talk — I had maybe forty words of Spanish and most of them were indecent.

So I sat still and stayed quiet and hoped nobody looked at me. That was my entire contribution to the rest of that night.

---

The cops got us about twenty minutes later.

Lights behind us, no siren, just the bar going and red and blue washing around the inside of the car. Rose put her blinker on, which I remember because of how ordinary it was, and pulled us onto gravel and weeds.

Two of them, in a truck. One came up to Rose's window with a flashlight. The other one didn't come up at all. He stood off the back corner where you'd have to turn most of the way around to see him.

Rose had her window down and both hands on top of the wheel before he got there. Glenda had her purse open and her ID out already. Neither of them had discussed it. They'd been doing this their whole lives and I was the only one in that car finding out it was a thing people do.

The flashlight went over Rose, over Glenda, back over the seat onto America — asleep sideways with an earbud in — and then it came onto me and stopped.

I don't know how long. It felt like a while.

Then: *Pasaporte.*

That was the only word in the next four minutes I understood.

Glenda started talking, fast and low and pleasant. Rose came in under her, steadier, in a voice I had never heard out of her. I caught *familia.* I caught *Navidad.* I think I caught the word for uncle. That is everything I got. Two women eighteen inches from my face negotiating what my freedom was worth, and I sat there with my hands flat on my knees like a dog that's been told to stay.

Six years I made my living moving strangers with nothing but a voice. I could hear a man go from no to maybe through a receiver. And I could not say one word in the only negotiation of my life that ever actually mattered, because I had never bothered to learn the language of the country I'd been living in for a year.

It went back and forth. Glenda said a number. He said a different number.  She had to offer it first, and in Mexico that is normal, but always a gamble.  You never know if you could get that one cop who couldn't be bribed.  It was already clear the gringo in the back didn't have documents because I couldn't produce any when asked.  He wasn't asking for long.  A bribe was our only option.

A thousand pesos. About fifty dollars.

Rose had it folded in her purse. He took it, put it in his shirt pocket without counting it, knocked twice on the roof and said something I didn't catch, and then they got in their truck and went up the road ahead of us and their taillights got small and then there was nothing.

Nobody spoke for a mile. Then Glenda laughed one syllable with nothing in it and said we were lucky.

---

We had to have gas.

Rose didn't want to stop. I watched her look at the needle and then the road and then the needle, but the light was on and she kept going anyway, and an hour or so on we pulled into a Pemex sitting out by itself with two pumps, a lit canopy, and a kid behind the counter inside.

I got out to stretch, and I remember being glad to be out of that car, which tells you how stupid two days of sitting will make a person about his own situation. It was still warm. The air smelled like gasoline and creosote. Past the light of the canopy there was nothing at all, just black and the sound of insects going in the brush.

Down there a man pumps it for you. Glenda handled it. I was standing by the back fender when the truck came in.

It didn't pull up to a pump. It came in off the road and swung wide and stopped at an angle close to us, engine running, and nobody got out. The window came down.

He was maybe thirty-five, ball cap, an elbow up on the sill. He said something to the kid pumping our gas and the kid stopped pumping. Mid-fill. Hand still on the handle. Looked at the ground.

Then he said something to Glenda. Six or seven words, and quiet — he never raised his voice, not one time.

I didn't understand a syllable of it. What I understood was my girlfriend's body. Her shoulders came down and in and her chin dropped about a half inch and she went from a woman buying gas to something small and agreeable in a second and a half. She said *sí, sí, claro.*

Then she came around the fender and didn't look at the truck and said into the side of my face, in English:

Give me the gas money.

I gave it to her. She walked it over and handed it up through the window.

He counted it with his thumb. That's the piece I've never been able to shake — how routine it was. A man in an idling truck counting bills in the dark like a route driver collecting off a vending machine. He said something else short and she nodded twice and came back and said get in.

We left before the tank was full. America said from the back, wasn't he going to finish, and Glenda said in a completely normal voice that we were in a hurry.

I don't actually know what happened at that gas station.

I got two different versions later at her uncle's kitchen table, days apart. In the first one the man was collecting — you don't pay the counter out there, you pay whoever the station answers to, and the counter is just paperwork sitting on top of an arrangement. In the second one he was nobody at all, some local with a mouth on him, and Glenda paid him because it was nine at night in the middle of nowhere with a kid and a gringo in the car and forty dollars is forty dollars.

The two women told it different ways on different nights and neither of them ever seemed to think it mattered which. That's the thing I can't get comfortable about, even now. Not knowing whether I was standing next to something enormous or whether I got scared of a guy in a hat.

Either way it was my money. That part is not in dispute.

---

The lights came up behind us sometime after that.

There wasn't a beginning to it. You're on a black road with nothing behind you and then there's a set of headlights back there a ways, and that's not anything, that's a car, cars exist.

Then they were closer. Then they were close, and high — a truck, sitting up over our back glass — and it came to about two car lengths and quit closing and stayed there.

Rose let off. Not obvious. Just let it fall away to eighty, eighty-five, and gave him the whole rest of an empty road to go around us on.

He came down with her and held the same two lengths.

Then his high beams came on and the inside of the car went white — my hands, the ceiling, the back of Glenda's head, all of it lit up hard, and our own shadows stood up off the dash onto the windshield. Rose reached up and turned the rearview away so it wasn't in her eyes.

I turned around one time. There was nothing to see. You can't see a truck behind lights like that. No cab, no windshield, no driver, just two suns coming at you out of the black. I turned back around and didn't do it again.

Glenda said it had been forty-five minutes. I'd have sworn to you it was two hours.

Most of that time is gone. What's left is pieces.

One of them is this. I worked out that if they ran us off the road, the brush came up close on the right and I could get into it. I mapped it out. The door handle, which way I'd go, how long it would take anybody behind us to sort out which one of us had run.

Four people in that car and I planned an exit for one.

I would never do that, but I thought it. In detail, with an eleven-year-old asleep against my shoulder. I felt ashamed that I was even capable of that thought. Then I thought about how I would grab her by the wrist, and not let go and just run. Then I thought how ridiculous that would be.

I wanted a drink. There wasn't one for fifty miles and I knew it and I wanted one anyway, hard, out of nowhere, after two days of not thinking about it once and congratulating myself the whole time.

And I prayed a little, badly, the way you do.

Then America woke up.

She came up out of it somewhere in the middle of all that white light with her hair stuck to one side of her face, and she sat up and looked out the back and then at the three of us.

Glenda said something in Spanish.

Nobody laughed. Rose said something after a second and Glenda answered her and it died. Glenda tried again, I would learn later would be about the way her uncle drives, and Rose answered that, and that died too. I said something. I don't remember what. I remember hearing my own voice come out too loud and too bright, and hearing that it was too loud and too bright, and not being able to do one thing about it.

Two, three minutes of that. Then it quit, and after that nobody talked at all.

America didn't ask again. She got small against the door and shut her eyes and stayed that way.

She wasn't asleep. I could see her breathing and it wasn't sleeping breathing.

I never said anything to her. Anything I said would have carried to the front seat and I couldn't trust my own voice, so I let her lie there pretending, and I sat there letting her, and we did that together the rest of the way.

Then the lights fell back.

No reason. He dropped to fifty yards, a hundred, and then his blinker came on — his blinker, the little orange courtesy of it after all that — and he turned off onto some cut in the brush I never saw.

The mirror had nothing in it.

Nobody spoke for miles. Then Rose let a long breath out through her teeth and reached over and put her hand on Glenda's leg, and Glenda put hers on top of it, and they drove like that a while.

I never found out what that truck was, or whether it was anything at all. You don't get to find out. Nobody comes back later and tells you.

---

Five miles out from the town they stopped us.

The lights of the place were up ahead by then, that low orange scatter you can see a long way off across flat ground. There was a mountain off to the right, black on a lesser black. Glenda said, not pointing, that there was a man up there. She said it in Spanish, but somehow, I understood. The words didn't matter. You learn the shape of those sentences after a while — the ones that mean *we are being watched*, or *we are not alone*, or *this is where the trouble is*. You can pick them out even when you can't catch the words.

There's always a man up there. A *halcón* — a lookout, eyes the town keeps high to watch the roads coming in. He'd have had our headlights twenty minutes before we got close.

They came out of the brush on both sides at the same time.

There was nothing to see and then there were men in our headlights, standing in the road, and Rose was already braking hard and all of us went into our belts and the food pan slid out from under America's feet.

Four of them, maybe five. Young. Two had rifles, held down along the leg. Nothing about them said police — no uniform, no patch, no vehicle with anything written on it, no lights. Just men in a road at eleven at night, taking their time.

One came to Rose's window. One walked around and stood a step off my door and looked at me through the glass with his hand not on the rifle and not off it.

The window came down.

He looked at me a long time. I looked at the seat back in front of me. Even I knew not to meet his eye and not to look away too hard.

And then Rose said her family's name.

Just said it. Flat, no pleading in it, the way you say a name to somebody who ought to already know it — her name, and her brother's name, and where the house is. I only know all this because it was explained to me later over laughter about how scared I must have been. Oh man, did they laugh.  It was quite the joke.  I was just the idiot gringo in the back seat, along for the ride.

Then Glenda said her name, and where she was born, and the school she went to.

The one at the window said a name back, a question, checking. Rose answered him and added something. He nodded a little.

Then he looked across the roof at the one standing by my door, and the one by my door looked at me one more time — the gringo, the thing in that car that didn't go with the rest of it — and gave a small nod of his own and stepped back off the door.

And the men in the road walked out of our headlights and into the dark and were not there anymore.

Glenda always said it was nothing. That they were only checking who was coming into town, that it happens, that we were never in a bit of danger. Maybe that's so. She also spent that whole drive telling America there were idiots on the road.

What I know is that two women said their names, and a man with a rifle made up his mind about me, and it took less than two minutes, and no piece of paper in the world would have done what those names did.

---

We drove the last five miles in a quiet I have never had before or since. Nobody turned the radio back on. Rose drove about forty. Dirt, then some pavement, then dogs in the road, and a wall with a mural on it, and lit windows, and a man carrying a case of beer across the street who didn't look up at us.

I sat in the back next to a girl who was still pretending to be asleep, with somebody's cooking against my shoe, and I felt good.

Not relieved. *Good.* Warm. There was something in getting waved through that landed in me the way a drink lands, and I sat in that back seat at eleven at night feeling like I'd been let into something.

It didn't occur to me to wonder about that. Not that night.

I did wonder about it later. About a year later, at a camp in the mountains a couple hours outside Rosarito, where there was another man on another hill and another set of men in the road and not one name on earth that was going to get me back out.

But that's a ways off yet. That night I was just a man who'd lived through a drive, coming into a sleeping Sinaloa town three weeks out from Christmas, and I slept fine.
