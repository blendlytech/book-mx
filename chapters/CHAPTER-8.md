Here is the part nobody warns you about. The trap that finally gets you almost never looks like a trap.

It looks like dinner.

But before dinner, it looked like a phone.

Before I let Paralee drive me anywhere, I peel a hundred dollars off Niki's roll. I buy the cheapest cellphone in the store. A plastic nothing. But it lights up. It gives me a number.

After a week of being erased, borrowing stranger's phones to prove I exist, I cannot explain what that cheap screen does to me. I can be called. I can call. I watch it wake up and I feel, God help me, powerful.

Remember that feeling. It does not last the night.

Paralee lives on the east side of town. The neighborhood has its act together. Real buildings. Working streetlights. A second-story apartment with a balcony. Tribal print. The smell of smoked salmon and stale cigarettes. It smells like a home.

After four days of jail and rain, walking into it feels like being handed something I have no business holding.

Carl is Paralee's husband. Early fifties. Black. Expertly drunk. He greets me like a returning soldier. Serves up the salmon and asparagus. I crack the case of Alaskan IPAs I carried up the stairs. Paralee smokes from her private stash. Carl spins a twenty-year love story over the bones of the fish. I laugh the kind of laugh that uses your whole chest.

We finish the case. Carl goes out the far side of drunk. I sit back, full of fish and beer, and think: I made it. A few weeks in the state, and I am at a warm table with my two new best friends.

Before the night gets loose, I slip off to a corner. I put the new phone to work. I reply to the onboarding email from Manny, my boss. I'm alive. Long story. I'm back, and I'm ready to work.

Everything rides on Manny reading it. The job. The reason I flew two thousand miles north. I fire the flare and spend the night stealing glances at a dark screen. Nothing comes back. I tell myself he is asleep on desert time. The beer carries me past it.

The talk drifts to the phones. The solar. The money. Carl lights up. He is out of work. The two of them are scraping. The financial rot is sitting under the apartment like an untraceable draft. Carl is dead certain he would be perfect for the job. I say sure, Carl, absolutely. We should talk. The way you say it when the beer is talking and nobody is writing anything down.

For four hours, I have the white-picket version of a life. I want that on the record. The rest comes fast.

Past eleven. I offer to grab a cab. Paralee won't hear of it. She drives me.

The second the doors shut and the engine turns over, the temperature changes.

Twenty minutes belted into the passenger seat. The solstice sun is still up. She lays it out low and steady. She and Carl are finished. The warm house is a show she is tired of putting on. She wants out.

I do not want one square inch of this. I try to walk her back to safer ground. Twenty years, Carl's a good guy, you'll find your way back. Careful. Like walking around a person standing too close to an edge.

Then she says the part I felt coming like weather. I really need to find a guy like you.

I know exactly where the car is going. It isn't the hostel.

I do the only thing I know how to do to stop a person walking toward me. I put myself on the ground. Oh, you don't want a guy like me, I tell her. I am a terrible boyfriend. I am too selfish to be any good to anybody.

I paint myself as ruined. Thick and ugly. A man bragging invites a woman in. A man confessing he's garbage shuts the door soft. And I need the door shut.

I don't know if she was making a pass or just being lonely out loud. It doesn't matter. She drives away knowing I am not on the menu.

She pulls up to the hostel. The lot is empty. The front is dark. A Domino's glows across the street. Four doors down, like the universe read my file, is an open liquor store. Before I get out, she makes me dig out the brand-new phone. Punch her number in. "There," she says. "No excuse not to call." I save the contact figuring I will never lay eyes on her again.

I try the front door of the hostel. Locked. A sign on the glass: 7 a.m. – 10 p.m.

You have got to be fucking kidding me.

Here is the joke. I have a phone. Charged. A live number for the first time in a week. And not one contact in it can open that door. I could call a cab, but a cab takes you somewhere. The only somewhere I have is on the other side of that glass. The one number that would come scoop me up belongs to Paralee. I would sleep in the gutter before I called her back to play rescuer.

I drift around the side of the building. A back lot. Plastic tables. Lawn chairs. And four doors down, the liquor store.

I say fuck it. I walk down, buy a six-pack, carry it to the lawn chair. I drink every one in the cold. Thumbing the dark phone between sips for a reply from Manny. I pass out cold. The phone sits on the table beside the empties.

Twelve inches of drywall behind my back is a clean cot with my name on it. It might as well be on the moon.

I wake up with a stiff neck and a head like a struck bell. Six empty bottles stand at attention on the table. Exactly how they would be lined up at home. Some part of me still keeping house in a blackout. The bottles are there. The phone is not.

I pat my pockets. Nothing. I sleep through a stranger walking up and lifting it clean off the table. I owned it for less than twelve hours. Robbed again. The city reaching into my pocket the second I close my eyes. Making sure I haven't forgotten whose table I am sitting at. It is light out. It is always light out. No phone. No watch. No idea what time it is.

I clear the bottles. Hide the evidence. I wait fifteen minutes. A tall, unsmiling man with a push broom comes around the corner.

My first read on the broom is weapon. He is big. I am a filthy stranger in his back lot. He looks me over. Asks flat if I am staying here. I give him my name. The paper ID. Locked out. His face cracks. "You should've just knocked on my door," he says. He points to a sun-faded trailer I took for abandoned. He softens. He grins. "I thought you were a meth head, man. Thought I was gonna have to whoop ya." I laugh. Mostly out of relief that the broom is just a broom.

He lets me in. The pod is a hole in the wall. A cot. A bolted desk. I fold myself into the box and sleep a couple of hard hours. The first roof that is only mine.

The climb back out is never one clean grab. It is a hundred small ones. Most slip.

Plan: buy the second cheapest phone at Walmart. Phone number two. Phone number one is gone with the email thread.

I check in with the owner. Laura. Six-thirty in the morning. Laura is a talker. She knows everyone and everything. I give her the short version of my week. She goes wide-eyed. Grabs my arm. "Mountain View?" "Oh, honey," she says. "You are lucky to be alive."

She gives examples. Tourists disappearing. Driven out to the woods and left for the bears. The bear eats the evidence and the body. Eight tourists this year. Eaten by bear, assumed. I sit there with my free oatmeal going cold. I run the addict's arithmetic. The only math where getting beaten, robbed, and jailed equals a win. I am lucky to be alive. That is the house's way of telling you it hasn't decided to collect yet. The bears aren't bad luck. The bears are the table. The table is patient.

Then Laura does a strange kindness. She has a laptop. Belonged to a tourist. Checked out for a hike a month back and never returned. No password. Sitting in the back room. I can use it.

I am looking at a dead man's laptop. A man digesting somewhere in the Chugach mountains. I am invited to rebuild my life on his machine.

I never open his folders. Whatever lives in there—photographs, half-finished emails, the people still texting a man the mountains ate—I leave sealed in the dark. I need the machine, not the man.

Reader, I build an empire on that ghost's laptop.

I get the new phone. I claw back into my email. A week-long war with the phone company over two-factor authentication for my bank. Trench warfare to prove I exist. The punchline? I call Arizona, and they mail me a new ID in two minutes. No verification. A week for a bank card, two minutes for the plastic that cost me countless miles of walking and a tour of an insane asylum. The bureaucracy drowns you in a teaspoon and hands you the ocean for free.

I get back on the phones.

Manny falls out of his chair when I surface. I thought you fell off a mountain, man. I tell him the story. He asks if I need money. He wires five hundred for me, and five hundred to Niki to pay her back.

Sit with that math. I work one week, then vanish off the earth for another. A no-call, no-show disappearance. The response from the top is a thousand dollars wired before I close a single new deal. It isn't charity. It is a good bet. They know what I am worth on the phones. A great closer is always worth more than he costs you. I earn it back by Thursday.

Inside of days, I am killing it. Two hundred, four hundred, twelve hundred dollars a day. The WhatsApp threads light up fast and hot. The machine I left in Vegas hums on a hostel desk on hardware belonging to a bear's lunch. The con outlives the mark. It always does.

I am king of the castle again. Glenda has me saved as Alaskan closer. We haven't exchanged one human sentence. Just deals flying down the wire. Opener and closer. I don't know it for what it is. I just know the threads ping, the money comes, and I feel like myself. I feel great. Feeling great is the most expensive thing I know how to feel.

I give up on my stolen stuff. The guy ghosts me twice. I walk into Best Buy and drop nine hundred dollars on a top-of-the-line laptop on clearence. I start buying new clothes. I am redressing the set. I mistake the new costume for a new man. Smoke and mirrors run on myself. The easiest mark in the building.

There are good days. Real ones. I hit the local bars. The hostel is a river of travelers. Laura puts me in charge of the Fourth of July cookout. I flip burgers in the white midnight daylight for a yard full of strangers. Briefly, I feel like a person who belongs.

But that first polite yes is still out there. Compounding.

Paralee starts coming by daily. Unloading about Carl and the marriage. It stops being about checking on me. Carl does the drunk math. He decides I am sleeping with his wife. I never touched the woman. Spent three weeks actively fleeing her. The texts start coming. Carl typing with one eye open. Ugly threats to my life. Then the threats curdle into a shakedown. i know what your doing with my wife. three hundred by friday closer boy or i come find you. Murder one minute, an invoice the next.

Then, at four in the morning. Out of nowhere. you know that was the best salmon you ever ate.

God help me, it was. The warm man from the dinner, reduced to shaking down a stranger for grocery money, then fishing for a compliment on his cooking. I never send a dime. But it sits there. Paralee gets bolder.

I do what I always do when a place gets complicated. I leave.

I book a flight out. Guess where the flight goes. The universe has a sense of humor and its whole act is callbacks.

Manny drops two tickets in my lap like a tip. Front row. Dave Chappelle and Joe Rogan at the MGM. Find somebody to take. In Las Vegas.  He can no longer make it.

Fuck my life. The exact city I fled three chapters ago. The place I starved and bled to escape. The wheel comes around to the same green felt it started on. The geographic cure's punchline is always the town you ran from.

The last loose end plays like a closing bit. My debit card arrives. The UPS driver misses me and hauls it back to the hub. Eight miles away. I make the walk on a beautiful Saturday afternoon. I have cash from a hostel friend. Broke on paper, but not stuck.

Standing at the UPS counter. Card in hand. Plane ticket in pocket. Money in my account. It all worked out.

It is the single most expensive sentence I have ever told myself. I tell it at the top of every flight of stairs I fall down. I am going back to Vegas. Smoothed-out and tan and dead sure I will walk out clean.

Except the court date I skipped has passed. The Vegas warrant is officially active. One slip and I am looking at a year in a cell. I book the flight anyway. Devil hates a coward.
