Here is the part nobody warns you about. The trap that finally gets you almost never looks like a trap.

It looks like dinner.

But before dinner, it looked like a phone.

The first thing I did with my second chance — before I let Paralee drive me anywhere that evening — was peel a hundred dollars off Niki's roll and buy the cheapest cellphone in the store. A plastic nothing, barely a phone at all, but it lit up when I plugged it in and gave me back a number — and after a week as a man nobody on earth could reach, erased, reduced to borrowing phones off strangers to prove I still existed, I can't put into words what that cheap little screen did for me. Now I could be called. Now I could call. I watched it wake up and felt, God help me, *powerful.* Remember that feeling. It didn't last the night.

Paralee lived twenty minutes from my pod and ten from the Harris Teeter, on the east side of town, in a neighborhood that had its act together in a way Mountain View never would. Real buildings. Streetlights that worked. Lawns somebody had decided were worth the trouble. A second-story apartment with a balcony, one bedroom, every wall of it done up in tribal print and warm color and the kind of small careful objects a person collects when they've made up their mind that *this*, whatever else, is going to be a home. It smelled like a home too — like cooking, like cigarettes gone stale in the good way, like a place that had been lived in on purpose. After four days of jail and rain, walking into it felt like being handed something I had no business holding.

And there was Carl.

Carl was Paralee's husband, and Carl was a *situation.* Heavyset Black man, early fifties, with a laugh that started somewhere down around his belt and a glass that never once went dry the whole night. He was already drunk when I got there — comfortably, expertly drunk, the drunk of a man who'd been at it since noon and had nowhere to be — and he greeted me like I was a soldier home from the war. He'd cooked. Smoked salmon, asparagus, rice, all of it laid out and timed to the minute, because the man could *cook,* genuinely cook, the kind of plate that makes you go quiet for the first three bites. I'd stopped on the way and grabbed a case of assorted Alaskan IPAs for the occasion, sweating cold against my arm up the stairs, and I cracked one and it was the best thing I'd tasted in a year.

Paralee didn't drink. She had her own little jar going, a private stash she'd thumb a pinch from and smoke off in the corner like a woman tending a small fire, and that was her speed and it suited her. I stuck to the beer. Carl stuck to whatever Carl was sticking to, and the two of us got after the case.

Over dinner they told me how they found each other. Cordova, more than twenty years back — Carl up on a fishing trip, Paralee working a bar near the water. He told it himself, of course he did, leaned back with the plate pushed away and his hands going, and the man could *spin* a thing. How he walked in that first day and clocked her section and parked himself in it. How he came back the next day, and the next, every single day of that trip, made damn sure he sat where she was working, and tipped her — the way he told it — well past the point of subtlety and clean into the point of *intent.* Six months later they were married. Twenty-some years after that they were still at it, and he told the whole arc of it with his wife sitting right there smiling at her jar, and I laughed the entire night, the real kind, the kind that uses your whole chest.

We finished the case. Carl, who'd come in drunk, went out the far side of drunk and kept going, and somewhere in there I sat back in my chair full of good fish and cold beer and other people's love story, and I thought: there it is. I made it. I'd been in this state a matter of weeks and here I was at a warm table with what I'd already, God help me, decided were my two new best friends in Alaska.

Somewhere in that first hour, before the night got loose, I'd slipped off to a corner of the warm apartment and put the new phone to its first real work: an email to Manny, my boss back in the Lower 48. *I'm alive. Long story. I'm back, and I'm ready — I responded to the origninal email the onboarding documents came in and I had signed a week prior.* Everything rode on Manny getting that email. The job, the whole reason I'd flown two thousand miles north — all of it hung on getting back into that man's good graces, and I fired the flare and then spent the rest of the night stealing glances at a screen that stayed stubbornly dark. Nothing came back. Not that night. I told myself it was late, it was a weekend, he was asleep on desert time — and I let the dinner and the beer carry me past it.

The talk drifted to my work — the phones, the solar, the money — and Carl lit up. Carl knew, with the bone-deep certainty of a man three sheets in, that he'd be *perfect* for it. Born for it. He'd been out of work for months, he told me, the two of them scraping, the financial thing sitting under everything in that apartment like a draft you couldn't find the source of. I said sure, Carl, absolutely, we should talk — the way you say it when the beer's talking and the night's good and nobody's writing anything down.

For about four hours that night I had the whole thing. The white-picket version. I want that on the record before I tell you the rest of it, because the rest of it came fast.

Before any of us clocked it, it was past eleven. I said I'd grab a cab. Paralee wouldn't hear of it — she'd drive me, she insisted, and I took the favor the way I'd been taking favors all week, with both hands and a guilty conscience, one more kindness I hadn't earned.

The second the doors shut and the engine turned over, the night changed temperature.

Twenty minutes she had me belted into that passenger seat, the solstice sun still up outside the glass well past eleven at night, and she used every one of them. She laid it out low and steady — she and Carl were finished; the warm, funny house I'd just spent four hours inside was a show she'd grown tired of putting on; she wanted *out,* away from him, gone. After the dinner I'd just sat through, it stopped me cold. I did not want one square inch of it. I kept trying to walk her back to safer ground — hey, everybody hits a rough patch, you two have twenty years, you'll find your way back — careful with it, careful with her, the way you go careful around a person standing too near an edge.

Then she said the part I'd felt coming the way you feel weather. *I really need to find a guy like you.* Good job. Good head on his shoulders.

And I knew exactly where the car was going, and it wasn't the hostel.

So I did the only thing I know how to do when I need a person to stop walking toward me: I put myself on the ground. I'm done with relationships, I told her, done, finished — and I meant it to sound final, total, a closed gate. I'm a terrible boyfriend, Paralee. I've tried, believe me, more times than you'd think. I'm just too selfish a person to be any good to anybody. I painted myself as ruined on purpose, laid it on thick and ugly, because a man bragging about what a catch he is invites a woman in and a man confessing he's garbage shuts the door soft, and I needed that door shut. To this day I don't honestly know if she was making a pass or just being kind and lonely out loud at midnight. Didn't matter. What mattered was that she drove away knowing, for certain, that I was not on the menu.

She pulled up to the hostel. It looked all right — looked fine, even — though the lot was poorly lit and the whole front of the place sat in shadow. A Domino's glowed across the street. And four doors down, naturally, predictably, like the universe had read my file: a liquor store, still lit, still open. Paralee put the car in park and before she let me out she made me dig out that brand-new phone and punch her number into it right there, thumbs on the screen, no torn scrap to conveniently lose. *There,* she said. *Now you've got no excuse not to call.* She'd love to hang out. Sure, I said, thank you, thank you for everything — and I thanked her over and over and I meant all of it, even as I saved the contact figuring I'd never lay eyes on the woman again. She drove off. I stood in the lot and thought: *finally. I'm back.*

Then I tried the front door, and it was locked.

I could see straight through the glass to the front desk, dark, unmanned, nobody home. And there on the door, in print I read three times like rereading it would change it: *7 a.m. – 10 p.m.*

You have *got* to be kidding me.

And here is the joke the whole night had been quietly setting up: I finally had a phone — barely an hour old, charged, a live number in my pocket for the first time in a week — and not one contact in it could open that door. I could call a cab, sure, but a cab takes you *somewhere,* and the only somewhere I had in the world was right on the other side of that locked glass. The one number in there that would've come and scooped me up belonged to Paralee — and I had just spent twenty minutes in her car prying her fingers loose. I'd have slept in the gutter before I called her back to play rescuer two hours later; that's a door you do not open unless you've decided to walk all the way through it. So my big triumph of the evening sat in my pocket and did me not one bit of good. I stood there running the short, sorry list of what was actually left. I thought about crossing to the Domino's and throwing myself on somebody's mercy — for what, exactly, I couldn't have told you — and I'd half turned to do it when I drifted around the side of the building instead and found a little back lot. A community area, sort of. A couple of plastic tables. Some lawn chairs. And four doors down, plain as anything in that midnight daylight, the liquor store. Still open.

I want to tell you I sat down and thought it through. I didn't. I said *fuck it* and I walked down and bought a six-pack and carried it back to that lawn chair and I sat there in the cold and drank it, every one, thumbing that dark little phone between sips for a reply from Manny that never came, until I passed clean out with the thing sitting right there on the table beside the empties. I knew — I *knew* — that on the other side of the wall at my back was a clean cot in a pod with my name on it. It was maybe twelve inches of drywall away. And it might as well have been on the moon, because it didn't open till seven, so it would have to wait, and in the meantime I had a chair, and I had the store, and that was a math I'd been good at my whole life.

I came to with a stiff neck and a head like a struck bell. Six empty bottles stood at attention on the table in front of me, lined up neat as soldiers, exactly the way they'd have been lined up at home — some part of me, even blackout drunk in a strange city, still keeping house. The bottles were there. The phone was not.

I knew it before I'd finished patting myself down — the table bare where I'd left it, nothing in my pockets, nothing under the chair, nothing in the grass. Somebody had walked up on a man passed out cold in a hostel lot and lifted that phone clean off the table, and I'd slept through the whole thing without so much as stirring. I'd owned it less than twelve hours. *Less than twelve hours.* Robbed again — in my sleep this time, which was a fresh new low even for that week — the city reaching right back into my pocket the instant I shut my eyes, just to make sure I hadn't forgotten whose table I was still sitting at. It was light out. Of course it was light out. It was *always* light out up there, that endless smeared Alaska daylight that tells you nothing, and now, all over again, I had no phone and no watch and no idea on God's earth what time it was. Less than a day on top, and I was back to nothing.

I cleaned the bottles off the table — old reflex, hide the evidence — and lit a cigarette and sat there waiting for any sign of life. Took maybe fifteen minutes. Then it came around the corner in the shape of a tall, unsmiling man with a push broom.

I'll be honest, my first read on the broom was not *janitor.* My first read was *weapon.* He was big, he wasn't smiling, and I was a filthy stranger sitting in his back lot at dawn surrounded by the ghosts of a six-pack. He looked me over and asked, flat, was I staying here. Yes, I said, and gave him my name and dug out the paper ID, told him I'd gotten in after hours and found the place locked. His face cracked. *Oh* — you should've just knocked on my door, he said, and nodded at a little trailer off to the side of the lot, a thing so sun-faded and forgotten-looking I'd taken it for abandoned. How in the hell, I wanted to ask him, would I have known to knock on *that,* or had the first idea who or what was going to answer if I did. I didn't say it. He was already softening. Come on, he said, I'll let you in, get you your pod — Laura'll have to do the real check-in in the morning. And then, grinning now, almost friendly: I thought you were a meth head, man. Thought I was gonna have to whoop ya. He laughed. I laughed too, mostly out of relief that the broom was just a broom.

He let me in and the pod was exactly what I'd known it would be from the website and somehow smaller — a hole in the wall, a cot, a little desk bolted under it, and that was the whole inventory. *You got no bags?* he asked, looking at my empty hands. I did not have the energy to tell that man the story of my week. They're coming, I said. I folded myself into the box, and I slept a couple of hard hours, and that was the first roof that was only mine in longer than I could stand to count.

The next part was war, and I want to be straight about it, because the way you climb back out of a hole like that is never one clean grab. It's a hundred small ones, and most of them slip.

The plan was simple in the way that all my plans were simple right up until the world got its hands on them: hit Walmart and buy the cheapest phone they sold. Again. The second cheapest phone in as many days — phone number two of the Alaska campaign, because somebody out in that lot was now the proud owner of phone number one and the dead little email thread still riding inside it. It was Friday. I had onboarding documents sitting in my inbox from Manny, plus that flare I'd shot him from Paralee's couch that he still hadn't answered, and the whole reason I had a job at all hung on me reaching that inbox. So the phone was everything. The phone was the key to the kingdom — the very same key it had been the night before, right up until the kingdom picked my pocket — assuming this one would let me back into my own email, which, after the week I'd had with codes and verifications and machines slamming shut in my face, was not a thing I was willing to assume.

But first, six-thirty in the morning, the real check-in, and the owner. Laura.

I'd made myself a cup of the free coffee and a bowl of the free oatmeal they keep out in the common area, and I was sitting at the little table I recognized from the photos online — everything looked a third smaller in person, the whole place did — when Laura showed up fifteen minutes early and squared me away. Laura was a *talker.* One of those women who knows everyone and everything and has a strong opinion on all of it and will hand it to you whether you reached for it or not. She got me checked in, and I gave her the short version of my week, Mountain View and the rest, and she went wide-eyed and grabbed my arm.

Oh, honey, she said. You are *lucky to be alive.*

And then she told me why, and I want you to understand she told it the way you'd tell somebody the bus runs late — plainly, with examples. People disappeared out of Mountain View, she said. Regular. What they'd do, the ones working that angle, was rob you and then drive you way out into the woods, somewhere you'd never walk your way back from, tie you off, and leave you for the bears — because a bear is the one witness that eats the evidence and the body both. Eight tourists *that year,* she said. Eight. Gone, never heard from again. Eaten by bear, assumed. She said it like a stamp. *Eaten by bear, assumed.*

And don't go hiking, she added, almost as an afterthought, because — and this was my favorite part, the part that has stayed with me — since COVID the bears had gone hungry. No tourists on the trails for a couple of seasons meant no easy meals, so the bears had gotten lean and bold and a little entitled about it, and now that people were trickling back, the trails were doing some collecting of their own. Hikers, gone. Eaten by bear, assumed.

I sat there with my free oatmeal going cold and did the addict's arithmetic, which is the only arithmetic where getting robbed and beaten and jailed comes out a *win.* Jesus, I thought. I'd been walking around inside the jaws of the thing for two weeks calling it bad luck. I wasn't unlucky at all. I was *lucky.* Lucky to be alive. I believed it, too — really believed it — and it would be a while yet before I understood that *lucky to be alive* is just the house's way of telling you it hasn't decided to collect yet. The bears weren't bad luck. The bears were the table. And the table is patient.

Then Laura, having scared the color out of me, did me a kindness so strange it deserves its own headstone.

She had a laptop, she said. Belonged to a tourist — a guest who'd checked out for a hike about a month back and simply never come back for his things. No password on it, she'd checked. It had just been sitting in the back room since, and she'd been meaning to sell it, and if I was any good at that sort of thing maybe I could move it for her once I got my own situation sorted. In the meantime, she said, if I needed a computer, it was right there. Use it.

So let me lay out what I was looking at. I was looking at a dead man's laptop. A man who, by the only available theory in this town, was at that very moment digesting somewhere in the Chugach, and whose machine I was now being invited to open up and rebuild my entire life on. *This,* I thought, taking it from her with both hands like it was warm, *is not going to be weird at all.*

Reader, I built an empire on that ghost's laptop.

I got the new phone at Walmart and had to claw my way back into my own email all over again — the same login dance, the same verification trap I'd barely cleared the night before on a handset that now belonged to a stranger. But I cleared it, second verse, and the documents from Manny were right there waiting where I'd left them. Then the phone fought me for a week anyway, because the world wasn't done. My number was a Metro PCS number and for reasons I never did fully untangle they would not port it over to the new handset, and I burned more than an hour in their queue before I gave up and started running a free TextNow number instead. Which sounds like a small thing and was very much not, because that dead Metro number was the one my bank had on file, and the bank — to mail me a replacement debit card so I could touch my own money again — needed to text a verification code to that exact number to prove I was me. The number I no longer controlled. To verify the identity of the man holding the phone. There's that wall again, the same one I keep running into my whole life: the paperwork that proves you exist, lost, and you standing right there in the flesh unable to convince anybody of the obvious.

It took a week. I called every day, three, four times a day, and got the same wall from twenty different people in twenty different voices, all of them very sorry, none of them able. And then — this is the part I hold onto — one woman pulled up my account, saw the whole sad logbook of my calling, daily, four times a day, twenty reps, the same dead end every time, and something in her just gave. *You poor man,* basically. She broke the rule. Did some override, switched my number in their system to the new TextNow line, fired the verification code to a phone I actually held, and just like that there was a debit card in the mail, two-day UPS, tracking number and all. One human being deciding, against policy, to let me back into my own life. It always comes down to one of those. The whole week, the whole *year,* was just me getting handed from one of those to the next, with long cold stretches of wall in between.

And here's the joke the week kept telling at my expense. The one document that had refused me every single door — a real, official ID, the thing that paper slip from the Alaska DMV could only pretend to be — turned out to be the easiest thing I touched the whole time. My license lived in Arizona. So I called Arizona. And Arizona just *mailed me a new one.* No code. No verification line. No manager fetched from the back. A week later it was in my hand, my actual face on actual plastic, the precise item that Harris Teeter clerk would have counted five hundred dollars into my palm for without a blink — and it had arrived in the mail like it had never once been a problem in the history of the world. A week of trench warfare for a bank card. A two-minute phone call for the only piece of paper that had genuinely cost me everything. That is exactly the kind of water a man drowns in down there. It'll let you go under in a teaspoon and then hand you the whole ocean for free, just to watch your face when you understand it could have all along.

And in between the walls, on a dead man's laptop, I got back on the phones.

I want to be honest about the shape of it, because this is the part where the chapter turns and the money comes in and it would be easy to let it sound like a comeback. It felt like one. God, it felt like one. Manny just about fell out of his chair when I surfaced. *I thought you fell off a mountain, man* — laughing, genuinely glad, the relief of a guy who'd written off an investment and watched it walk back in the door. I told him the whole thing, Mountain View to the rose bush to the jail to the bears, and he kept saying *wow, dude, just wow,* and then he asked if I needed money. I had no clean way to even receive it — that was the whole problem, the card still days out — but I told him he could send it to my sister and she could Western Union it to me. And he did. Five hundred for me. And then five hundred more straight to Niki, to pay back the five hundred she'd wired me at the Harris Teeter, money I'd never breathed a word to him about.

Sit with that math a second, because I did. I had worked for these men exactly one week. Then I'd vanished off the face of the earth for another week — no call, no show, no nothing, the kind of disappearance that gets you fired from a paper route. And the response to that, from the top, was a thousand dollars wired to me before I'd closed a single new deal. That's not charity. I want to be clear it wasn't charity, because understanding that it wasn't is the whole sad engine of my life. It was a *good bet.* They'd heard me on the phones for one week and they knew what I was worth on them, and a great closer is the rare commodity that's always, every single time, worth more than he costs you. They knew I'd earn it back by Thursday. And I did. I was killing it again inside of days — two hundred, four hundred, twelve hundred dollars in a single day at the peak of it, my best day ever to that point — the WhatsApp threads lighting up fast and hot, deals stacking, the whole machine I thought I'd left behind in Vegas, two thousand miles south, humming away again right there on a hostel desk, on hardware that used to belong to a man the bears ate. The con outlived the mark. It always does. The machine had followed me north, and now it didn't even need me to own the computer.

I was king of the castle again. That's the phrase that was in my head and I'll keep it because it's the true one and it's the damning one. Glenda had me saved in her phone as *Alaskan closer* — that was my name in the WhatsApp, the handle, the legend — and the two of us still hadn't so much as had a single personal word, just deals flying back and forth down the wire, hers and mine, opener and closer, three weeks in and not yet one human sentence between us. I didn't know it then for what it was. I just knew the threads pinged and the money came and I felt, for the first time since Vegas, like myself.

Which is to say I felt great, and felt great is the most expensive thing I know how to feel.

I quit trying to get my old stuff back. Twice in that stretch Paralee drove me out to the house where I was supposed to meet the man who'd agreed to hand over my belongings without the cops involved, and twice the man simply didn't show — and by then I was making so much money, so fast, that I just looked at the whole errand one day and said *fuck it.* I walked into Best Buy and bought a brand-new laptop, top of the line, nine hundred dollars, didn't blink. Started buying new clothes a piece at a time, a shirt here, pants there, day after day, until I'd built a whole wardrobe out of nothing. I never contacted that man again. Let him keep the old bones of my life. I was redressing the set, and I mistook the new costume for a new man, the way I always did — fresh clothes, fresh laptop, money in the pocket, smoke and mirrors I was running on *myself* now, the easiest mark in the building.

There were good days in there, real ones, and I'll give them their due. I'd stopped hiking — Laura's bears had cured me of that — but I started hitting the local bars, and the hostel turned out to be a river of people, travelers coming and going, a new face every couple of nights, and I was good in a room and the money was good and for a few weeks I had something that could pass for a life. Laura took a real liking to me. She put me in charge of the cookout on the Fourth of July, of all things — handed me the grill and the spatula and a mountain of patties, and I stood out in that white midnight daylight flipping burgers for a yard full of strangers and felt, briefly, like a person who belonged somewhere. I cooked for everybody. It was a good day. I want it in here among the rest.

But that first polite yes was still out there, compounding, the way they always do.

Paralee started coming by. Daily. Pulling up to the hostel to ask did I need anything, did I want for anything, and then staying to unload about Carl — the marriage, the money, the slow grind of it — until the visits stopped being about whether I needed anything at all. And Carl, drunk and broke and watching his wife drive across town every day to check on the young guy with the good job, did the math any drunk man does. He decided I was sleeping with her. I wasn't. I want that flat on the page: I never touched the woman, never wanted to, spent three weeks actively *fleeing* the woman. Didn't matter. The texts started coming in, Carl drunk on the other end, threats to my life at first, ugly ones, and then — and this is the part that told me exactly how bad off they really were over there — the threats turned into a shakedown. Send me money or I'll hurt you. Pay up. Over and over, the funny warm man from the salmon dinner reduced to extorting a stranger by phone for grocery money. I never sent him a dime and I never answered, and nothing ever came of it, the way nothing ever comes of most of the threats men make from the bottom of a bottle. But it sat there. And Paralee only got bolder, fresher, more open about what she was driving across town for, and I spent the back half of those three weeks doing nothing so much as trying to get out from under the kindness of the one person who'd saved my life — which, if you want the whole curriculum of how I've lived, is right there in a sentence.

I couldn't shake her. So I did what I have always done when a place gets complicated. I left.

I paid for one extra week at the pod and I booked a flight out, and I want you to guess where the flight went, because the universe has a sense of humor and its whole act is callbacks.

Manny and the other owner had two tickets they couldn't use. Front row. Dave Chappelle and Joe Rogan, same bill, at the MGM. They put both in my name and told me to find somebody to take. Front-row seats to the biggest comedy night in the country, dropped in my lap like a tip.

In Las Vegas.

*Fuck my life.* I was going back to Vegas. The exact city I'd fled in a blackout three chapters of my life ago, the place I'd flown two thousand miles and frozen and starved and bled and gone to jail to *get away from,* and here it came back around to meet me, smiling, holding two front-row tickets like a maître d'. That's the geographic cure for you, the whole con of it in one plane ticket: you can run as far as Alaska, you can get eaten down to nothing and build yourself back up on a dead man's laptop, and the wheel still comes around to the same green felt it started on. The cure's punchline is always the town you ran from.

Even the last loose end played like a closing bit. My debit card finally showed — except the UPS driver pulled up to the hostel, didn't see a soul at that part-time front desk, didn't try the bell, didn't poke around, just shrugged and hauled my card back to the hub eight miles off. So on a Saturday afternoon, three weeks and a lifetime out of a Mountain View ditch, I made the walk to go collect the one piece of plastic that proved I had money — and I'd already met a guy at the hostel who'd let me Zelle him cash and hand me the green, so even broke-on-paper I wasn't broke, and even stranded I wasn't stuck.  I could have taken a cab, but the day was too beautiful and what fun would that have been. All in all it wasn't half bad. All in all it worked out.

That's what I'd have told you then, standing at that UPS counter with my card in my hand and a plane ticket in my pocket and a thousand of another man's dollars in my account. *It all worked out.*

It's the single most expensive sentence I have ever told myself, and I've told it at the top of every flight of stairs I ever fell down. I was going back to Vegas, and I was sure — dead sure, smoothed-out and tan and king-of-the-castle sure — that this time I'd walk in and walk out clean. Except by now I'd missed my court date, and that warrant, the Vegas one I'd skipped town on, had gone officially active. One slip in that town and I'd be looking at more than a year in a cell. I booked the flight anyway. Devil hates a coward...
