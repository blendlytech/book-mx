# Chapter 49 — Zach
I've had a lot of time to reflect since sitting in that Mexican rehab. It's funny - I guess I saved the best chapter for last. I'm not saying this to be dramatic, I'm saying this because it's true. It's also funny, and it's also going to make me cry. It's about my nephew, Zach, and it's about the best thing that ever happened to me.  It took me a while to get here, but I'm finally here.  Family is hard for me to talk about.  It's hard for me to talk about anything, really.  But Zach is different.

Everybody in that family said the same thing about Zach, my whole life and his.

*He's little Clay.*

They said it about his face, which was fair — the kid came out looking like me and never stopped, to the point where people who hadn't seen either of us in years would get us confused in a photograph.

And then, somewhere in there, they started saying it the other way.

*You're going to end up like your uncle Clay.*

His mother said that to him.  My mother, my father. My own sister Niki, who has bailed me out of more holes than any human being alive, said it to her son in her own kitchen, and she wasn't being cruel and she wasn't wrong to be afraid. She'd watched me my whole life. She had the data.

So the boy grew up with my face and my habits and my name used as a warning in the house he lived in.

He is not me. I need that on the page before anything else in this chapter. He is not me and he never was, and everybody who said it was looking at the wrong things.

He's smarter than I am. Not a little. He is honest in a way I have never once in forty-six years managed for a full day.

---

Let me just tell you what kind of honest, because it's the funniest thing in this book and it's also the whole point of the chapter.

Zach told his mother about his first sexual experience and many there after.  He told her about a lot of other things too.  Too many to report in this book.  All of them leaving me completely floored.

Told her. Voluntarily. Came in and reported it, the way you'd report a grade.

Now, I want to walk you through what my own brain does with that, because the two reactions are the whole difference between him and me.

The first thing I feel is delight. It is, flat out, one of the funniest things I have ever heard. Looney tunes. The kid was looney tunes.

And then the second thing lands about a half second behind it, and it isn't funny at all, and it took me about twenty years to be able to see it.

He wasn't being reckless. He wasn't showing off. There was no calculation in it of any kind, which is exactly what made it unthinkable to me — because at that age, with that same mother, I would have run four separate plays before the door shut. What does she know. What can she prove. What's the smallest true thing I can hand her that makes the rest of it go away. That's not something I'd have had to think about. That's the machine. It runs before I get there.

Zach didn't have the machine. He had a person in front of him and no wall between them at all, and so the truth came out.

That is not a defect. I spent a long time filing it as a defect — *the kid can't get out of his own way* — and I said it fondly and I said it a lot, and what I actually meant, if I'd been man enough to translate it, was *the kid has no exits and I have never once in my life been in a room without checking for one.*

---

Here's the other one, and it's the thing he taught me.

If there was trouble, Zach walked straight at it.

Something would go wrong — a job, a girl, money, a thing he'd done — and he'd go handle it. Same day. He'd drive over there. He'd make the call. He'd walk into the room where the bad conversation was waiting and have the bad conversation.

I go to my room and think about it and hoped it would never happen.

I mean that literally, and I mean it as the description of my entire adult life. I have never in my life walked at a hard thing. I go sideways. I go around. I sit with it and I build a version of it that's easier to carry, and then I get in a vehicle and take that version somewhere else, and I have crossed international borders to avoid conversations that Zach would have finished before lunch.

He's twenty-some years younger than me and he had that from the start and I have never gotten it at all. I'd say I admire it, but admire is too soft a word and too safe. It's the only thing anybody in my family ever had that I actually wanted, and I never once told him so, and here it is.

He never used it on me. Not once. Not in a lifetime, not in a single conversation, not sideways at a table with everybody listening.

He went right on defending me.

That's the thing about Zach that I cannot get to the bottom of. The family would get going about me at a table.  I would only know, because he would tell me later.  That's how deeply offended he was. — and understand, they had every right, and mostly they weren't even being unkind, they were just tired — and Zach would go to bat. Every time. *He's doing really well now. He's got a great job. He's out near his friends. He's got a little family there.*

He'd say it, into a room full of people who had all long since done the arithmetic.  It would fall on deaf ears every single time, and he'd do it again.

He had a version of me in his head that he'd built when he was a boy and he wanted it to be real. I know that. And what I've had to sit with is that there came a point — I don't know the year, I don't know the day, and I'm not going to insult him by guessing — when he knew it wasn't. When he had all the same information everybody else at that table had.

And he kept going to bat anyway.

That's not belief. Belief is easy. That's something else and I don't have a name for it. He decided, knowing better, to keep standing where he'd always stood.

---

I'd been calling him my rock for years before I understood why he was able to be one.

It's this, and I've only just worked it out.

Zach is the one person on this earth I have never had to lie to.

I want to be precise, because I've spent this whole book being precise about my own dishonesty, so let me be precise about the exception.

I'm sure I've lied about something I wasn't ready to admit.  But I never tried hard with him because I didn't have to feel judged. I'd tell that kid the whole ugly truth of wherever I was — the drinking, the money, the jail, the woman, the wreck of it — and I'd tell it flat, without the shape on it, because there was no percentage in front of me. There was nothing to get from him. He wasn't a mark and he wasn't a lender and he wasn't a room to be worked. He was just a kid who loved me, so the voice had nothing to sell and it stayed off.  I knew there wasn't a judgement behind it.  Just a conversation with someone who loved me and wanted to hear about my life.  He's the only one I've ever had I could do that with.  I never felt like I had to be extra or less.  I could be me.  I don't know if he ever really realized that.

About ten years of that. One clean account.

He's got a kid now.

Recently. And I wasn't there, and that's a sentence I'm going to be carrying a while.

He met her — Peyton — and I actually got to watch that happen, which is more than I got with most things in that family, and she is a genuinely wonderful woman who has put up with a truly staggering amount of Zach's nonsense and stayed. Because he's got plenty. He's honest as the day is long and he still cannot get out of his own way, and those two facts are not in conflict, they're the same fact, which makes him..and why, in the end, she loves him and I love him.

I don't know her well enough to write her down, and I want to be straight about why, because it isn't a scheduling problem. I wasn't there. That's the whole reason. I have detailed sensory recall of a neighborhood in Anchorage where I got mugged and I could not tell you three true things about the woman my nephew loves, and the reason I'm telling you this is that for the entire stretch of years in which a person gets to know somebody, I was in a motel, or Vegas, or a barn in Michigan, or a bus with black paint on the windows. 

What I can tell you is that she lives in the heart of the one man on this planet who has shown me more love than anybody, and that puts her somewhere in me permanently whether I ever earn the acquaintance or not.  She is a very pleasant woman as far as I can see and I'm happy for that, because she's got Zach's heart and that's more than enough for me.  Welcome to the family kid.

There's a baby in South Carolina somewhere, and I've never held him.

---

I'd give my right arm for him. I would genuinely trade the arm, today, and not think hard about it.

But I can't. Not from here, and not the way I am, and here's the part that took me a fence and a plastic chair and a man twice a week to understand:

I can't hold that kid until I make me whole, because the only thing I have ever actually given Zach is an example, and he has taken every single one I ever set. He took the face. He took the mouth. He took the drinking the week he was legally allowed to, in his mother's house, because I was there and being uncle Clay was what he wanted and that was the cost of the ticket.

So the only thing I can build for that kid is a version of me worth copying.

That's it. That's the whole secret and it's the least original thought a man has ever had and I got to it at forty-six, in a rehab, behind a fence, about twenty-five years after it would have done Zach the most good. I'm nowwhere near it yet, but that is the plan. I know Zach will hold me to it, but not judge me if I don't.  He will still accept me in his life and the only one disappointed will be me.  That's what love looks like.  I've never been that kind of man.  I'm not there yet.  But I'm working on it.  I will meet my grand-nephew in a few years.

He was my Clayita before there was a Clayita.

I've thought about that a lot out here. A half-wild thing that decided I was hers and didn't take a vote on it, that followed me everywhere for no reason I ever understood, that ate whatever I had and stayed anyway.

I got that once from a cat on a mountain when I had nothing left, and it kept me alive, and I wrote a whole chapter about it.

I'd been getting it from a boy in Charleston for twenty-five years and I never once wrote it down while he was doing the same thing in every way that mattered. Keeping me alive. That's how I'll write about him. That's the truest thing.
