# Chapter 49 — About the Chair

I told you at the start I was telling you this from a chair, a free man.

It's a plastic one, in a courtyard in Mexicali, and there's a fence around the courtyard. My discharge date was supposed to be October, but I'm out. It is the first of August as I write this. I turn forty-seven in eight days, and I'll be turning it in an office building I rented, wearing a t-shirt that isn't mine, in a country where I still have no legal right to be standing.

So. Free man. You can decide what that's worth. I've decided what it's worth to me, and the answer is: more than anything else I have ever owned, and I'm not going to apologize for using the word.

I said I could walk out that gate and nobody would stop me. And they didn't. That's the entire difference between this fence and the last one, and it turns out that's the only difference that matters. Nobody on this earth was forcing me to sit in that chair. I signed a piece of paper saying I'd sit there for six months and I sat in it, and some days that is the single accomplishment I can put my finger on.

***

And I can still reach down and touch it.

I did just now, before I wrote that sentence. Right hand, down past the ankle bone, and there they are — faint, gone shiny and white the way old scar tissue goes. You'd have to know what to look for; somebody at a beach would never notice them.

They never really faded. I told you that on the very first page, and it's still true nearly fifty chapters later. It’s the only physical piece of that mountain I can hold in my hand.

Here's the part I didn't know when I started writing:

I go looking for them. Not just once, but regularly. I'll be sitting there in that chair and my hand will drift down on its own to find them, the way your tongue finds a chipped tooth. I run my thumb across those four marks just to verify they're still there.

I've thought a long time about why a man would do that, and I have an answer I don't particularly like: it's because they're proof. Everything else that happened to me on that mountain exists in exactly one place — the inside of my own skull, which is the least reliable instrument I have ever been issued. I have no photographs. No police report. No newspaper clippings. Not one document anywhere on earth says I was ever up there. My best friend of thirty years believes I invented the entire thing to shake three hundred dollars out of him, and I've already explained why that was a completely rational conclusion.

So I reach down and count the marks. Because it happened, and here is the receipt, and I need to check it about twice a day.

***

Let me give you the honest inventory. No sales pitch. I've been practicing.

I have no passport. The application came back rejected over a date I wrote down wrong; I never resubmitted it, and I'd have to start from scratch. I have no Social Security card. I have two active warrants — a DUI in Ohio and a protection-order violation out of Vegas — and both are just as live as the day I boarded that plane. Every plan I have for walking back into my own country runs directly through a cell.

I don't have a dollar to my name. I don't own the laptop I'm typing on. I don't have a phone number that belongs to me.

And after two and a half years in this country — after crossing a border, living on a beach, traveling a thousand miles to Sinaloa, surviving a cartel mountain, and walking four days through a brutal desert — I still do not speak Spanish.

It is the single most damning fact in this entire book, and it isn't close. I have been functionally mute in this country by choice since the day I arrived. I sat at a table of thirty people and let an eleven-year-old girl translate the room for me. I sat in a car while two women negotiated my freedom eighteen inches from my face and couldn't contribute a single syllable to the only negotiation of my life that actually mattered. I spent five months owned by men whose language I couldn't be bothered to study, and every beating I took up there was delivered in words I had two years and every reason on earth to learn, and didn't.

The counselor was real. Omar kept his word about that, just like he kept his word about everything else — which is a level of decency I still haven't gotten used to.

He speaks English and he saw me twice a week. I want to tell you what happened during our very first session, because it's the only moment in that facility I’d call a turning point, and it lasted about four seconds.

He asked me an ordinary question from the front of the intake folder.

I answered it. And as the words came out, I heard myself talking.

That voice. The greased, charming one. The voice the medicine dragged out into the open in a concrete room in Sinaloa and forced me to listen to for what felt like an eternity. The voice forged in a driveway fighting my sister at seven years old and monetized at thirty on a beige carpet under fluorescent lights in Las Vegas. It rolled out of my mouth in a folding chair in front of a man whose entire job was to help me — smooth, warm, working the room, and I could hear every gear turning.

I was pitching him. Pitching him on *me*. On how well I was doing.

I stopped dead in the middle of a sentence. He waited. He’s exceptional at waiting — that’s ninety percent of what good counselors do, and I'd only ever met one other person who knew how to hold silence like that, and she was sitting on a tile floor in Sinaloa with a plate of cold rice.

I looked at him and said, "I'm doing the thing. Right now. I'm running game on you."

He nodded calmly. "I know. Keep going, and tell me when it starts up again."

We've been doing that for months. That is the entire treatment as far as I can tell. You say something real, the salesman takes over to polish it up, somebody who sees through the hustle says *there it is*, and you back up and try again without the performance.

I'm not great at it. I’d say I land about one honest sentence out of four when you’re asking me about something I’m not comfortable talking about. But a year ago I was zero for four and didn't even know there was a count.

***

Glenda is on the women's side. Twenty yards and a concrete wall.

I see her, but not the way you'd picture. There’s a strict schedule, rigid rules, and always a staffer in the room — which, I’ve come to believe, is the single greatest mercy that could have happened to us.

Because here is what we are, after months of counseling and a plastic chair to think it through: we are two people who, combined, cannot scrape together one functional adult, but who are, apart, each doing all right. She’s doing the work. I’m doing the work. Every single hour we have ever spent unsupervised in the same room over the last two and a half years ended with a drink in our hands. Every one. Including the good ones. Especially the good ones.

***

The cat is in Rosarito.

Glenda managed to get word to the couple who hauled feed — the same two who hid her under a tarp in the bed of their truck and drove her off that hill. She asked them, the next time they ran a delivery up the mountain, to look for a little grey kitten that wouldn't let anyone else near her, and scoop her up.

They did it. They actually went back and did it.

I don't have the money to go get her. I reached out to my father for help in an awkward, roundabout way — which is another story altogether.

We'll see. I'll give it a day. I owe that cat everything, but if it doesn't work out, I'm done. That's a sick man typing. A man who has reached his limit and stopped caring. But I need this. I really do. Just to start caring about something again. A cat. That’s how a sick mind works.

She’s the one piece I'd trade the rest for. Not the clean passport, not the dismissed warrants, not clearing my name with Josh. Just the cat. I’d take the rest of the wreckage exactly as it sits if someone walked through that front gate holding her.

***

So here is where we stand, you and me.

I promised you on the opening page that you'd want to know how a man ends up where I ended up, and I told you the answer was one bad left turn at a time. I've spent this entire book walking you through those turns, but now that I've laid them out end-to-end, I need to correct the record.

It wasn't the turns. Anybody can make a wrong turn. Josh made a few. Troy made a few. My father made plenty. The only thing unique about my disaster is that every single time I hit a wall, I got in a vehicle.

Five years old, my cousin died, and they put me in a car and drove me to my grandmother's. That was done out of love, but it was the only coping mechanism anyone ever handed me: when it hurts, run. 

Vegas to Alaska. Alaska to Vegas. Vegas to Michigan. Michigan to Ohio. Ohio to Mexico. Mexicali to Peñasco to Sinaloa to Peñasco to Mexicali to San Diego to Rosarito. Walking twenty-three miles on foot into Tijuana in the dark because I'd rather march through the most dangerous city on the border than hold my hand out to another human being. A death wish disguised as independence. Four days crawling through the desert. Every catastrophe of my adult life followed that identical script: take the hit, slam the ledger shut, get behind the wheel, and go feed the beast somewhere else.

That’s why the clinic worked. Not the ayahuasca. Not the mountain. Not the beatings, the attack dog, or the desert. It worked because it was a fence I had the physical power to walk away from, and I chose not to walk. One day at a time. Until I finally left. I’m still sick. I’m still not completely right. But they let me walk out.

I’ve made bold declarations in this book at least four separate times, and every single time, the floor gave way beneath me within a season. On a beach in Peñasco holding a cold Tecate. In a bus with blacked-out windows. Standing in the ruins of an app with forty-three brewery owners to placate.

I know what the medicine showed me in Sinaloa, and I've written it down so I can never hide from it: the grief was never the foundation the addiction was built on. I dragged every buried ghost into the sunlight in that concrete room — the terrified boy, the abandoned daughter, the grandmother at the card table who couldn't stand a cheater. I wept over every single one of them. And then I got in a car, drove north, and the craving was sitting right there in the passenger seat, exactly the same size it had always been.

So I don't get a tidy Hollywood epiphany. There isn't one coming. The thirst stands entirely on its own. It was sitting on the edge of the bed the morning I walked out that gate, asking the exact same question it always asks, and it will ask it again tomorrow morning.

What I had instead was a fence, a counselor twice a week who can hear the pitch start up in my throat, a woman across the courtyard doing her own healing, and four white puncture scars on the back of my ankle that I touch twice a day just to verify the nightmare was real.

***

My daughter is fifteen now. She carries another good man's last name. She has no idea I exist, and if we passed each other on the sidewalk tomorrow, she wouldn't glance twice in my direction.

That is the correct outcome. I've stopped fighting it. I gave her up because walking away is the only skill I ever truly mastered. I spent this entire book laying out the raw truth of who I was so that if she ever comes looking — twenty years from now, forty years from now — there is a record. A document. Her father wasn't an enigma or a softened myth her mother had to invent. He was this. Exactly this. Every ugly, selfish mile of it, without an ounce of paint on the canvas.

That is what this manuscript is. That is what it was always for. Everything else was just how I managed to survive long enough to write it down.

***

I owe an accounting for the missing chapters, and I'd rather do it once here than leave loose threads scattered across the pages.

I only remembered the ruptures. The only things that print in high definition on my memory are the things that shattered. I can recall the exact weight of a fistful of my sister's hair, the screech of locking brakes the year I was eleven, and the suffocating smell of a bus with blacked-out windows. But the people who were steadily, quietly, unspectacularly good to me for thirty or forty years without fail — they never broke anything, so there’s no dramatic film rolling in my head.

That means a memoir like this is fundamentally rigged. It awards the most ink to whoever inflicted pain, or whoever I destroyed, and the absolute least to the people who simply held the line. The souls I owe the greatest debt to are the ones who gave me the least drama to write about.

I don't have an excuse for that. It’s the very flaw that landed me here: I never paid attention to the things that were working. I only came running when the engine blew up.

***

The sun drops low and orange over the desert at the end of the day, looking just like it did on that hill outside a small Sinaloa town, where a little girl who wasn't mine used to walk a tall, foolish gringo past the last houses so his phone could find a signal, kicking rocks and teasing him about which uncle was full of shit.

America is somewhere in Mexicali tonight with her grandmother, as far as I know, which is the single untainted piece of good news to come out of this entire saga, and I hold onto it with both hands.

I never did get a hug from that kid.

Two years together, and it was always the flat palm and the high five, right up to the end. I’d have traded my right arm for one hug.

*She’d have gotten around to it.* That’s the lie I tell myself out here as the light fades, and I have no way on earth to verify it, but I’m going to keep telling myself anyway.

***

I didn't know what I was stepping into when I walked out. No passport, no papers, no nation that will welcome me through the front gate. A woman I love deeply and cannot safely share a room with. A lifelong friend who thinks I threatened his family. 

Every old instinct I have is already sketching a map. I can feel the gears grinding right now, sitting here — the ancient survival engine firing up, calculating the angle, the hustle, the pretty illusion with nothing solid beneath it. Forty-seven years old next week, and that machine has never shut off. I don't think it ever will.

The only difference is that now, I recognize the sound. That’s the only progress I've made, but it's everything I've got. I hear the motor start, I see which way the needle is swinging, and even though every broken part of me wants to follow it — because sick men always crave what poisons them — I know what it is.

A broken compass.

It’s not like a stopped clock.

It isn't right twice a day.

As I walk towards my Airbnb in Mexicali, 2 month's sober, not the 6 month's I promised myself, the only thing running through my mind was Tecate.  Mexcio's finest local beer.  I drop into Six and purchase a pack of cigerettes, a lighter and a tall-boy.  It's 120 degrees outside, as it always is in Mexicali during the summer.  The heat radiates off the concrete in shimmering waves.  There's a breeze that moves the trash down the street, but it doesn't do a damn thing to cool the air.  The Tecate is already sweating in my hands on tht hot side walk. 4 pulls later, it's in a recycle bin.  1.5 miles to the Airbnb, cake walk.  Two large bags full of clothes that belonged to another rehaber months back.  Half fit and I'm currsing myself for bringing any of them about halfway through the walk, drenched in sweat.  I'll get there.  I always get there.  What happens after that is anyone's guess.  The map is blank, the future is uncertain.  Where this broken compass takes me next, is anyone's guess.  I'll come up with something.

And that's it... that's the end of the book.
