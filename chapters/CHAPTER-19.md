---

There is one thing about Sinaloa I turned over in my hands the whole two months and never did get set down flat, so I'll hand it to you the way it came to me.

That state grows and moves and sells a fair share of all the misery that ever ended up in an American vein or up an American nose. It is, in the plainest words I've got, the company town for the entire industry of getting high. And it holds the lowest opinion of a person who *actually gets high* that I have come across anywhere on this earth.

Down there, a man who does *drugs* is *basura.* Trash. A loser — and not the soft way we sling that word around up here, but a real one, a stain stamped across the whole family that raised him. And it isn't only the hard stuff that earns it. A weed smoker, a fella who likes a quiet joint at the end of his day, the most harmless stoner the Lord ever breathed life into — in that town he's a bum, a nobody, a thing his people are ashamed to put their name next to. They will sell the rest of the world its poison by the truckload and look straight down their nose at the one cousin soft enough to swallow any of it. I never squared it. I doubt I ever will.

But drink — drink is something else entirely. Drink they love. The whole place gets *gloriously* hammered, top to bottom, grandmother to grandson; there is a bottle on every table and a cold case in every truck bed and not one ounce of shame anywhere near any of it. In Sinaloa the drunk is just a man enjoying himself. It's the *druggie* they spit on. So the one substance on God's earth that had its hooks all the way through me was not just permitted down there — it was practically a civic duty.

Which is the whole reason what we did next was a lie, and a small ugly one.

Because we were drinking again. Of course we were. We'd cracked that seal back in Peñasco months before and we were never truly going to get it shut again, and a place where the booze flowed like that was the last place on earth two people like us were going to hold a line. But I had made Rose a promise — looked that woman in the eye and swore I would not let her daughter drink — and so we did the coward's version of keeping it. We hid it. Rose never knew we'd started back up; she went the whole two months believing her girl was bone dry. And America never saw a drop touch our lips, not once. If I wanted a beer, I poured it into a Yeti — the same trick, the same cup, I'd run on my own newly sober father a year before, the tumbler that turns a drink into a coffee, a water, a nothing — and I drank it like that, in front of the child, hidden in plain sight in my own hand. I had crossed a border and a desert and a mountain of grief and I was right back to drinking out of a Yeti so the people who loved me wouldn't see. Some things you carry no matter how far you run. The Yeti was one of mine.

That was the truth of those two months, under the family and the cousins and the long uncle's table — good, and real, and shot all the way through with a lie I poured into a steel cup. And it was into the middle of all that that they put the medicine in front of us.

---

What they put in front of us was older than the lie in my cup and older than the booze underneath it — older, maybe, than Sinaloa itself.

Ayahuasca. *La medicina.* The family knew of a man who held the ceremonies, and the whole idea of it — the pitch, if a sacrament can have a pitch — was that this was the thing that finally cleaned a person out for good. That it reached down past whatever you'd been numbing and pulled the thing itself up by the root, and that a man who sat with it honestly could walk away from the bottle, the pipe, the whole load he'd been carrying, and never want it again. Two weeks of it. A course of nights. And Glenda and I, who had just spent half a year proving to each other that we could not be trusted with a Tecate, signed on for the express and only purpose of finally killing the thirst.

That is not remotely what either of us got.

There was a diet first, a *dieta* — no salt, no pork, no liquor, no sex, your body scraped plain so there's nothing in you but you. And then the nights. You come in around dark and you lie down on a mat in a circle with the others and a bucket of your own set close by your head, and the man sings you in, and he pours out a measure of the foulest thing I have ever willingly put in my mouth — a thick bitter brown that tastes like the bottom of the oldest tree in the world — and you drink it, and you lie back in the dark, and you wait for it to come up in you.

And then it comes up in you.

---

I did not go in there for my daughter. I want that on the record. I went in there for the drink, like we'd planned, my intention all squared away and pointed at the bottle like a good soldier.

The medicine didn't care what I'd planned. The medicine went straight past the drink like it wasn't even worth its time, down to the oldest thing in me, the thing I had spent fifteen years and four thousand miles and an ocean of vodka never once looking at sober — and it took my face in both its hands and it turned it toward my little girl.

I had a daughter. I have told you that, in passing, the way I've trained myself to say it — *had a two-year-old once, lost her to adoption, a lifetime ago in Alaska* — fast and flat, the way you'd read a line off a form, so the words can get out of my mouth before the thing behind them catches up. That night the thing caught up. The medicine sat me down in front of ten years of it at once. Two years old when she went. Two. Old enough to have a face, a real one, the one I'd spent a decade not letting myself see, and there it was. And the math a man does at the bottom of that brown bitter dark — that she was twelve now, a near-teenage girl somewhere out in the world, with some other good man's last name, who would not have known me on a street if I'd walked straight into her.

And I purged. They call it *la purga* and they are not being delicate — it comes up out of you, the body wringing itself out into that bucket, and the tears come up the very same way, from the very same place, ten years of a grief I had drowned every single time it ever tried to surface, all of it heaving up out of me at once in the dark while a stranger sang over my body in a language I didn't know. I wept the way I have not wept since I was a boy. For her. For not fighting. For the lie I'd told myself the whole time — that letting her go had been the kind thing, the strong thing, when it was only the same thing I have always done, which is leave before the leaving can be done to me. A geographic cure of the soul. I'd left my own child standing in the road the way I left every place I ever got scared of.

And the cruelest cut of the whole night — the medicine made sure I didn't miss it — was that I had come all the way down to this country and fallen straight into trying to be a father to a girl who wasn't mine. America, with her high fives and her held-back trust. I was over here aching to father somebody else's daughter while my own walked around the earth a stranger to me. The medicine laid those two girls side by side in the dark and made me look at both of them until I couldn't breathe.

---

Across the room, in her own dark, Glenda was having her own.

I could hear her — you can always hear the others, the room is a chorus of everybody's worst thing coming up out of them at once — and hers had nothing to do with a bottle either. She told me the shape of it after, in the gray hours when we were both wrung out and emptied and gentle with each other the way you only get after something like that.

Hers was America's father. The man who'd given that girl half her blood and then given her mother the back of his hand, over and over, in the years before Glenda got out from under him — the things he'd done to her, the fear she'd lived inside, all of it she'd packed down under three years of drinking on a screen with me and never once spoken plain. And under that, the deeper one: that her girl was growing up with no father at all, a hole where a man should be, because the only one on offer had been a man with fists. That's what came up out of Glenda into her bucket in the dark. Not the drink. The fists, and the fatherless girl, and all the years she'd swallowed both.

We had each gone in there to be cured of being drunks. And the medicine had reached into each of us and pulled up something else entirely — the dead daughter out of me, the live wound out of her — and held it up to the light and said, *this. This is what's actually down here. Not the thing you came to show me.*

---

Here is what nobody warns you about the medicine, and what I have come to believe is the truest thing it taught me, though it taught it to me sideways and at my own expense.

It worked. On everything except the one thing we'd asked it for.

We came out of those two weeks scoured down to nothing, hollowed and grieved-empty and lighter in some deep place than either of us had been in years — and not one single degree less thirsty for a drink. It had cracked the floor of both of us clean open and shown us the oldest broken things lying down there in the dark, and it had not laid so much as a finger on the bottle. We'd been to the very bottom of ourselves, the place all the experts and all the sad-eyed counselors swear the drinking comes *from* — and we'd found the bottom didn't have the drinking in it at all.

Because that's the lie, the comfortable one, the one everybody wants to be true: that a man drinks *because of* something, and if you could only reach the something and heal it, the drinking would lift off him like a fog. I believed it a little, going in. I do not believe it now. You can drag the dead daughter and the fists and the fatherless child all up out of the dark and lay them to rest one by one, and the wanting will still be sitting there in the morning, clean and patient and entirely its own animal, asking the same thing it always asks. The grief was real. The grief was down there. The grief was just never what the drink was standing on.

So we grieved. And we healed something neither of us had been able to touch in ten years. And then January ran out, and the holidays were over, and it was time to start the long way back north — back toward Peñasco, back across that toll road I prayed we'd find this time, back toward a line where a man was allowed to drink again.

And the second we crossed it, we would.
