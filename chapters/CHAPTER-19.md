There is one thing about Sinaloa I turned over in my hands the whole two months and never did get set down flat, so I'll hand it to you the way it came to me.

That state grows and moves and sells a fair share of all the misery that ever ended up in an American vein or up an American nose. It is, in the plainest words I've got, the company town for the entire industry of getting high. And it holds the lowest opinion of a person who *actually gets high* that I have come across anywhere on this earth.

Down there, a man who does *drugs* is *basura.* Trash. A loser — and not the soft way we sling that word around up here, but a real one, a stain stamped across the whole family that raised him. And it isn't only the hard stuff that earns it. A weed smoker, a fella who likes a quiet joint at the end of his day, the most harmless stoner the Lord ever breathed life into — in that town he's a bum, a nobody, a thing his people are ashamed to put their name next to. They will sell the rest of the world its poison by the truckload and look straight down their nose at the one cousin soft enough to swallow any of it. I never squared it. I doubt I ever will.

But drink — drink is something else entirely. Drink they love. The whole place gets *gloriously* hammered, top to bottom, grandmother to grandson; there is a bottle on every table and a cold case in every truck bed and not one ounce of shame anywhere near any of it. In Sinaloa the drunk is just a man enjoying himself. It's the *druggie* they spit on. So the one substance on God's earth that had its hooks all the way through me was not just permitted down there — it was practically a civic duty.

Which is the whole reason what we did next was a lie, and a small ugly one.

Because we were drinking again. Of course we were. We'd cracked that seal back in Peñasco months before and we were never truly going to get it shut again, and a place where the booze flowed like that was the last place on earth two people like us were going to hold a line. But I had made Rose a promise — looked that woman in the eye and swore I would not let her daughter drink — and so we did the coward's version of keeping it. We hid it. Rose never knew we'd started back up; she went the whole two months believing her girl was bone dry. And America never saw a drop touch our lips, not once. If I wanted a beer, I poured it into a Yeti — the same trick, the same cup, I'd run on my own newly sober father a year before, the tumbler that turns a drink into a coffee, a water, a nothing — and I drank it like that, in front of the child, hidden in plain sight in my own hand.  Now, we didn't drink more than a couple.  We never got drunk.  We had our one or two and then put it down, but still I had crossed a border and a desert and a mountain of grief and I was right back to drinking out of a Yeti so the people who loved me wouldn't see. Some things you carry no matter how far you run. The Yeti was one of mine.

That was the truth of those two months, under the family and the cousins and the long uncle's table — good, and real, and shot all the way through with a lie I poured into a steel cup. And it was into the middle of What they put in front of us was older than the lie in my cup and older than the booze underneath it — older, maybe, than Sinaloa itself.

Ayahuasca. *La medicina.* The family knew of a *curandero*, a man who ran ceremonies out of a concrete and corrugated-tin compound on the dust-choked edge of town. The pitch — if a sacrament can have a pitch — was that this was the thing that finally cleaned a person out for good. That it reached down past whatever you'd been numbing and pulled the thing itself up by the root. The promise was that a man who sat with it honestly could walk away from the bottle, the pipe, the needle, the whole unbearable load he'd been carrying, and never want any of it again.

It was a two-week commitment. Five ceremonies spaced out every two or three days. And Glenda and I, who had just spent half a year proving to each other that we could not be trusted with a single sweating can of Tecate, signed on for the express and only purpose of finally killing the thirst.

That is not remotely what either of us got.

### The Dieta

It started with the *dieta*. You do not just walk off the street and drink the brew. A full week before you ever see the inside of the compound, they start stripping you down, taking a wire brush to your biology. No salt. No sugar. No pork. No red meat. No dairy. No caffeine. No sex. And, most terrifying of all for two people whose blood was essentially eighty proof by volume: no liquor.

For seven days, our entire intake consisted of boiled green plantains, plain white rice, and room-temperature water.

There is a specific kind of madness that sets into the body when you remove every single comfort it has ever known at the exact same time. The first two days, the caffeine withdrawals alone split my skull. It felt like a hot iron spike was being driven through the bridge of my nose, right between the eyes. My joints ached. My skin crawled. Glenda was no better. We moved around Rose’s brother’s house like ghosts, pale and trembling, sweating through our t-shirts in the Sinaloa heat.

The hunger was a living thing. Boiled plantains without salt taste like damp cardboard, and after the third day, the stomach just stops recognizing it as food. You eat it solely to keep your stomach acid from eating the lining of your own gut.

And then there was the environment. The sheer, cruel irony of undertaking this monastic asceticism in the middle of a Sinaloa holiday. Outside our bedroom door, the house was a carnival of excess. The uncles were roasting whole pigs, the smell of charred pork fat and garlic wafting under the door frame, so rich it made my empty stomach cramp violently. They were drinking cases of Pacifico, the *t-ssss* of aluminum cans cracking open acting as a constant metronome to our suffering. Tequila poured freely. The laughter was loud, the music was loud, the life was loud. And there we were, locked in a guest room, eating wet rice, pretending we were doing something noble.

And we held the line. That was the hardest part. For the first time, we put the Yeti cups away completely. We had spent the weeks prior keeping the drinking down to a dull roar—just one or two a day, sneaking it so we wouldn't get drunk, just enough to keep the engine turning over. But the *dieta* demanded absolute zero. Two weeks of absolute zero.

And we gave it absolute zero. We were staring down the barrel of a medicine that demanded purity, and we were trying, desperately, to be pure. We didn't cheat. We didn't crack. But without that daily buffer, the alcohol wasn't masking the pain anymore. Every nerve ending was suddenly exposed to the air. The anxiety didn't just spike; it settled into our chests like wet cement.

By the time the sun began to set on the seventh day, the day of the first ceremony, my body was a hollowed-out shell. I was starving, I was exhausted from the sheer psychological weight of being totally dry in a house full of drinkers, and my blood was a cold soup of pure, unmedicated dread.

### Ceremony One: The Meat Grinder

The compound was a mile off the paved road, down a deeply rutted dirt path that our truck’s suspension complained about every inch of the way. Dust hung in the air, illuminated by the headlights, thick as snow.

We pulled up to a concrete wall topped with broken glass. A heavy wooden gate swung open, pulled by a young man who didn't speak. He motioned us inside.

The *maloca*—the ceremonial room—was a large, square concrete structure with a corrugated tin roof. Inside, it was an oven. The heat of the day was trapped beneath the tin, radiating downward. The room was lit only by a few flickering candles and the cherry of the *curandero’s* cigarette.

His name was Mateo. He was a small, leathery man with eyes that looked like they had seen the bottom of the ocean. He didn't offer a warm greeting. He didn't ask us about our trauma or our hopes. He just sat cross-legged at the head of the room, smoking *mapacho*—a raw, jungle tobacco that smelled like burning tires and damp earth. The smoke hung in the stagnant air, so thick it stung the eyes and coated the back of the throat.

There were twelve of us. Twelve thin, foam mattresses arranged in a circle along the concrete walls. Beside each mattress was a red plastic bucket. A roll of cheap toilet paper sat inside each bucket.

Glenda and I took our places side by side. I sat cross-legged on my thin mat, feeling the hard concrete through the foam. The room was dead silent, save for the rhythmic, wet sound of Mateo pulling on his *mapacho* and the distant bark of a stray dog.

When it was time, Mateo extinguished the candles. The darkness was absolute. It wasn't the kind of dark where your eyes eventually adjust; it was a heavy, suffocating blackness that pressed against the retinas.

I heard the pouring first. The sound of thick liquid glugging from a two-liter plastic Coca-Cola bottle into a wooden cup.

He called us up one by one. I crawled forward in the dark, feeling the cold concrete under my palms until my knees touched the edge of his mat. I could barely see his silhouette. He handed me the wooden cup.

The smell hit me before the rim even touched my lips. It smelled like the grave. It smelled like wet dirt, rotting bark, and something deeply, inherently toxic. It triggered a gag reflex instantly.

I held my breath, tilted my head back, and swallowed.

It was the foulest thing I have ever willingly put in my body. It was thick, almost viscous, like motor oil. The taste was a brutal, sour, bitter brown that coated my tongue and throat. It tasted like the bottom of the oldest tree in the world, mashed into mud and left to ferment in the sun. I choked it down, handed the cup back, wiped my mouth with the back of a trembling hand, and crawled back to my mat.

I lay down. And I waited.

For the first forty-five minutes, there was nothing. Just the stifling heat, the suffocating dark, and the anxious breathing of eleven other people. I started to wonder if it was a scam. Maybe the medicine was weak. Maybe I was just too broken for it to fix.

Then, the *icaros* began.

Mateo started shaking a *chacapa*—a bundle of dried leaves. *Shhh-shhh-shhh-shhh.* The sound was crisp, rhythmic, cutting through the heavy air. Then, he began to sing. His voice was high, nasal, and haunting. The melodies were strange, repetitive, sliding up and down scales that didn't make sense to Western ears. The song seemed to circle the room, bouncing off the concrete walls, digging directly into my inner ear.

With the music came the physical shift.

It started as a vibration at the base of my spine, a low hum like a heavy piece of machinery kicking on in the basement of my body. My core temperature spiked. Instantly, I was slick with cold sweat. My heart rate doubled, thudding against my ribs like a trapped bird.

I closed my eyes, but the darkness was gone. Against the backs of my eyelids, jagged neon geometric patterns began to breathe. A kaleidoscope of razor-sharp fractals, twisting and folding in on themselves, pulsing perfectly in time with the rhythm of the *chacapa*. It wasn't beautiful. It was terrifying. It felt invasive, as if an alien intelligence had hacked into my optic nerves.

And then the nausea hit.

It wasn't the nausea of food poisoning. It wasn't the nausea of a brutal hangover. It was a cellular rejection. It felt as if my skeleton was trying to expel itself from my flesh. My stomach clamped down into a fist. The thick, bitter sludge I had swallowed suddenly felt like it was expanding, multiplying, turning into a solid mass of rot inside my gut.

They call it *la purga*. The purge. And they are not being delicate about the terminology.

When it came, it offered no warning. I rolled over onto my hands and knees, dragging the plastic bucket under my face. I heaved. The force of it lifted my knees off the mat. I threw up the medicine. I threw up the boiled plantains. I threw up the water. When there was nothing left, I threw up pure, burning stomach acid.

And when the acid was gone, the medicine kept squeezing. I dry-heaved until my intercostal muscles spasmed, until my ribs felt like they were splintering, until tears of sheer biological agony streamed down my face.

All around the room, the chorus of retching began. The sound of twelve people violently, helplessly emptying themselves into plastic buckets in the pitch black. The stench of vomit mixed with the heavy smoke of the *mapacho*. Through it all, Mateo’s voice grew louder, the shaking of the leaves grew faster, driving the sickness out, forcing the bodies to wring themselves completely dry.

There were no profound spiritual insights that first night. There were no visions of ancestors or deep emotional healings. There was only pure, unadulterated physical violence. The medicine didn't touch my soul; it just took a wire brush to my guts and scraped me raw.

When the sun finally came up, I lay on my mat, a trembling, hollowed-out husk, staring at the corrugated tin ceiling. I had survived, but I was broken. And I knew, with a sickening drop in my stomach, that I had to do this four more times.

### Days 4-5: The Purgatory

The days between the first and second ceremonies were a bruised, silent purgatory. If the *dieta* had made us weak, the first night of the medicine had turned us into ghosts.

Glenda and I barely spoke. There was nothing to say. We sat on the tiled patio of Rose's brother's house, staring out at the dust kicked up by the passing pickup trucks, drinking warm water. Every muscle fiber in my body ached as if I had been beaten with a rubber hose. My throat was raw and burned from the acid. My eyes were sunken, ringed with bruised purple shadows.

Inside the house, the holiday raged on. The laughter, the music, the endless cracking of beer cans. It sounded like it was happening on another planet. I didn't even want a drink anymore. Not because I was cured, but because the physical trauma of the purge had left me too battered to stomach the idea of swallowing anything at all.

As the sun began to dip below the horizon on the fifth day, painting the Sinaloa sky in violent streaks of orange and bruised violet, a cold dread settled into my chest. The shadows lengthened. It was time to go back.

Getting into the truck felt like walking to the gallows. I looked over at Glenda in the passenger seat. She was staring straight ahead, her face pale, her jaw locked tight. We were volunteering for a firing squad, paying a man to poison us in the dark. But we were desperate. Desperate enough to believe that if we just pushed through the physical horror, the promised miracle would be waiting on the other side.

### Ceremony Two: The Hall of Mirrors

The second night, the maloca felt different. The heat was the same, the suffocating blackness was the same, but the air felt heavier, charged with a strange, static electricity.

Mateo poured the brew. It tasted exactly as vile as I remembered—the bitter mud of an ancient grave. I swallowed it, crawled to my mat, and curled into a fetal position, bracing myself for the violence of the purge. I gripped the plastic bucket with white knuckles, waiting for the nausea to tear my ribs apart again.

But the medicine is a trickster. It rarely gives you what you are braced for.

The physical purge never came. My stomach cramped, a low dull ache, but it held. Instead, the temperature in the room plummeted. I was shivering, wrapping my thin arms around my knees. And then, without warning, the darkness shattered.

I was no longer on a mat in Sinaloa. The geometric fractals—the neon jaguars and shifting mandalas that people talk about when they talk about psychedelics—didn't appear. Instead, the medicine dragged me directly into my own past.

I was standing on the beige carpet of a Las Vegas telemarketing floor. The fluorescent lights overhead buzzed with a sickening, high-pitched frequency. The air smelled of stale coffee, cheap cologne, and the metallic tang of desperation. Row after row of cubicles stretched out to infinity.

And I was talking. I was pitching.

I could hear my own voice echoing through the endless room, but it didn't sound like me. It sounded slick, greasy, devoid of anything human. It was the voice of the hustle. The armor I had built over forty years to survive, to con, to charm my way out of consequences. I watched myself spin lies to faceless marks on the phone, watched myself manipulate, watched the effortless, charismatic sociopathy that had kept me fed and funded my drinking for decades.

The medicine wasn't showing me monsters; it was showing me myself. And it was showing me without the protective lens of my own ego.

Every lie I had ever told played back on a loop. Every manipulation, every half-truth, every excuse I had made for leaving, running, abandoning people when things got hard. The medicine stripped away the romantic narrative I had built around my life—the gritty survivor, the tragic outlaw. It showed me the raw, ugly machinery underneath. I was a coward wrapped in charm. A fraud who used words to keep everyone at a safe, untouchable distance.

I tried to turn away. I tried to open my eyes and force myself back to the concrete room in Mexico. But you cannot close your eyes to the medicine. The visions are projected onto the inside of your skull.

The telemarketing floor folded away. The fluorescent lights snapped off, the hum of a hundred hustlers going dead quiet, replaced by the heavy, humid stillness of a suburban afternoon.

And then I was five years old.

I was standing in the driveway behind my childhood home. And standing next to me was my cousin David.

My mother’s brother Dudley had exactly one boy. Just one. And there he was, five years old, looking up at the heavy wooden panels of the garage door. The medicine didn't just show me the memory; it dropped me back into the small, fragile body I had back then. I felt the hot pavement through my sneakers. I felt the innocent, childish pride swelling in my chest as I reached up to show him the magic trick. *Look how it works. Look what the button does.* I showed him the automatic opener.

I didn't mean to kill him. We were five. But the door came down. And David didn't get out from under it.

The medicine forced me to stand there and watch the thing that had torn our families cleanly in half. It forced me to look at my uncle Dudley, a man who had his only son crushed out of existence on a random afternoon, while my father—standing right there in the wreckage of our family—got to keep his. They lost their boy. We kept ours.

The driveway faded, and the labyrinth of mirrors came up. Everywhere I turned, I saw a reflection of the man I had become.

The guilt wasn't just suffocating; it was absolute. It was the survivor’s guilt that had been rotting in my basement for forty years. The undeniable, agonizing math of it: *I survived.* The universe spared me and took David. And what had I done with the life I got to keep? I had turned it into a playground of alcohol, drugs, and cheap Vegas cons. I had taken the oxygen that David was denied and used it to spin lies to strangers on the phone. I had taken the gift of growing up and pissed it away in dive bars from Anchorage to Cabo.

I looked at my own reflection in the dark of the *maloca* and I was so deeply, viscerally embarrassed to be alive. I felt an overwhelming sorrow for my uncle, for my family, for the ghost of a five-year-old boy who traded places with a fraud.

I spent four hours in that psychological meat grinder. When Mateo finally blew out the candles and the ceremony ended, I was hyperventilating, drenched in sweat, my hands clawing at the foam mat. I hadn't thrown up a drop, but I felt infinitely sicker than I had on the first night. The medicine had found the bedrock of my shame, and it had begun to dismantle me, piece by terrifying piece.

### Ceremony Three: The Core Wound

The third ceremony, two nights later, was the one that broke the floor open.

I went in exhausted. I went in terrified. I did not go in there for my daughter. I want that on the record. I went in there for the drink, like we'd planned. My intention was all squared away, rehearsed in my head, pointed at the bottle like a good soldier. *Show me why I drink. Take the thirst away.* That was the deal. That was the transaction I thought I was making.

The medicine didn't care what I'd planned. The medicine went straight past the alcohol like it wasn't even worth its time. It bypassed the Vegas scams, went under the terror of the cartel roadblocks, ignored the petty grifts and the daily lies. It dove down to the bedrock. Down to the oldest, coldest thing in me. The thing I had spent ten years, four thousand miles, and an ocean of vodka actively refusing to look at.

It took my face in both its hands, and it turned it toward my little girl.

I had a daughter. I have told you that, in passing, the way I've trained myself to say it. *Had a two-year-old once, lost her to adoption, a lifetime ago in Alaska.* I say it fast and flat, like I'm reading a line off a tax form, so the words can get out of my mouth before the emotion behind them catches up. I had spent a decade building a concrete vault around that memory. I had drowned it in every bar from Anchorage to Cabo.

That night, in the dark of the *maloca*, the vault blew open.

The room didn't just fade away; it ceased to exist. The air turned bitterly cold, the kind of dry, biting cold that belongs only to the Alaskan winter. I could hear the crunch of snow under boots. And then, there she was.

She wasn't an abstract concept or a blurry memory. She was vivid. She was real. She was two years old, standing in front of me in a thick pink snowsuit. I could smell the Johnson & Johnson baby shampoo in her hair. I could see the exact shade of her eyes, the way her small, mittened hand reached out for mine. I felt the physical weight of her in my arms, a phantom pressure against my chest that was so real my body physically ached.

And then, the brutal, unforgiving math began.

The medicine forced me to do the calculation I had spent a decade avoiding. She was two when she went. Ten years had passed. She was twelve now.

Twelve.

The toddler in the pink snowsuit dissolved, replaced by a ghost of a pre-teen girl. A near-teenage girl somewhere out in the world, living a life I knew nothing about. She had some other good man's last name. She was learning algebra, fighting with her mother, discovering the world, and I wasn't there. Worse than not being there—I was a ghost. If I walked past her on a street tomorrow, she wouldn't know my face. She wouldn't look twice at the broken, weary man passing her on the sidewalk.

The purge that came then wasn't physical. It didn't start in my stomach. It started deep in the marrow of my bones.

It was grief. Pure, unadulterated, decade-old grief heaving up out of me all at once. The kind of sorrow that is so vast and heavy it feels like it will crack your spine. I fell off my mat. I collapsed onto the hard concrete floor, curling into a tight ball around the plastic bucket, and I wept.

I didn't cry. I wailed. It was a guttural, animal sound, the kind of sobbing that tears your throat raw and strips your vocal cords. I wept the way I have not wept since I was a terrified boy hiding in the dark.

I wept for her. I wept for the milestones I missed. But mostly, I wept for the fact that I hadn't fought for her.

The medicine stripped away the central lie of my life: the lie that letting her go had been the kind thing, the strong thing, the selfless act of a man who knew he couldn't provide. That was bullshit. It had always been bullshit. I let her go because it was the exact same thing I have always done. I leave before the leaving can be done to me. It was a geographic cure of the soul. I had abandoned my own child standing in the road the same way I abandoned every town, every job, every relationship I ever got scared of.

And then came the cruelest cut of the whole night. The medicine made absolutely sure I didn't miss the irony.

It pulled up the image of America. Young, eleven-year-old America, throwing up high-fives in the truck, holding back her trust, watching me with careful eyes. I had come all the way down to this country, crossed a desert, braved cartel checkpoints, and fallen straight into trying to play father to a girl who wasn't mine. I was pouring every ounce of my paternal ache into Glenda's daughter, desperate to protect her, to guide her, to be a man for her, while my own flesh and blood walked around the earth a stranger to me.

The medicine laid those two girls side by side in the dark. The phantom twelve-year-old in Alaska, and the living eleven-year-old sleeping in a bedroom a few miles away. It made me look at both of them, forced me to measure the distance between my guilt and my desperate attempts at redemption, until I couldn't draw breath.

Through the roaring in my ears, I heard Mateo. He had moved from the head of the room and was kneeling directly over me. The smell of *mapacho* smoke drifted down over my face. He was shaking the *chacapa* inches from my head, the dry leaves rustling like a sudden rainstorm. And he was singing an *icaro* just for me. A fast, urgent melody designed to pull the sorrow out of the tissue, to drag the black root of the grief out through the tears.

I lay on the concrete, drenched in sweat and snot and tears, my hands gripping the edges of the bucket like it was the only solid thing left in the universe, and I let the ten years of grief break me completely in half.

### Ceremony Four: The Fists

By the time the fourth ceremony rolled around, the physical toll of the retreat had rendered both of us almost entirely mute. We were moving through the days on a baseline of severe caloric deficit and chronic sleep deprivation. The mere thought of liquor made my already-battered stomach violently contract. We had been stripped down to the studs.

I went into the fourth night expecting another war. I lay on my mat, gripping my bucket, waiting for the medicine to drag me back into the frozen Alaskan dark or to plunge me into some new, undiscovered ring of psychological hell.

But it didn't.

For me, the fourth night was strangely quiet. The medicine washed over me like a heavy, suffocating blanket, but it didn't dig. I felt a profound, heavy exhaustion, a physical weight that pressed me flat against the concrete floor, rendering me entirely paralyzed. I lay there in the dark, unable to move a muscle, breathing shallowly into the humid air, functioning only as an observer.

It was on this fourth night that the medicine finally came for Glenda.

You can always hear the others in the room. The *maloca* is a symphony of everybody's worst, most deeply buried trauma coming up out of them all at once. Usually, it's a cacophony of dry heaves, retching, and low groans. But Glenda’s purge was different. It cut through the ambient noise of the room like a siren.

It started as a rapid, terrified hyperventilation. I lay paralyzed on my mat, listening to her breath hitch and catch in the dark just a few feet away. Then came the sound of her scrambling backward, her foam mat scraping against the concrete, as if she were desperately trying to get away from something standing right in front of her.

And then, she began to sob.

It wasn't the cathartic, grieving wail I had let out the night before. This was a guttural, jagged sound of sheer, unadulterated terror. It was the sound of an animal caught in a trap. I wanted to reach out to her. I wanted to tell her she was safe, that she was just in a concrete room in Sinaloa, but the medicine held me pinned to the floor. All I could do was listen to the woman I loved experience a horror I couldn't see.

Mateo’s *chacapa* grew louder, faster. He moved to her side of the room, his *icaros* changing from a high melody into a rapid, rhythmic chanting, trying to weave a net under her, trying to catch her before she fell completely into the dark. She sobbed for what felt like hours, her body shaking so violently I could hear the foam mat vibrating against the floor.

She told me the shape of it the next morning.

We were sitting on the edge of the guest bed in Rose's brother's house. The Sinaloa sun was just beginning to bleach the sky. We were both wrung out, hollowed, and gentle with each other the way two people only get after surviving something entirely unspeakable. Her eyes were swollen shut. Her hands, resting in her lap, were trembling.

Her nightmare, it turned out, had nothing to do with a bottle, either.

Hers was America's father.

The medicine had reached down into the deepest, darkest corner of her mind and dragged her back into the rooms she had spent the last three years drinking to forget. The man who had given America half her blood, and then given her mother the back of his hand, over and over, in the years before Glenda finally found the courage to get out from under him.

"I was back there," she whispered, her voice barely a scrape against the quiet of the morning. "I was right back in the corner of the living room."

The medicine hadn't just shown her the memories; it had forced her to physically relive them. It made her feel the weight of his fists again. It made her feel the exact, suffocating helplessness of being trapped in a room with a man twice her size who wanted to break her. It dragged every ounce of the sheer, animal terror she had packed down under three years of vodka and boxed wine straight to the surface. She hadn't just remembered the abuse; her nervous system had re-experienced it, firing on all cylinders, trapped in the pitch black of the *maloca*.

But under the terror of the fists, the medicine had found the deeper wound.

It was the guilt. The crushing, suffocating guilt that her little girl was growing up with no father at all. Glenda wept on the edge of the bed as she explained it. She had left a monster to protect her child, but in doing so, she had left a gaping hole in America's life where a man should be. That was what came up out of Glenda into her plastic bucket in the dark. Not the drink. The fists, the terror, the bruises, and the heavy, unshakeable guilt of a mother raising a fatherless girl.

We sat there in the dawn light, two broken people who had come down to Mexico to cure a drinking problem. And the medicine had reached into each of us, bypassed the alcohol entirely, and pulled up our deepest, most agonizing failures—the crushed boy and the dead daughter out of me, the live wound out of her. It had held them up to the light and said, *This. This is what's actually down here. Not the thing you came to show me.*

### Ceremony Five: The Empty Room

The fifth and final ceremony felt like a funeral. We went in knowing there was nothing left to give. We had vomited until our stomachs bled. We had wept until our tear ducts were dry. We had faced the absolute worst versions of ourselves and the most terrifying ghosts of our pasts.

When I drank the brew for the fifth time, my body didn't even fight it. I lay down, closed my eyes, and waited.

And nothing happened.

There were no neon fractals. There were no Alaskan snowdrifts. There were no Vegas telemarketing floors. There was no nausea, no purge, no weeping. There was just an empty, quiet room.

I lay on my mat for hours, listening to Mateo sing his final *icaros*, feeling an overwhelming sense of lightness. It wasn't joy. It wasn't euphoria. It was simply the absence of weight. I felt like a house that had been completely gutted by a fire. The walls were scorched, the furniture was gone, the roof was missing, but the foundation was finally, mercifully bare.

I had been scoured clean. We both had. We had walked through the fire, let the medicine burn away the thick layers of trauma and denial we had spent decades building, and we had survived.

When the sun rose on the final morning, we packed our bags. We hugged Mateo, who looked at us with those ancient, ocean-deep eyes, nodding once as if to say, *the work is done.* We got into the truck, said goodbye the uncles, and began the long drive north, out of Sinaloa, heading back toward the border.

### The Cruel Joke

Here is what nobody warns you about the medicine. Here is the truest, most brutal thing it taught me, a lesson it delivered sideways and entirely at my own expense.

It worked. It worked on absolutely everything, except the one thing we had asked it for.

We came out of those two weeks hollowed out, grieved-empty, and lighter in our souls than either of us had been since we were children. We had faced the absolute bottom of our own darkness. We had laid the ghosts to rest.

And as the truck tires ate up the highway miles heading north, I realized something that made my blood run cold.

I was not one single degree less thirsty for a drink.

It had cracked the floor of my psychology clean open. It had shown me the oldest, most broken things lying down there in the dark. It had forced me to face the ghost of my little cousin and process a decade of buried grief over my daughter. And it had not laid so much as a finger on the bottle.

We had been to the very bottom of ourselves. We had excavated the exact place that all the experts, all the sad-eyed counselors, and all the rehab brochures swear the drinking comes *from*. And we had found that the bottom didn't have the drinking in it at all.

Because that is the lie. That is the comfortable, marketable lie that everybody wants to be true: that a man drinks *because of* something. That addiction is just a symptom of trauma, and if you can only reach the trauma and heal it, the drinking will lift off him like a morning fog.

I believed it a little, going in. I do not believe it now.

You can drag the crushed boy, and the dead daughter, and the telemarketing lies, and the bruises, and the fists, and the fatherless child all up out of the dark. You can face them, you can weep over them, you can lay them to rest one by one until your soul is as clean as a freshly washed floor.

And the wanting will still be sitting there in the morning.

Clean, patient, and entirely its own animal. It will be sitting right there on the edge of the bed, asking the exact same thing it always asks.

The grief was real. The trauma was down there. The pain was legitimate. But the grief was never what the drink was standing on. The drink stands on nothing but itself.

So we grieved. And we healed something neither of us had been able to touch in ten years. And then January ran out, and the holidays were over, and it was time to start the long way back north. Back toward Peñasco. Back across that toll road I prayed we'd find this time. Back toward a line on a map where a man was allowed to walk into a store, buy a bottle, and drink it in the open.

And the absolute, terrifying truth of the matter was this: the second we crossed that line, we both knew we were going to drink again.
