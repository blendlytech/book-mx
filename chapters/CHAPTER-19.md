# Chapter 19 — The Company Town

There is one thing about Sinaloa I turned over in my hands the whole two months and never did get to lie flat, so I'll hand it to you the way it was handed to me.

That state grows and moves and sells a fair share of every bit of misery that ever ended up in an American vein or up an American nose. It is, in the plainest words I've got, the company town for the entire industry of getting high. And it holds the lowest opinion of a person who *actually gets high* that I have ever come across anywhere on this earth.

Down there a man who does *drugs* is *basura.* Trash. A loser — and not the soft way we throw that word around up here, but the real kind, a stain stamped across the whole family that raised him. And it isn't just the hard stuff that earns it. A weed smoker, a fella who likes a quiet joint at the end of his day, the most harmless stoner the good Lord ever made — in that town he's a bum, a nobody, a thing his people are ashamed to put their name beside. They will sell the rest of the world its poison by the truckload and look straight down their nose at the one cousin soft enough to swallow any of it. I never squared that. I doubt I ever will.

But drink — drink is another animal entirely. Drink they love. The whole place gets *gloriously* hammered, top to bottom, grandmother to grandson, a bottle on every table and a cold case in every truck bed and not one ounce of shame within a mile of any of it. In Sinaloa the drunk is just a man enjoying his evening. It's the druggie they spit on. So the one substance on God's green earth that had its hooks clean through me wasn't only allowed down there — it was damn near a civic duty.

Which is the whole reason what we did next was a lie. A small, ugly one.

Because we were drinking again. Of course we were. We'd cracked that seal back in Peñasco months before and we were never truly going to get it shut, and a place where the booze ran like that was the last spot on earth two people like us were going to hold any kind of line. But I'd made Rose a promise — looked that woman dead in the eye and swore I would not let her daughter drink — so we kept it the coward's way. We hid it. Rose went the entire time believing her girl was bone dry. And America never saw a drop touch our lips, not once.

If I wanted a beer I poured it into a Yeti — the same cup, the same trick I'd run on my own newly sober father a year before, the tumbler that turns a beer into a coffee, a water, a nothing — and I drank it like that, in front of the child, hidden in plain sight in my own hand. We never took it far. One or two, never drunk; we'd have our couple and set it down. But there it was anyway: I had crossed a border and a desert and a whole mountain range of grief, and I was right back to drinking out of a steel cup so the people I loved wouldn't see. Some things you carry no matter how far you run. The Yeti was one of mine.

That was the truth of those two months, underneath the family and the cousins and the long table at the uncle's house — good, and real, and shot all the way through with a lie I poured into a cup.

---

Now here's the part I've skipped, and it's the biggest part of those two months.

I couldn't talk to anybody.

Forty words of Spanish, and most of them the kind you pick up off men working on a car. I'd been living in that country a year and never learned it, and Sinaloa is where that bill came due. It isn't a border town. Nobody there has any reason on this earth to own one word of English, and near enough nobody did.

The uncle's house sat behind a concrete wall on a street of concrete walls. Iron gate, dogs that lived up on the roof, a courtyard of poured concrete with a mango tree coming up out of a cut square in the middle of it, and under a tin lean-to a long table where every single thing that ever happened in that family happened. Sometimes as many as thirty people at it. Cousins I never did get sorted out. Kids underfoot, a radio going, meat over coals, a cooler nobody ever let get empty. One night, I remember, it rained like a curtain coming down, sheet water hitting that tin roof so hard you couldn't hear yourself think, but the meal kept going. The plates stayed on the table, the meat kept coming, the laughter never wavered.

And me down at the end of it, smiling like a man in a photograph.

The uncle — Rose's brother, short and wide and somewhere in his seventies, forearms like fence posts — had about six words of English and he spent all six of them on me the first night. *Welcome. My house. Good, good.* Then he was out of ammunition and we both knew it, and after that he handled me about the way you'd handle a big dog somebody left at your place over the holidays. He'd catch my eye down the table and lift his chin at me. He'd point at a dish and then point at me and wait there until I ate it.

The second night he held a cold Pacifico out across the table and I had to wave it off with Rose sitting four feet away, and I watched a small confusion come across that man's face and settle in. Rose said something again and his face said it all, that a grown man in Sinaloa won't take a beer at his host's table. He never asked me about it. Probably because he really couldn't in my language. He never offered again, either. I'd made myself strange to him in the one language the two of us didn't share and there was no way back across it to explain.

He worked it out on his own eventually. Three weeks in he came around the corner of the house while I was pouring one into the Yeti and he stopped, and looked at the can, and looked at the cup, and looked at me. Then he laughed once through his nose and went on by. As far as I know he never said a word about it to anybody. Down there I wasn't doing a thing wrong. I was just doing it strangely.

There was no internet in that house. Not slow internet — none. No line coming in, no box on the wall, nothing to log into.

What there was, was spots. You went out and got it. There was a place up the street where the wall ended and two bars would come in if you stood still in the one particular place. There was the corner by the school. And there was a rise out past the last houses where the whole town fell away under you and a signal came across all that flat ground from wherever it came from, and if the weather was right you could get a call to go through. You didn't have internet down there. You gathered it, out of the air, standing up. You walked out with your phone in your hand like a man going to a well.

That's where the money lived, too. Once a week or so I'd walk out to that rise and let the phone drink for twenty minutes and watch the numbers come in off Edgar's floor a thousand miles back north — the last of my life that ran in English, arriving on a hill outside a Sinaloa town at four in the afternoon.

So everything came to me through somebody else. That's the whole shape of those two months. Two months, and I did not have one unmediated conversation with another human being on this earth outside of Glenda and the girl.

Rose translated when she had it in her. But Rose was *home* — first time in a long while, a brother to sit with, a hundred people to see, a kitchen she'd been running since she was a kid. She'd drop into the chair beside me and hand me the last ten minutes in thirty seconds and then somebody would call her name and she'd be gone.

Glenda translated the way anybody translates when they've been at it all day, which is to say less and less as it went. I'd ask what the table was laughing at and she'd say, nothing, they're just talking. A ten-minute story with a whole family coming apart at the end of it, and what I'd get was: it's about a horse. She wasn't being cruel. Interpreting is labor, and she was on vacation, and she wanted for two months to be a woman at her uncle's table instead of a machine that turns one language into another.

And then there was the kid.

I don't know what got into America down there. Something did. Whatever it was that had held that girl out at arm's length from me for three solid months in Peñasco, she set it down when we crossed into Sinaloa, and she took me over.

That's the only way I know how to put it. She took me over. Eleven years old.

She'd come find me. That was the first thing and it never stopped. Twenty people at that table and the noise going and I'd have been out of it for half an hour, and she'd notice — she'd be clear across the courtyard with three cousins and she'd *notice* — and she'd come drop into the chair beside me and start feeding me the room, low, out the side of her mouth, like a girl calling a horse race.

She didn't translate the words. She translated the *room*. That's the difference, and I didn't have a name for it then. Rose gave me sentences. America gave me who was mad at who, and which uncle was full of it, and which cousin wasn't supposed to have brought that girl, and whether the thing the whole table had just roared at was about me. She always told me when it was about me. She always told me it was fine.

She rated them, too. She'd give me a joke and then tell me it wasn't even that funny, they just think it's funny.

And she told me what to do. Eat this one. Don't eat that one, it's too hot for you. When somebody put a plate in my hands: take it, say gracias, that's all, you don't have to say anything else. When I started reaching for my wallet at a store one afternoon, a flat little hand came down on my forearm — no. Not here. My grandma will get mad.

She walked me out to the rise for the internet. Every time. She knew every spot in that town and she took it on like a job somebody had handed her, marching the tall stupid gringo out past the last houses with the dogs going and the light coming down orange over all that flat country, standing there kicking rocks and narrating her cousins while my phone drank.

And when she got bored she was gone. She'd run the room for me for twenty minutes and then a cousin would holler her name and she'd be up and out of there for four hours and I'd be right back to nodding at people. She was eleven. She wasn't a saint and she wasn't a little angel sent to look after me. She was just the only person in that house who ever looked over and saw a man sitting there with nothing to say and no way to say it, and did something about it.

I never did get a hug out of that girl. Two months in Sinaloa and it was still the flat hand out and the high five and nothing else, same arrangement as always.

And she did every bit of it for a man drinking beer out of a coffee cup in front of her.

---

And it was into the middle of all of that that they put the medicine in front of us.
