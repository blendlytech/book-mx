# Chapter 15 — Courting on a Leash

Here is the part nobody warns you about when you finally decide to get clean in the same season you finally fall in love: you have to do them both stone sober, in a bad neighborhood, in a country where the word they hung on the smartest man in the building was just the Spanish for a sad face.

I'll get to him.

---

Glenda went back to the free side of the rehab — could come and go now, run an errand, walk to the store, come down to me of an evening — but on a leash with a length to it. A curfew. And a cup every single time she came back through that door, plus the lady leaning in close to smell her breath, the way they do. She'd earned the come-and-go and not one inch past it.

So I rented an Airbnb a few blocks off. Another bad neighborhood, another bad part of another town. I was getting to be a connoisseur of those. But this time was different from every other bad room I'd ever holed up in, and it was different in the one way I'd never once managed before: this time I was going to be sober for the whole of it.

I mean that the way they meant it down there. I put myself on the same cup. Same breath check. I couldn't have a drunk night and then stand at that door in the morning trying to chew my way past that lady's nose — and more than that, I didn't want to. That's the part that still catches me when I turn it over. I actually didn't want to. Underneath the deal and the floor and the girl and every loud thing I'd built on top of it, getting clean was a piece of what I'd come down to do, and I'd just never said it out loud, maybe not even to myself, until I was standing in a bad little kitchen in Mexicali blowing into a cup with nothing on my breath to hide.

---

She cooked for me every night.

She'd come down to the Airbnb in the evening and make us dinner — bottled water in every pot from then on, never the tap again, not after the tuition I'd paid on that lesson — and we'd eat at the little table like two people who'd been doing it for twenty years instead of three weeks in the flesh. We'd sit. We'd talk. We'd be a thing that had a kitchen.

And every night at 8:30 I'd walk her back.

A few blocks, her hand in mine, through the kind of dark you keep your head up in, to the door of the place she had to be inside of by curfew — and I'd kiss her goodnight on the step and tell her *see you in the morning,* and she'd go in and pee in the cup, and I'd walk home alone and sober to a bed with nobody in it. Three years I'd kept this woman two thousand miles away on the far side of a screen because the distance felt like safety. Now the whole entire distance between us was four blocks and a cage door and a curfew, and I walked it twice a day, dead sober, and let me tell you, that is the closest I have ever come in my life to courting somebody. That walk back was the realest thing I did down there. More than the money. More than any of it.

And then — and this is the part that still gets me — I'd get back to the Airbnb, sober and alone, and the two of us would get on a video call anyway. For the pure nostalgia of it. Same glass, same lit-up faces, same as two thousand miles and three years of nights had trained us to do — except this time we'd actually held each other not twenty minutes before, the real warm weight of her still on my arms, and here we were back on the screen laughing about it. Laughing that we still did this. That we couldn't quite quit it. The screen that had been the entire shape of us for three years, the thing I'd called my safety, downgraded in one week to something we did for old times' sake. And both of us sober now, on both sides of it, for the first time in the whole history of the thing. No bottle in my hand and none in hers. Just two clear faces on two screens, a few blocks apart, laughing at the screen.

The room itself was a steal — you can get a steal in Mexico for next to nothing if you're not particular, and I'd long since quit being particular. One bedroom, a kitchen, a little living room, decent appliances, a propane stove that lit with a match and a prayer, cable, internet. I'd been paid out a week's salary plus two sales off the back end, fourteen hundred dollars in one check, and that fourteen hundred covered the place and all our living expenses for two whole months and we lived good for that time. It wasn't half bad. For two months it was the most settled four walls I'd had in years, and it was mine.

---

It took the better part of that first month to get the floor humming like a machine, but it got there.

Ten reps on the phones, setting fifteen or twenty appointments a day for the South Carolina floor. The reps never saw a dime of it; their labor was their payment to the rehab. My cut was a flat three hundred a week, plus a point and a half of every dollar that closed. Inside a month, I was clearing five thousand in commission. You don't get rich off a room full of addicts. You just get by, cleaner than you've ever gotten by before.

---

Then it came time to train my replacement, and that's where I met the saddest man I knew down there.

They called him Tristá.  He is actually a great dude. It's not a name — it's the Spanish for a sad face, more or less, and somebody had pinned it on him years back and it stuck the way a true thing sticks. His real name was Edgar. His English was better than half the closers I'd worked with stateside, and he was sharp, sharp enough that I had him running the floor inside a week, sharp enough to know exactly what he was and have no illusions left about getting out from under it.

Edgar had been in that building three years on heroin and had no plans to leave, because every single time he'd ever tried, he'd put a needle in his arm before the week was out, and his family had run clean out of forgiveness for it. They were never signing him out. He knew that. He was a lifer, and he said it about himself flat, no self-pity in it.

And that was the first time I really understood where I was.

Down there, if you get caught using — anything, and I mean anything, weed included, one joint, one bad cup — you can lose every right you have as a person. Your family can put you in a place like that and lock the door and throw the key in the gutter, and there is not one thing on God's earth you can do about it. One failed marijuana test, if the people who love you have decided they can't trust you loose, you are done. There were men in that building serving life sentences who didn't even know it for certain — because the same thing that could keep them there forever could also end tomorrow, the second one letter arrived from the very same family that had called the dogs to drag them out of their bedroom in the first place. That was the whole sentence and the whole pardon, sitting in one envelope that might never come. Freedom in there wasn't a date. It was a mood, somebody else's.

---

I knew exactly how that envelope got delivered, because I rode the van that did the dragging.

In the evenings they'd run pickups, and I started going along. A family calls the house — their boy, girl, husband, wife, uncle, anybody gone off the rails, drunk, stoned, lit up on meth, doesn't matter which, the menu's long and it all leads to the same phone call. They've got the money — 2,000 pesos, about $125 — and the house has got the van. You don't even need it upfront; they'll take payments. And if they don't have the money at all, the house will still go get them and put them to work. So we'd load up and roll out toward whatever address they'd given us, and we'd pile out and walk up to a door that the family opened for us themselves, from the inside, with their own hands — and we'd go in and take their loved one out of their room and put them in the van. Kicking. Screaming. Not understanding that the people who loved them were the ones who'd sent us.

I went on better than two dozen of those because Zaual needed the help.  I'm not sure to this day if he was just fucking with me and wanted me to see it, or if he really needed the help, but I went.  He'd text me and they would pick me up at the airbnb.  It was fucking crazy and it actually felt kind of good.  Like being needed or wanted or apart of something.  I don't know.

I was putting some weight on at this point and was about a foot taller than anynoe there so even as a skinny gringo, I was probably a bit intimidating to a skinny five foot meth head.  For some reason, I was all about it and was always the first in.  I think I just wanted to prove myself.  There was a kid one night who'd thrown his weight against his bedroom door to keep us out, and I leaned into it from my side, and the whole door came off the hinges at once — it was a flimsy thing to begin with — and pitched into the room with me on top of it. And I will never lose his face. A six-foot-one skinny gringo coming up off a busted door to wrap him in a bear hug, in his own bedroom, in his own house, with his own family standing in the hall who'd let me in. I have to figure it was the worst day of that kid's life. And I was the shape it took.

I don't know how that felt.  I couldn't sort the good from the bad of it even while my arms were around him.  I knew he needed it.  I'd needed it myself for twenty years and never once gotten it.  And I knew the pure animal terror going through him, the not-knowing whether this was a month or a year or the rest of his natural life, because I'd just been taught it could be any of the three.  To be clear, I only actually wrapped my arms around 3 people.  I remember each and every face and looking back now, it makes me laugh, but I still don't know what become of two of those people.  I only know of one and that will come later in the book.  

---

So that was me, that spring. Two months sober in a bad little room, watching my girl blow into the nose of a security guard and knowing she was pissing clean into a cup so I could walk my girl to dinner and back to a cage by 8:30. Running a phone floor and training new reps for a hundred and fifty to three hundred dollars a sale. Riding a van with Zaul at night, hauling strangers kicking into the same building I'd flown down here to *use* — the rehab where I'd cooked up a sales machine and bolted it on like a parasite to the side of a place full of broken people.

This cage with no clock on it, the one I was helping fill, was the one that was finally going to let her out.
