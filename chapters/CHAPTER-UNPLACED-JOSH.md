# Josh

Josh drove me to the airport.

I've already put that day on the page — his knuckles going white on the wheel, the questions he didn't ask, the silence that came off that man heavier than concrete. He didn't try to talk me out of it. He'd given up on speeches about a decade before, and he just drove, and he put me out at the curb, and he let me go do the thing I was going to do anyway.

That's the last time I was a whole person to him.

I didn't know it. You never know it. Nobody gets told which handshake is the last one.

---

You have to understand what Josh was to me, because otherwise this chapter is just a story about three hundred dollars.

He was my oldest friend. Not a guy I ran with — an *old* friend, the childhood kind, the kind where there's no origin story because there's no version of your life that starts before him. He built his own house with his own hands. He had the job, the family, the whole steady American thing, and he'd earned every inch of it while I was out lighting mine on fire in five states.

And he never once cut me loose for it. He'd come back to a hotel room and find his oldest friend face-down on the bedspread between an empty bottle and a glowing laptop, and he wouldn't make a speech, he'd step over me and let me sleep, and in the morning we'd laugh about it.

I wrote earlier in this book what that actually was, and I'll say it again because it turns out to be the hinge of everything: Josh had already preemptively mourned me. He'd watched me drown a hundred times and he loved me anyway, and somewhere in there he had quietly finished grieving a man who was still walking around.

Remember that. That's the part that ends up mattering.

---

By the time I got to the point of reaching out to him, I was up in that camp.

I sent him the whole thing. Everything. Where I was, what had happened, what they wanted, what would happen if it didn't come — the entire true account of it, typed out on a laptop that wasn't mine, in a place I couldn't name, off one bar of internet bled out of a yellow house.

And Josh wrote back: *How do I know this is Clay.*

That's the sanest sentence anybody said to me that whole year. Because he was reading a message that had every single marker of the oldest scam on earth. Friend in trouble overseas, can't talk about it, dangerous people, need money fast, don't tell anybody. He'd have been a fool not to ask.

So I got him video. No audio — the connection wouldn't carry both — just my face, in that light, looking like what I looked like by then. And then we got a call through, and he heard my voice, and he believed me.

And then he did the thing that still takes the wind out of me when I think about it. He didn't send advice. He didn't send scripture. He wanted to *come get me.* He wanted the location. He was going to drive down into Baja and pull his friend out of a mountain camp with his own two hands, the same hands he built that house with.

I told him no. I told him the cops down there were bought, that the whole apparatus was theirs, that if he came rolling up that road we'd both end up in it. I've written elsewhere what I said — that the cartel isn't a gang down there, it's the weather.

He didn't like it. He sent the money anyway.

Three hundred dollars.

---

Now here's what I did not know, sitting there feeling like somebody had finally thrown me a rope.

Omar had my accounts.

All of them. Every one. That laptop had my whole life logged into it — my email, my Facebook, the lot — because it was the machine I'd been using back at Rose's house while my own computer ground away at something else, and I had never once logged out of anything, because who does. So when they put that laptop in my lap in a lawn chair in front of a bus, I wasn't just a man with no money. I was a man whose entire identity was already sitting open on a screen in their hands.

I didn't know that at the time. I want to be exact: I did not know, while I was typing the truth to my best friend, that the man standing behind me had the keys to the same house and was already inside it.

And Omar was desperate too. That's a thing I understood much later and much too well. He wasn't the boss. He was a low man with a problem of his own, and the problem was that we owed money and he was the one on the hook for producing it.

So he went into my accounts and he went to work on the people I loved.

He messaged my family. And he messaged Josh. Demanding money. And when the money didn't move fast enough, the messages turned — Josh's family, by name, and what would happen to them.

---

Here's where God or whatever runs the machine decided to have a little fun with me.

It happened at the same time.

Not the same week. Not close enough to sort out later. The same hours. I would be talking to Josh — really me, really talking, telling him what was true — and then right on the backside of that conversation, out of the same account, from the same name on his screen, would come a message threatening his wife and children if he didn't send more.

Omar didn't know I was talking to them. I didn't know he was. Two men working the same keyboard from opposite ends of the earth, neither one aware of the other, and everything landing on Josh's phone in one unbroken stream with my face on it.

---

So let me do the thing I'm actually good at, one more time, and build the case against myself. Because it's not a hard case to build. It's the easiest case I've ever seen.

Here's what it looked like from where Josh was standing.

His oldest friend — the drunk, the one he'd already grieved, the one who'd been face-down in a hundred hotel rooms — disappears into Mexico. Months of nothing. Then a message arrives that reads exactly, word for word, structure for structure, like the most common wire fraud on the internet. Held by a cartel. Can't say where. Send money.

He asks for proof. He gets a video with no audio, of a man who looks like a skeleton, which is precisely what his friend looked like the last several times he saw him sober or otherwise.

He talks himself into believing it. He offers to come. He's told no — don't come, don't call anybody, don't involve the police — which is what a man in a cartel camp would say, and it is *also* exactly what a man running a con would say, and there is no way on this earth to tell those two apart from a couch in Ohio.

He sends three hundred dollars.

And then the threats start coming. From his friend's account. With his friend's name on them. Naming his family.

Now put it together the way he had to. A drunk asks you for a favor. You give him the money. Then the drunk gets drunker and starts sending you threats out of his own account, pretending to be some dangerous somebody, trying to squeeze more out of you — sloppy about it, because he's drunk, sloppy enough to be doing it from his own name.

That's not a crazy conclusion. That's the *sane* conclusion. That's the only conclusion available to a reasonable man with the information he had. If you handed me those facts about somebody else I'd have reached it in about four seconds, and I'd have been proud of how fast I got there.

So Josh has it that I scammed him out of three hundred dollars to fund a drug binge.

And he can't be talked out of it, because every single thing I would say to talk him out of it is a thing a man running that exact con would also say.

---

I never got out on that money. I want that in here, though it changes nothing. It never became a bus ticket, never became a border, never bought me one mile of anything. It got trapped in the machinery like everything else did that year. He thinks I drank it. I never had my hands on it.

But that's not the part that keeps me up.

The part that keeps me up is that this is the one time in my entire adult life that I was telling somebody the flat, whole, unembellished truth with nothing underneath it. No angle. No play. No percentage for me at the end of the sentence. For thirty-five years I had a voice on me that could take a stranger's card number in four minutes, and I used it on everybody, including him, including his hotel rooms, including a hundred mornings we laughed about.

And the first time in my life I ever opened my mouth with nothing but the truth in it — it came out sounding exactly like my best work.

That's the bill. That's what the whole con actually costs, and nobody ever tells you this part when you're twenty-three and good at it. It isn't the money and it isn't the warrants and it isn't the people who stop taking your calls. It's that you spend the currency down to nothing, and then one day something true happens to you — the truest thing that will ever happen to you — and you go to spend it and there's not one cent left in the account.

I earned this. That's the ugly bottom of it. Josh isn't wrong about who I was. He's just wrong about one week, and I'm the reason he can't tell the difference.

---

He doesn't accept my messages anymore.

I've tried. Not constantly — I'm not going to sit here and tell you I've laid siege to the man, because I haven't, and part of me thinks I don't have the standing. But I've tried, and they don't go anywhere, and I don't know what he's got in his head about me now. I can't imagine it. I've tried to imagine it and I always stop, because in his version I'm a man who threatened his kids.

I understand why he feels that way. I need that written down where he might see it someday. **I understand.** I'm not angry at him and I never have been for one second. He did more than anybody. He was the only one who offered to get in a truck.

And I don't have a way to explain it. That's the honest end of this chapter. There's no email I can send. There's no order to put the facts in that makes it land, because the facts are insane — a cartel man in a mountain camp in Baja was logged into my email and neither one of us knew about the other and we were typing to the same man on the same afternoon.

You really can't explain that to anybody. Read it back. It sounds like a story a drunk would build.

My whole life sounds like a story a drunk would build. That's the problem with having actually been one.

Josh — you drove me to the airport. You didn't lecture me, you just drove, and then months later you were the only man on this earth who said *tell me where you are and I'll come get you.* Whatever you believe about me now, you should know that you were the best friend I ever had and that the last thing I ever wanted was for anything with my name on it to come near your family.

It wasn't me. I know that doesn't land. I know exactly how that reads coming from me.

I'm going to say it anyway.
