# Chapter 37 — A Stolen Packet of Hot Sauce
The day bled out into night and so did we. Somewhere in the dark of it I started coaching her up off the floor.

Come on, babe. You can do it. Come up here with me. You can't stay down there — it's better up here, come on. Low and steady, over and over, the way you'd talk to somebody standing on a ledge. And it took her everything she had left, every last thing, but she dragged herself up off that filthy floor and across to me and got onto the bed.

And we held each other. And that's when I cried.

I'd held it through all of it — through the boot, through watching them take her apart, through my own turn in the dirt, through a gun barrel against the back of my skull. I gave them none of it. But the second her body was up against mine in that bed, the whole thing came up out of me and I cried like a baby. We both did. We lay there wrecked and bleeding and held on to each other and sobbed, and somewhere in there it turned into apologies — she was sorry, I was sorry, both of us saying it again and again for everything and nothing.

And then I told her the lies. The good ones. The ones you tell because the truth has no use in a moment like that. *We're going to make it through this. I'll find a way — you know I always find a way. We just have to be strong.* I didn't believe a word of it. Not one word. But I said it like I did, because it was the only thing left in the world I could hand her, and a man gives what he's got.

---

Here's the thing, though. Lying there, the cold part of my brain that never fully shuts off started working the problem, and it landed somewhere that was almost worse than the killing.

If they'd wanted us dead, we'd be dead. They had every reason and every opportunity, and they didn't take it. Which meant the beating wasn't an ending. It was a correction. We weren't being executed — we were being *kept.* And the only reason men like that keep you is because you're still worth something to them, and the only thing I had ever been worth to anybody up there was money.

They'd just pulled five hundred dollars off my body at a bank. I'd later find out they were watching us the whole time.  The guy withdrwaing his money next to us was them.  They were just waiting for us.  They knew it was coming.  They had paitence.  They aren't animals.  They are smart. Easy money, money that had appeared out of nowhere the second they squeezed. So why on earth would they stop? Why not run the gringo a while and see how much more comes out of him before they're done? Maybe they'd start in on my family directly now — and there it was, the real fear, settling in cold: they couldn't get a peso out of Glenda's family, everybody knew that, but mine? Mine had houses. Mine had jobs. Mine, far as a cartel could tell from the outside, was a well worth working. And little did I know, Glenda would give them all my passwords and emails she knew.

And then the worst of it, the thought I could barely let myself finish. Glenda. A beautiful woman who now, in every way that mattered up on that mountain, belonged to the cartel. I knew enough to know how that story tends to go, and I knew enough not to say a word of it out loud. I just held her tighter and kept telling her the lies.

I'd find out, in time, that the cold part of my brain had it just about right.

---

Eventually our own bodies took the decision away from us, and we fell asleep — if you can call it that. One of us came up out of it about every hour, every single hour, a raw *owwwwe* dragging out of whoever moved first as we tried to find some new angle that hurt a little less than the last one. There wasn't one. We just rotated through the kinds of pain until the sky started going gray behind the black paint on the windows.

Nobody came in that night. Nobody came the next.

When we finally woke for real, there was something just inside the door that hadn't been there before. A five-gallon jug of clean water. A bag of rice. A bag of black beans. Two bowls, a mixing spoon, a couple of sporks.

I looked at it a long time, and I understood what I was looking at. Not a kindness. An accounting. You feed an animal you intend to keep.

I might as well get used to it, I thought.

Because for the next five months, that was all I would eat.

---

It takes about a week for bodies as old and used-up as mine and hers to quit screaming after a beating like that. The body heals slow. But it does heal — that's the deal it makes with you. The mind never signs the same contract. To this day I'll come straight up out of sleep in a full-blown panic, heart going, having lived one more time through some piece of that mountain I haven't even gotten to in this book yet.

That first week, they didn't ask us to do a single thing but mend. Every day the door would open and Omar would set down a fresh bag of rice and beans and not say one word. He'd run his eyes over the two of us, quick, the way you check on livestock, and we'd look back at him and say nothing, and he'd leave. I can't fully put into words how bad that was — the not knowing. No idea what the plan was. And worse than that, for the first time in my whole scheming life, I couldn't *make* one. I had finally run clean out of plans. There was nothing to angle, nothing to work, no door to talk my way through. Just the four black-painted walls of that bus and a radio playing somewhere outside it, Spanish, day and night, and the two of us waiting to find out what they wanted from us first.

I told you the two days I did in that Alaska jail were the longest of my life. I'd have given my right arm to be back in that cell. I'd have taken the same bunkmate, the serial masturbator and all, and I'd have handed the man his napkin with a smile on my face. That's where I was.

---

After a week we'd come back around to looking like human beings, and the pain had dropped down to something a person could carry. So now we worked.

Glenda had to learn it first, and then teach it to me in English — the boss showing her, her turning around and showing the stupid gringo. We got our inventory: fifty bags of meth, thirty of weed, ten of coke. We were to put none of it into ourselves, not a grain — and now the liquor was gone too, off the list, the one free-for-all up there shut down for us. But that day, that one day, they handed us a single pack of cheap cigarettes to share between us. The label was in English. *Smoking Gun,* it said, with a little picture of two pistols crossed over each other into an X. Full-flavored and harsh enough to take the top of your head off, and we took them grateful — as grateful as a person can be for anything. One book of matches to go with them.

We'd work the first week side by side, so she could walk the gringo through it. And the work ran like clockwork, because everything up there ran like clockwork.

You picked your product up at the start of every shift, six in the morning. You got it from the big yellow house — the upstairs door, the one set right below the rooftop where the men paced back and forth with their guns. You counted it out right there in front of them, on camera. At the end of the shift, eight at night, you brought back whatever you hadn't sold along with every peso it had earned. And God help you if the numbers didn't line up. I never learned what they did to a man who came up short, and I never wanted to learn, because I made sure as long as I breathed that it was never going to be me.

When it all added up — and it had better add up — you got paid in the only currency that mattered up there. A bag of rice every day. A bag of beans every other. A daily pack of cigarettes. A pack of cheap toilet paper every three or four days. A bar of soap, upon request. We wern't allowed razors.  Once a week Omar would bring by a disposable one and watch as we would share it and then take it and dispose of it. You filled your five gallons of water on Mondays, Thursdays, and Saturdays, and that same five gallons was everything: drinking, and flushing, and washing. So you learned to ration it down to nothing. You only flushed for the brown stuff. You bathed with it too — there was no shower, just a patch of floor with a drain in it next to the toilet, and you'd stand on it and make a few cups of water do the whole job. A fresh towel and washcloth every two days. A toothbrush once a month with a little tube of paste you had to nurse like it was medicine. No salt for the food, no pepper, nothing. And every so often Glenda would turn up a packet or two of hot sauce from God knows where, and on those days it was like Thanksgiving in that bus. I am not exaggerating that even a little. A stolen packet of hot sauce over rice and beans was, for five months, the finest thing life had to offer me.
