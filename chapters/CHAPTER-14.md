I had waited three years for this one night and built it up in my head into something the size of a cathedral, so let me tell you right at the top how it actually went, because the daylight between the two is about the realest thing I've got to hand you in this entire book. It was a clumsy, fumbling, half-forgettable mess. And it was the best night of my life. Both of those are true, and they are true in that order, and if you have ever once loved a person past the point of performing for them, you already know exactly how the two of them fit inside the same night.

---

It came in the second week, and it came on the back of a clean cup.

That was the gate I'd been waiting on — the next check — and this time it came back the way I needed it to, the weed finally gone out of me, nothing left in that cup but the proof the cage had done the job it does whether you ask it to or not. On the strength of it they let me off the men's side. Not into the Airbnb I'd been picturing — into something I hadn't even had the sense to want. A block down from the rehab the place kept a little apartment for exactly this, a conjugal setup, a couple of plain rooms with a door that locked from the inside and not one living soul on the far side of it. Ours. For the night. The first true privacy the two of us had ever had in three years of loving each other under the little watching light of a webcam.

I want you to hold that up next to the whole history of us. Three years, and every intimate minute of it had happened through glass — me on one end and her on the other and two thousand miles of desert and a border full of guns in between, the screen always there, the screen I told you was the safety, the door I could shut any time the wanting got too real. And now somebody had gone and handed me a key to a room with no screen in it. Just her, and me, and a lock on the inside, and nowhere left on earth to hide.

---

She cooked.

That was the first thing, and I know how small it reads written down, but I need you to understand it just about broke me open right there in the doorway. She made me a meal — chicken,noodles, vegetables, peppers in a cream sauce.  It was delicious and the first time I found out Glenda could cook and cook amazingly.  She was a woman moving around a borrowed little community kitchen below our borrowed apartment, making her man some dinner — and I stood there and watched her do it like it was the most exotic act I had ever witnessed in my life. Because it was. Three years, and I had never once gotten to watch this woman simply *move around a room.* Never seen her reach for a thing on a high shelf. Never seen what her face did when it wasn't pointed straight into a camera for my benefit. I watched her cook, and I felt like a man who'd loved a painting his whole life and finally walked into the museum to find out it could turn its head.

We propped my laptop up on something and put a movie on, and I could not tell you a single frame of it, because we never watched a second of it. We lay down together to watch and instead we just — lay there. Curled up, the both of us, careful, the way you're careful with a thing you've wanted so long you're half afraid it'll come apart in your hands. And slowly, clumsily, we got into it.

And here is what made that night so strange and so hard, the thing I keep circling back around to: it was sober. Stone sober, the both of us, for the first time in the entire history of the thing. You have to understand what that meant for two people like us. For three years, every single time we had ever reached for each other, there'd been a bottle in it — a warm loose slur on both ends greasing the whole machine, the liquor doing the part of the talking neither one of us had the nerve to do straight. Take the drink away and take the screen away in the very same night, and what you are left holding is two people who had loved each other for three years standing in a room dead sober with absolutely nothing left to hide behind. Just the flesh. And the flesh doesn't lie. It can't. It never learned how.

---

So I'll be honest with you about the flesh, the way I've tried to be honest about everything else in here, even the parts that don't flatter me. Especially those.

It was bad.

Not bad in any way that mattered in the end — but bad in just about every way a first time can be bad when you've built it into a cathedral and then turned up sober to find out it's only a room. I hadn't been with a woman in better than a year and a half. There'd been a girl, once or twice, back during one of the breaks Glenda and I took in those years — the kind of break two drunks take when they've torn a hole in one another they can't quite see past — but that was a long time gone, and to say I was out of practice does a disservice to men who are merely out of practice. It was clunky. It was awkward. It was the two of us fumbling at a thing we'd done a thousand times through a screen and not one time ever in the same square foot of air. And if you want the whole truth, it was forgettable, on both our ends, and we both knew it, and neither of us said one word about it. Not that night. We didn't get around to admitting it out loud to each other until much, much later, a long way down the road in us — and by then we could lie there together and laugh about it, the great long-awaited first time, the one that wasn't worth the film it never got shot on. It would get better. Lord, would it get better. But that first night was nobody's highlight reel, and I'm not going to sit here and sell you one.

And none of that — not one bit of it — is what I remember about the night, or why I told you it was the best one of my life.

What I remember is the after. The fumbling done and let go of and already forgiven, the light off, the two of us folding up into each other in the dark of that little borrowed room. Her head on me. My arms around an actual warm body that for three years had been nothing in this world but light on a screen I could switch off. We fell asleep like that, holding on to one another — and *that,* right there, not a single thing about the rest of it, was the whole entire reason I had crossed a border and sold off a life and gotten myself stripped naked in a detox to arrive at. Just to fall asleep with my arms around her. That is all it ever was, the whole time, underneath every loud thing I built on top of it. And we got it. For one night — clumsy and sober and perfect in the only way that ever counted for anything — we got it. I would not trade that night for any of the smooth ones that came after.

---

And then I woke up dying.

I'll keep some proportion on it, so: I did not, in the literal event, die. But I would like it entered into the official record that on the morning after the most romantic night of my entire life, the love of that life damn near killed me with a bowl of noodles.

It came on hard and it came on from both ends at once — the kind of sick that wrings a man out and then keeps reaching down for more that isn't there to give. Puking, and worse than puking, and puking again, hour on hour of it, my buck-and-a-half of a body trying its level best to turn itself fully inside out in a bathroom about the size of a phone booth. It took me a good while, in the gaps between rounds, to piece together what had even happened to me — and when I did, it was almost funny. Almost. The noodles. She had boiled them in the tap water. The very tap water that every sign and every gringo and every guidebook on God's green earth will tell a man like me he must not, under any circumstance available to him, so much as let touch his lips — and it had not crossed her mind for half a second to think twice about it, because she had been boiling that same tap water her whole life long and her stomach had made its peace with the place back before she could walk. Hers was fine. Mine was a tourist's gut sat down in a local's kitchen, and it folded like a cheap tent in a wind.

Two days. Two full days it laid me flat out. And I have turned it over a lot of times in the years since, the exact shape of it, because you genuinely could not write it any better than it wrote itself: the first home-cooked meal that woman ever made me — made with her own two hands, in our first private room, on the back of the first night we ever once spent inside each other's arms — and it just about put me in the Mexican ground. The first meal Glenda ever cooked for me almost killed me. It is the truest little love story I know, that one. It only took me a couple of days bent double over a toilet to recognize it for what it was.
