# Two Worlds

I got voted prom king and I didn't tell my family.

Not out of modesty. I want to be clear about that, because it sounds like modesty and it was the exact opposite. I didn't tell them because I could not think of a way to walk in that door and say a thing like that out loud with my face doing whatever my face would have done.

So I just didn't. I came home and went upstairs.

Brandy found out first. She called me — I can still hear the pitch of her voice, surprised as hell, that rising thing a person does when the information doesn't fit in the slot they've got for you. And then it went through the house the way things go through a house, and my father was at the bottom of the stairs hollering up them.

You got prom king?

And then, right behind it, the part that actually landed:

You didn't tell me.

And I did what I did. I came out on the landing and I shrugged. Ah. It's not a big deal.

It was the biggest thing that had ever happened to me.

I don't expect that to make sense to anybody who was somebody in high school. I was not somebody. I was a dork and a nobody straight through my sophomore year — small, scared, working out the hallways, running the route that kept me clear of the guys two grades up. And I went home from that to a house with Jimmy in it and two beautiful girls at the table, which is its own kind of daily arithmetic about where a boy ranks.

Then somewhere in my junior year the thing turned over. I came into myself. And the whole school apparently took a vote and told me out loud, in front of everybody, that I was not what I had privately known myself to be for sixteen years.

And I stood on a landing and shrugged at my father like somebody had handed me a coupon.

Too cool to let it show. That's what I'd have told you at the time. That's what I *did* tell myself, and I got away with it, because nobody in that house was ever going to press me on it.

---

Here's what actually happened in that junior year, and it isn't a growth spurt.

I found out I was funny.

I'd always had the wit. It was in me early — that's not the part I discovered. What I discovered was that it *worked.* That you could take a room of people who had no particular use for you and do about ninety seconds of material and come out the other side with the whole thing in your hand.

I became the class clown. Not a class clown — *the* one. I made everybody laugh and I did it constantly, all day, every period, the way a man works a job he's good at. And I was good at it. I was the funniest guy in any room I walked into, and I knew it, and I want to be honest and say that knowing it was the first time in my life I ever felt like I had a floor under me.

Here's what I didn't understand until about four decades later.

I was doing my father.

The funniest man in any room he ever stood in was my dad. That was his whole operating system. He could take a table of strangers and own it inside of five minutes, and I grew up watching him do it, and when I finally found something in myself that made people look at me the way they looked at him, I ran with it and never asked one question about where it came from or what it was for.

It's the same voice. That's the thing that turns my stomach a little now. The greased-up thing the medicine held in front of me on that mat in Sinaloa, the telemarketing voice, the one that could take a stranger's credit card off them in four minutes — that is not a separate machine. That's this. That's the sixteen-year-old finding out he can work a room, wired straight through to a man on a phone in Las Vegas taking money off people who couldn't afford to lose it. Same tool. I just found out later what else it could open.

---

But none of that came home.

That's the part I've never been able to explain to anybody, and it's the actual subject of this chapter.

At school I was the funniest guy alive. At home I was a quiet kid on a landing shrugging at his father.

Not a *different mood.* A different person. I built two of them and I ran them both, and there was no door between the rooms. The one at school was loud and quick and entirely at ease and had a hundred people who'd tell you he was a good time. The one at home said as little as he could get away with, kept his face flat, moved through the house on the routes that didn't cost anything, and gave up nothing — not a win, not a fear, not a prom king.

And that is precisely why the trouble never made sense to them.

Because the letters came home. They always came home. Phone calls, notes, that specific car silence that means somebody's already been called and you don't know it yet. And my parents would sit there holding a piece of paper about a kid who ran his mouth all day and disrupted every class he was in, and then they'd look up at the quiet boy standing in their kitchen who barely said two words at supper, and they could not make the two of them into one person.

They'd ask me what happened. And what was I supposed to say? *That isn't me. That's the other one.*

I let them stay confused. For years. It was easier than the alternative, which would have been to be one guy in both places, and I did not have one guy in me. I had two, and I was the only person on earth who'd met both.

---

There was exactly one door between those two rooms, and I found it early, and I've been paying for it my whole life.

When my father and I drank, the school one came out.

That's it. That's the whole mechanism. Put a drink in me at that table and the quiet kid was gone and the funny one showed up, at home, in front of my dad, and the two of us would go — trading, riffing, the timing lining up the way it only does between two people built off the same blueprint. He'd get going and I'd get going and for a couple of hours I was the same guy in both worlds.

It was the best I ever felt in that house.

I want to sit in that a second, because I've written a whole book about a bottle and I have never once put this part down. Everybody wants the drinking to be about pain. Pain is the marketable story, and I've already said in these pages what I think of it — the drink stands on nothing but itself. But if you want to know what it *gave* me, at fifteen, sixteen, before it ever cost me one dollar or one person, here it is:

Alcohol was the only thing I ever found that made me one person.

Not happy. Not numb. One. The two of them collapsed into a single man for a couple of hours, and that man could sit at a table with his father and be entirely, unguardedly himself, and that had never happened before in my life and it has not happened sober many times since.

That's a hell of a thing to hand a kid who's been running two selves since he was five. And nobody handed it to me at a party or in a parking lot with the guys two grades up. I got it at my own kitchen table, from the funniest man I ever knew, who was also the man I was trying to be.

---

I stayed two people. I'd like to tell you the prom king vote fixed something, and it didn't. I shrugged on that landing, and then I took both of those men out into the world with me, and they ran side by side for the next thirty-five years.

The loud one got the jobs. The loud one got the girls and the floors and the phone and the rooms — every good thing I ever came into, the loud one went and got.

The quiet one is the one who did all the leaving.

And the only place they were ever the same man was in a glass, which is the joke this entire book is built on, and it took me until a concrete floor in Mexico to hear it.
