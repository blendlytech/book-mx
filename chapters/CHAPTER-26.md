# Chapter 26 — The Empty Room

The fifth and last felt like a funeral. We went in knowing there was nothing left to give — we'd vomited till we bled, wept till we ran dry, faced the worst of ourselves and the worst of our ghosts. I drank the brew and my body didn't even fight it. I lay down and waited.

And nothing came.

No fractals. No Alaskan snow. No Vegas floor. No nausea, no purge, no weeping. Just an empty, quiet room. I lay there for hours listening to Mateo sing the last *icaros*, and what I felt wasn't joy and wasn't peace — it was the absence of weight. A house gutted by fire: walls scorched, furniture gone, roof open to the sky, and the foundation finally, mercifully bare.

We'd been scoured clean. Both of us. We had walked through the fire and let it burn off the years of trauma and denial we'd built, and we had come out the other side.

When the sun rose on the last morning we packed our bags. We hugged Mateo, who looked at us out of those ancient ocean-deep eyes and nodded once, as if to say *the work is done.* We said goodbye to the uncles, climbed in the car, and started the long drive north toward the border.

## The Cruel Joke

Here is what nobody warns you about the medicine. The truest, most brutal thing it ever taught me, and it taught it sideways and entirely at my own expense.

It worked. It worked on damn near everything — except the one thing we'd asked it for.

We came out of those two weeks hollowed out, grieved-empty, lighter in the soul than either of us had been since we were children. We'd faced the very bottom of our own darkness. We'd laid the ghosts down.

And as the tires ate up the miles north, a cold thing turned over in me. I was not one single degree less thirsty for a drink.

It had cracked the floor of me wide open and shown me the oldest broken things lying down there in the dark. It had walked me up to the ghost of a five-year-old boy and made me carry a decade of grief over my daughter out into the light. And it had not laid so much as a finger on the bottle.

We'd been to the very bottom of ourselves — dug down to the exact place all the experts and the soft-eyed counselors and the rehab brochures swear the drinking comes *from* — and we'd found the bottom didn't have the drinking in it at all.

Because that's the lie. The comfortable, marketable lie everybody wants to be true: that a man drinks *because of* something, that the addiction is just the smoke off some buried fire, and reach the fire, heal it, and the drinking lifts off you like morning fog. I believed it a little, going in. I don't believe it now.

You can drag the crushed boy and the lost daughter and the telemarketing lies and the bruises and the fists and the fatherless child all up out of the dark. You can face every one of them, weep over them, lay them to rest one at a time until your soul is as clean as a scrubbed floor.

And the wanting will still be there in the morning. Clean, patient, entirely its own animal, sitting on the edge of the bed, asking the same thing it always asks.

The grief was real. The trauma was down there. The pain was true. But the grief was never what the drink was standing on. The drink stands on nothing but itself.

So we grieved. And we healed something neither of us had been able to touch in ten years. And then January ran out, and the holidays were over, and it was time to start the long way back — back toward Peñasco, back across that toll road I prayed we'd find this time, back toward a line on the map where a man is allowed to walk into a store, buy a bottle, and drink it in the open.

And the plain, terrible truth of it was this: the second we crossed that line, we both knew we were going to drink.
