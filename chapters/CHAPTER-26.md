# Chapter 26 — Pictures Without Captions

My mother is a beautiful person.

She has a sense of humor that will take your legs out from under you. She'd get going about somebody — some observation, dead accurate and not particularly kind. I've had a hard life and most of it was my own doing, and there is still a pile of afternoons in that kitchen I would not trade for anything I ever got out of a casino.

She has a sense of pride too, and she earns it. Dinner was on that table every night of my childhood and a lot of my adulthood. The house was clean and it stayed clean and everybody living under that roof got taken care of whether they'd done a thing that week to deserve it or not. She doesn't make speeches. She feeds you and she keeps a good roof over you, and if you can't read that as love then that's a failure of your reading and not of hers.

And she kept that door open for me for four decades.

Every time the floor went out from under me — and the floor has gone out from under me more times than fits in one book — there was a house in it. Broke, jobless, run off, run out, coming back off a thing I couldn't explain to anybody. She never once made me stand there and make a case for myself. She'd just start figuring out where to put me.

She also tried to get me well. More than once, and earlier than anybody else did. And I refused her flat every single time.

---

Here is what she was handed.

She was handed a bill nobody on this earth should have to pay.

Her brother's boy. Dudley had exactly one, and the garage door came down on him, and my mother was standing in the middle of that family when it happened.

And in the same year — I still don't know the order and there's nobody left who'd give me a straight answer — the divorce of her and my father, and she was left in a house with four children in it and nobody to hand a single one of them to.

Two things. One year. And there is no line down the middle of a woman where you get to sort out which piece did which damage.

---

She was younger then than I am now.

Considerably younger. I'm forty-six years old and I'm writing this from a plastic chair in a courtyard with a fence around it because I could not run my own life. I have no children in my care. No house. Nobody on this earth depending on me for a single thing. And she was somewhere in her early thirties, burying a nephew, watching a marriage go down, four kids in the house and all of them hers.

I cannot do it. I've tried. I sit here and try to put myself at that age with that load and my mind just refuses the problem, the way it refuses to picture the number of stars.

---

Then a couple of men tried to step up to the plate, walking into a household with four kids in it.

A boy learns to read a man fast under those conditions. Is this one going to stick. Do I have to learn this one. What does he want from the room, and what does he want from me.

I have never once stopped doing it. Every room I have walked into since I was five years old I have worked — read it, played it, pitched it, watched the door. That reflex made me a living on a telephone for twenty years and it has cost me everything I ever had, and it got installed in a living room in Ohio while I was still small enough to be underfoot.

---

I was a thief.

Not a kid who took a candy bar. A thief. I took anything in that house that wasn't nailed down, constantly, from everybody, and I was good at it early. 

I did it out of spite.

I did it because I had already decided I was bad. That was settled, in my own mind, by about seven years old — that I was the bad one, the one who broke things, the one they said the thing about — and once a boy has signed off on that there's a strange freedom in it. Nothing left to protect. No reputation to keep. You've already got the verdict, so you may as well earn the sentence.

And underneath the spite, the plainer thing: I did not feel cared about. I'm not going to stand here and argue that I *wasn't.* I've written this whole chapter to say she was doing a job no person should have been asked to do. But five-year-olds don't grade on a curve. There was a woman in that house carrying a thing that would have put most people on the floor, and four children watching her carry it, and the youngest one and the only boy came out of it certain that nobody in the building had him.

So I stole. And I raised hell. And I got worse every year.

She didn't know how to handle me.

I've watched people read that sentence as an accusation, in my own head, for years — and it isn't one. Who would? Who on this earth would have known how to handle me? I have since been handled by police departments in three states, a Nevada jail, an Alaskan jail, a Mexican cartel, and two rehabs, and not one of them managed it either. She was thirty-something and worn down to the bone, with three girls who were fine and one boy who was a professional problem, and she did not have the tools — and neither has one single person who's tried since.

---

Which brings us to twelve.

I could not go live with my father until I was twelve years old. That was the rule. Whatever the arrangement was that came out of that divorce, it had an age on it, and the age was twelve.

So I waited.

A boy in a house like that, who has already decided he's the bad one, who has a father across town and a grandmother's kitchen every other weekend where nobody makes him work the room — that boy knows exactly how old he has to be, and he counts.

And she was counting too.

I'm not reading that into the past from here. It was in the house. It was the temperature of the place. There was a date coming when the hardest thing in her life became somebody else's to carry, and both of us knew the date, and neither one of us ever said one word about it out loud.

That's the whole family in one picture, if you want it. Two people in the same house counting to the same number for opposite reasons and never once mentioning it at dinner.

At twelve the vehicle came on a schedule, and we both had it circled.

Then I went, and Ginger became my step-mother for the next six years, and I have already written what that woman did and what it was worth.

---

But before I got to twelve, Ben showed up.

I think I was ten. I couldn't swear to it and there's nobody I'm going to call to check.

Ben was one of the men coming through, and then he wasn't one of the men coming through anymore, because Ben stayed. He's the one that stuck, and he's one of my favorite people who has ever lived, and I need to do this part right because I've owed it for thirty-six years.

He worked a steel mill. Twelve-hour swing shifts, which if you've never been around it means your body never once gets to learn what time it is — days for a stretch and then nights for a stretch, on and on, for decades, in a mill. That job damn near killed him and he went and did it, and then he came home and did whatever needed doing, and he kept the smile on.

He built her a house with his own two hands.

He is the kindest man you will ever meet. He's not warm the way people mean when they say warm — he's not going to make a speech at you or ask how you're feeling. He's just *there.* Permanently. At the same temperature, doing the thing that needs doing.

Years later, when I was grown and back living under his roof — because of course I was — he'd come through and ask me what games I had going. What I liked that night. How the car lot had gone.

Sit with that a second, because it took me until this chair to see it.

A man who worked twelve-hour swings in a steel mill for a living, coming home to find his wife's grown son on the couch with a phone and a card, and what he asks is *who do you like tonight.* No lecture. No look. He just took an interest in the only thing I actually cared about, in the only language I was speaking at the time, and asked about my day like a man with a real job.

Nobody else in my life ever did that. Everybody else met me at the door with the verdict.

---

And that roof of his is the other thing I owe an honest paragraph.

I lived in that house as a grown man. Not once. Over and over, for years — years past when a man ought to have to knock on his mother's door, past the age where there's any cute way to say it. A man in his thirties in a room in his mother's house.

There was never any fuss about it. Not one time did I have to stand at that door and sell it, and I can sell anything. She just opened it and started figuring out where to put me, and then dinner was on the table at the same hour it had been on the table when I was six years old.

What she would not do is pretend.

My mother does not put up with bullshit. She has never in her life hidden it when she was disappointed in me, and she has never held her tongue about me either. You always knew exactly where you stood in that house, which as a grown drunk is not the thing you're shopping for. You'd come through the door and she had already done the arithmetic on you, and she was going to say the number out loud.

I used to take offense at that. Took it as an attack, for about thirty years.

She was right. Every time. There is not one thing that woman ever said to me or anyone else about myself that the following ten years didn't prove out to the decimal.

And she wasn't trying to win. That's the part I could never see with a drink in my hand. She wasn't scoring on me. She only ever wanted me to see it — to look one time at the same thing she was looking at, with my own eyes. And there comes a point where you have said the true thing to somebody four hundred times and they have looked straight through you four hundred times, and there is nothing left to do but throw your hands up in the air.

She threw her hands up. She'd have been a fool not to.

And then she'd set a plate down in front of me anyway.

---

She's also the one who said the word out loud.

Nobody else did. My father was a functional alcoholic and it was never going to come from him. My sisters had their own lives and their own way of handling me, which was mostly to handle the wreckage and not the man. My mother said it. To my face, more than once, and in front of people, and years before there was any Mexico in this story — back when it still looked to some like a guy who liked to have a good time.

She was more worried about my drinking, out loud, than any other living person.

And I filed it as betrayal. I kept an actual ledger on it in my head: she's the one who won't let it go, she's the one telling people.

It wasn't betrayal.

It was the only accurate thing anybody in my family said about me for twenty years, and she said it on purpose, out loud, in the open, knowing exactly what it was going to cost her with me — because she was more afraid of me dying than she was of me being angry with her.

That is not a betrayal. That is the opposite of one. That's the only person in the building willing to pay a price to tell you the truth. I made her pay it every single time, and she went ahead and said it again the next year.

---

It wasn't the house.

I've said that in this book and I meant it and I still mean it. I've got three sisters who came up in the same wreckage and not one of them caught it. Carrie did her arithmetic every day for thirty years. Brandy struck out for two cities alone and built the actual grown-up life. Niki has spent forty years being the phone call.

Same house. Same year. Same everything.

It's me. It has always been me. And I have never once needed a better excuse than the ones I was already handed, which is exactly why I'm not going to pick one up now that I've finally got a good one lying here in my hand.

---

There is no wound in me with her name carved in it.

I want that stated plain, because I know how a chapter like this reads. I've just handed you the worst year of a young woman's life and then a boy who stole everything in her house, and I know what a person does with that. Goes looking for the crack. Tries to find the place where a mother made a drunk.

It isn't there. I have looked for it longer and harder than anybody alive, and there were thirty years in there where I badly wanted to find it, and it is not there and it never was.

She has been exactly where she has always been, this whole time. In a clean house, with dinner going on at the same hour it has always gone on, waiting to see whether the phone rings.

---

Ben, if you ever read this — and you might.

You were kind to me on the days I had not done one single thing on this earth to earn it, which is the only kind of kindness that actually counts, and it is the rarest thing I have ever been around.

I know what those shifts cost you. I know what that house cost you to build. I understand now — at forty-six, in a rehab, in a country I'm not supposed to be in — exactly what kind of sacrifice that was, and exactly how little any of us ever handed back.

I love you. I never got to say it. I'm saying it now, out of order and late and in print, which is how everything in my life has ever gotten said.

You didn't have to take any of us on. You did it anyway, and then you built the roof over it with your own two hands.

---

And Mom.

I'm going to say this plain, the way you've said everything to me my whole life, because you'd see through it any other way.

You fed me every night I ever lived under your roof, and I lived under it years longer than a grown man should have had to, and you never once made me ask twice. You never made me stand at that door and explain myself.

You told me the truth about myself when it cost you something to say it. I made sure it cost you every time, and you went ahead and said it again the next year. You were right about all of it, and you were right early, and I want it on the record: I knew you were right a long time before I ever gave you the satisfaction of admitting it.

You were handed a thing in your early thirties that would have flattened most people I have ever met, and you did not put it down, and you did not put us down either. I can't do that math. I've been trying for a year in a plastic chair with nothing but time and my head still won't hold it.

And with every bit of that in the ledger, both directions, the thing that keeps coming back to me in here isn't any of it. It's the laughter and the love. There were whole years in my life where I did not have one other thing to laugh about, and I still had that, but you would still laugh with me.

I have spent my entire life leaving. Alaska, Vegas, Michigan, Ohio, Mexico, one door after another, and I have never once been able to sit still in a place that was good to me.

I never had to earn my way back into your house. You were there and the door was open.

I love you, Mom. I'm sorry it took a fence around a courtyard to get it written down. But it's written down now, and it's in print, and it will outlast the both of us.
