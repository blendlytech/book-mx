# Chapter 50 — About the Chair
I told you at the start I was telling you this from a chair, a free man.

I want to be straight with you about the chair.

It's a plastic one, and it's in a courtyard in Mexicali, and there's a fence around the courtyard. My out date was in October, but I'm out. It is the first of August as I write this and I turn forty-seven in eight days, and I'll be turning it in in a office building I rented, in a t-shirt that isn't mine, in a country I still have no legal right to be standing in.

So. Free man. You can decide what that's worth. I've decided what it's worth to me, and the answer is: more than anything else I've ever had, and I'm not going to apologize for the word.

I said I can walk out that gate. Nobody would stop me. And they didn't. That's the entire difference between that fence and the last one, and it turns out that's the whole difference there is. Nobody on this earth was making me sit in that chair. I signed a piece of paper saying I'd sit in it for six months and I was sitting in it, and some days that is the only accomplishment I can put my hand on.

---

And I can still reach down and touch it.

I did just now, before I wrote that. Right hand, down past the ankle bone, and there they are — faint, gone shiny and white the way old scars go. You'd have to know to look. Somebody at a beach would never see them.

They never really faded. I told you that on the first page and it's still true four hundred pages later, and it's the only piece of that mountain I can hold in my hand.

Here's the part I didn't know when I wrote that.

I go looking for them. Not once. Regularly. I'd be sitting there in that chair and my hand will just go down there on its own and find them, the way your tongue goes to a chipped tooth, and I'll run my thumb across those four marks and check that they're still there.

I've thought a long time about why a man would do that, and I've got an answer I don't love. It's because they're proof. Everything else that happened to me up on that hill exists in exactly one place, and that place is the inside of my own head, which is the least reliable structure I have ever been issued. I've got no photographs. No police report. No newspaper clipping. Not one document anywhere on this earth says I was ever up there. My best friend of thirty years believes I made the whole thing up to shake three hundred dollars out of him, and I have already explained to you why that is the sane conclusion.

So I reach down and count them. Because it happened, and here's the proof, and I need it about twice a day.

---

Let me give you the honest inventory. No pitch in it. I've been practicing.

I have no passport. The application came back denied over a date I wrote down wrong, and I never resubmitted it, and I'd have to start it over from the bottom. I have no Social Security card. I have two warrants — a DUI in Ohio and a protection-order violation out of Vegas — and both of them are exactly as active as they were the day I got on that plane, and every plan I've got for walking back into my own country runs directly through a cage.

I don't have a dollar. I don't have a laptop that's mine. I don't have a phone number that belongs to me.

And after two years and a half in this country — after a border, a beach, a thousand miles to Sinaloa, a mountain, and three days on foot through a desert — I still do not speak Spanish.

It is the single most damning fact in this entire book and it isn't close. I have been mute in this country by choice since the day I arrived. I sat at a table of thirty people and let an eleven-year-old girl translate a room for me. I sat in a car while two women negotiated my freedom eighteen inches from my face and I could not contribute one word to the only negotiation of my life that ever actually mattered. I spent five months owned by men whose language I could not be bothered to learn, and every single beating I took up there arrived in a language I'd had two years and every reason on earth to learn and had not.

The counselor was real. Omar kept his word about that, the way he's kept his word about everything, which is a thing I still haven't gotten used to and I've been trying.

He speaks English and he saw me twice a week and I want to tell you what happened the first time, because it's the only thing that happened in that building that I'd call a turning point, and it lasted about four seconds.

He asked me a question. I don't remember which one — something ordinary, something out of the front of the folder.

And I answered it, and I heard myself answer it.

That voice. The greased one. The one the medicine held up in front of me in a concrete room in Sinaloa and made me listen to for what felt like a lifetime — the one built in a driveway fighting my sister at seven years old and monetized at thirty on a beige carpet under fluorescent light in Las Vegas. It came out of my mouth in a folding chair in front of a man whose entire job was to help me, and it was smooth, and it was warm, and it was working, and I could hear it doing it.

I was pitching him. On me. On how well I was doing.

I stopped in the middle of a sentence. He waited. He's good at waiting — that's about ninety percent of what the good ones do, and I'd only ever met one other person in my life who knew how, and she was sitting on a tile floor in Sinaloa with a plate of cold rice she wasn't going to eat.

And I said: I'm doing the thing. Right now. I'm doing it at you.

He said: I know. Keep going, and tell me when it starts again.

We've been doing that for months. That's the whole treatment as far as I can tell. Some man says a true thing, and then the machine takes over and starts selling, and somebody who isn't fooled says *there it is,* and you go back and try it again without the voice on.

I'm not good at it. I'd say I'm about one sentence in four. But a year ago I was zero in four and didn't know there was a count.

Glenda's on the women's side. Twenty yards and a wall.

I see her. Not the way you'd think. There's a schedule for it and there are rules and there is always somebody in the room, which is, I have come to believe, the single best thing that could have happened to the two of us.

Because here is what we are, and I've had a few months of counseling and a plastic chair to work it out. We are two people who between us cannot manage one functional adult, and who are, separately, each doing all right. She's doing the work. I'm doing the work. Every hour we've ever spent unsupervised in the same room in two and a half years has ended with a drink in it, and I include the good ones. Especially the good ones

The cat is in Rosarito

Glenda got a message to the feed couple — the same two who put her under a tarp in the bed of a truck and drove her off that mountain. She asked them, next time they run the feed in, to look for a little gray thing that won't let anybody but them touch her and to put her in the truck.

They did it.  They actually did it.

I don't have the money to go get her.  I reached out to my father to help in a weird way.  Which is a whole 'nother story.

We will see.  I'll give it a day.  I owe her more, but if that doesn't happen.  I'm gone.  I'm done. That's a sick man typing.  A man who has had enough and stopped caring.  I really need this.  I do.  To start caring again.  A cat.  That's a sick man.

That's the one I'd trade the rest for. Not the passport, not the warrants, not my name cleared with Josh. The cat. I'd sign for all the rest of it exactly as it is if somebody walked through that gate with her.

---

So here's where we are, you and me.

I promised you on the first page that you'd want to know how a man ends up where I ended up, and I told you the honest answer was one bad left turn at a time. I've spent this whole book showing you the turns and I'd like to correct the record slightly, now that I've had to lay them all out end to end.

It wasn't the turns. Anybody can take a wrong turn. Josh took a few. Troy took a few. My father took a few. The only thing unique about my story is that I kept getting in a vehicle.

It's that every single time I took one, I got in a vehicle.

Five years old and my cousin was dead and they put me in a car and took me to my grandmother's, and that was kindness, and it was the only tool anybody ever put in my hand. Vegas to Alaska. Alaska to Vegas. Vegas to Michigan. Michigan to Ohio. Ohio to Mexico. Mexicali to Peñasco to Sinaloa to Peñasco to Mexicali to San Diego to Rosarito. Twenty-three miles on foot into Tijuana because I would rather walk through the worst city in the country in the dark than put my hand out to one more stranger. A death wish in someone else's hands. Three days through a desert. Every catastrophe of my adult life, the same procedure: take it, close the ledger, get in the vehicle, go eat somewhere else.

That's it. That's the whole treatment. Not the ayahuasca. Not the mountain. Not the beating and not the dog and not the desert. A fence I could walk out of, and me not walking out of it, one day at a time.  But then I left.  I'm sick.  I"m not right.  They let me leave.

I've told you that in this book, out loud, in those exact words, at least four separate times, and every single time the floor came out from under me inside of a season. On a beach in Peñasco with a Tecate in my hand. In a bus with black paint on the windows. Standing in the wreck of an app with forty-three brewery owners' emails to write.

I know what the medicine taught me and I've written it down where I can't get around it: the grief was never what the drink was standing on. I dragged every buried thing I own up into the light in a concrete room in Sinaloa — the crushed boy, the lost daughter, the woman in the road who was glad it wasn't me — and I laid them all out and wept over every one of them, and then I got in a car and drove north and the wanting was sitting in the passenger seat exactly the same size it had always been.

So I don't get an epiphany. There isn't one coming. The drink stands on nothing but itself, and it will be sitting on the edge of the bed when I walked out that gate, asking the same thing it always asks, and it will ask again the morning after that.

What I had instead is a fence, a man twice a week who can hear the voice come on, a woman across a courtyard doing the same work.  And white marks on the back of an ankle that I check about twice a day to make sure any of it was real.

---

My daughter is thirteen now. She has another good man's last name and no idea I exist, and if I passed her on a sidewalk tomorrow she would not look twice at me.

That's the correct outcome. I've stopped arguing with it. I gave her up because leaving is the only thing I have ever been any good at, and I have spent this entire book telling you the truth about myself so that if she ever comes looking — twenty years from now, forty — there is a document. Somebody wrote it down. He wasn't a mystery and he wasn't a story her mother had to soften. He was this, exactly this, all of it, and he didn't dress up one page of it.

That's what this is. That's what it was always for. Everything else is just how I ended up with something to write about
---

I owe somebody an accounting on the chapters, and I'd rather do it once here than keep doing it a page at a time.

I've been handing them out in this book like a man with an unlimited supply. Carrie gets one and I tell her she deserved a book. Ginger gets one and I tell her she deserved three.  And every time I do that I'm doing the same thing — buying my way out of the debt by naming it, which is a con, and it's one I've run my whole life, and I'd know it anywhere.

So let me just say the true thing and quit.

There is no chapter for Tyler, who is my nephew. There is no chapter for Peyton, who is raising a baby with my boy. There's none for Ben, my step-dad, who was gentle every day of his life and never once raised his voice and did everything my mother asked and never got it back, and who I loved and never told. None for Holly and Natalie, who came into my house at twelve and were my sisters for six years. None for Rose, who put her hands on the wheel for a thousand miles for a man who wouldn't learn her language.

And there is no chapter for Niki.

My oldest sister is on every third page of this book. She's the first phone call in every disaster in it. She wired the money to Alaska. She bought the plane ticket. She converted a closet. And I never gave her a chapter, and she'd be the last person on earth to bring it up.

Here's the reason, and it's not a good one, but it's the accurate one.

I only kept the ruptures. Everything I can see in high definition is a thing that broke. I can give you the exact weight of a handful of my sister's hair and the sound of a car locking up its brakes in 1985 and the smell of a bus with black paint on the windows. The people who were just steadily, boringly, unspectacularly good to me for thirty and forty years at a stretch — they didn't break anything, so there's no film on them.

Which means a book like this one is structurally rigged. It gives the most room to whoever hurt me or whoever I hurt, and the least to whoever simply held. The ones I owe the most to are the ones who gave me the least to write about.

I don't have a fix for that. It's the same defect that got me here — I never once noticed the thing that was working. I only ever came running when it stopped.

---

The light goes long and orange out there at the end of the day the way it did on that rise outside a Sinaloa town, where a girl who wasn't mine used to walk a tall stupid gringo out past the last houses so his phone could drink, and kick rocks, and tell him which uncle was full of it.

I don't know where America is tonight. Mexicali somewhere, with her grandmother, which is the single piece of good news I got out of any of this and I hold it hard.

I never did get a hug out of that kid.

Two years, and it was the flat hand out and the high five, every time, right to the end. And I'd have traded my right arm.

She'd have gotten around to it. That's what I tell myself out here at nine o'clock, and I have no way on this earth to check it, and I'm going to go on telling myself anyway.

I didin't know what I was walking into. No passport, no papers, no country that'll have me through the front door, two warrants that have been sitting there the whole time and are in no hurry at all. A woman I love and can't be alone in a room with. A best friend who believes I threatened his children. A cat on a mountain, or not.

Every instinct I own is already drawing a map. I can feel it doing it right now, sitting here — the old machine warming up, sketching the angle, the play, the beautiful rendering with nothing under it. Forty-seven years old in eight days and it has never once shut off and I don't believe it's going to.

But I know what it is now. It is time. That's the entire difference and it isn't much and it's all I've got. I know the sound it makes when it starts up, because a man in an office who gets paid to notice taught me to hear it, and because I sat on a concrete floor in Sinaloa and had it played back to me by something older than I am.

So I know. I hear it start, and I'll know exactly which direction it's pointing me, and I want to follow it, because I always want to follow it.  A sick man.

A broken compass. It's not like a clock.

It isn't right twice a day.

I hope I caught everyone.  All of you, you were there.  I"m not going to say I'm sorry.  I'm going to say I'm greatful.  Everyone who made the book had a big impact on my life.  I mean that.  In the end, I just couldn't handle it.
