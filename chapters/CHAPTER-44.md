# Chapter 44 — One Bridge

I left Reina’s about two hours before the ride was set to leave.

The shared ride picked up at a Burger King a little over a mile away. A mile is nothing — a walk to the corner. Except this mile ran straight through the spine of Rosarito. There was no way around it. To get there I had to cross the highway, and to cross the highway there was exactly one bridge, unless I wanted to hike hours out of my way to hunt for another. One bridge. One chokepoint. One stretch of open, exposed ground where a hunted man would be caught flat under the lights for anyone to see.

I got maybe halfway there and my legs locked up under me.

I stood on that sidewalk staring at the route ahead — the bridge, the open crossing, the single bottleneck — and every animal instinct that had kept me breathing this long screamed *no.* It told me they’d have eyes on that bridge. It told me if I walked across, I wouldn't walk off the other side. I don’t know if it was true. I’ll never know. But after everything, I had learned to trust that gut over my own head, and that gut said no way in hell.

So I let the ride go.

I turned my back on the bridge, the Burger King, and the thirty dollars Reina’s family had scraped together out of their own empty pockets. I cut into the scrub, turned toward the open country, and pointed myself toward Mexicali on foot.

***

I hadn’t told a soul in Mexicali I was coming. I had friends there — people from our four months, people from rehab — but reaching out meant a phone, and a phone meant a digital trail. I was done leaving threads behind me. I’d either turn up on their doorstep or I wouldn’t.

On paper, Rosarito to Mexicali is about forty-five hours of straight walking. But you can't walk in a straight line out there. I had two bags of coke left, so the plan was the same as the mountain: buy five or six hours of hard walking with a bump, over and over, until a city rose out of the dirt. I figured it would take three days. It took four.

I stayed off the pavement, cutting out into the desert, shadowing the highway far enough away that I was just a heat-shimmer to passing cars, but close enough not to lose my bearings. Back into the mountains. Only this time, I didn't have five gallons of water sloshing against my chest — only a small jug I’d filled at Reina’s sink. By the end of day two, it was bone dry.

The fourth day was the one that nearly put me in the ground.

***

The heat came off that desert like an engine block. The closer I got to Mexicali, the flatter, whiter, and more merciless the land became. No trees, no brush, no shade to lean on. Just the ribbon of highway off to my right and the white sun overhead wringing the last drops of sweat out of me.

The water was gone. The coke was down to dust in the corners of the baggies. I hadn’t slept in better than three days, and somewhere out on that hardpan, the border between what was in front of my face and what was in my skull completely broke down. 

I started seeing Clayita. 

She was trotting out ahead of me across the cracked dirt, looking back over her shoulder just like she used to do around the barn to make sure I was following. I knew she was a ghost. I followed her anyway. In a desert like that, you take whatever guide is offered.

Then the dust kicked up.

A thick plume rose off a dirt service road — a vehicle coming hard, and me caught in the flat open with nowhere to hide. My knees gave out. A dry wash was cut into the hardpan twenty yards off, a waist-deep gully. I half-ran, half-tumbled down into it, flattened myself against the baking dirt, and froze. I still had the bamboo stick from outside Reina's place gripped tight in both hands, white-knuckling it like a sharpened stick could do a damn thing against what was rolling up.

The truck slowed.

The engine dropped to an idle. Tires crunched into the roadside gravel and stopped right above me. Close enough that I heard the cab doors creak open. Close enough that I heard voices — easy, unhurried Mexican Spanish. 

Every cell in my body went ice cold: *They found me. After the mountain, after dodging the bridge, after four days of walking through hell to stay invisible — they found me anyway, and it ends right here in a ditch.*

And then the strangest realization hit me, lying face-down in that dust waiting for the execution.

I didn’t want to die.

I want you to understand what that meant. I’d spent that whole winter on that cartel mountain, and plenty of wasted years before it, half in love with the idea of finally being put out of my misery. I’d welcomed the pistol. I’d looked the man from Sinaloa in the eyes and told him to pull the trigger. Coming off the back of that bus, I gave myself a twenty-percent shot at surviving and was completely at peace with the other eighty. 

But down in that wash, with boots twenty feet away and my throat swollen shut for water, I found the bottom of myself: I wanted to live. Not with pride, not because I thought I deserved it, but the way an animal wants to breathe. I dug my fingers into the dirt and wanted to live so badly it pulled tears out of me — tears I hadn't shed for beatings, or gun barrels, or the wreckage of my entire life.

Boots hit the gravel above. 

A man walked right toward the edge of the wash. I heard each heavy step crunching down until he stopped directly at the lip. I held my breath, gripped that piece of bamboo, and waited for his shadow to cut across the dirt and find me.

It didn't.

I heard him spit. Then came the sound of him unzipping and relieving himself into the brush not ten feet from my head. He yelled something back to the truck, somebody chuckled from inside the cab, and then the boots crunched back through the gravel. Doors slammed. The transmission engaged, tires threw stones, and the truck roared off down the road.

They never had a clue. A gringo that half the coast was hunting was lying close enough to touch while they took a roadside piss.

I don’t know who they were. Cartel runners, ranch hands, or just guys on an errand through the badlands. I’ll never know. The terrifying thing is how thin the margin was: my life hung on which direction a total stranger happened to look, just like it had a dozen times before, and I hadn’t earned an ounce of that luck.

I stayed buried in that dirt long after the dust settled, shaking and sobbing into the ground. Crying for Glenda, who was never coming home. For Clayita, who was never out there in the heat. For the years I’d burned and the family I’d abandoned. 

And then, because the only other choice was dying right there in the ditch, I stood back up.

***

I crested the final rise late that fourth afternoon, sun-blind, lips cracked open, running on pure fumes. 

And there it was.

Mexicali. Sprawled out flat, dusty, and shimmering under the heat haze. The city I’d once been hauled into in handcuffs and hauled out of in an immigration van. The city where Glenda had first kicked the habit, where Zaul had built the compound that this whole nightmare had spun out of. 

The border was right there. Safety was right there.

There was no dramatic finish. I just came down off that ridge and kept putting one boot in front of the other across the scrub until the pavement met me, the buildings closed in, and the desert quietly handed me back to the world.

Against every odd on that mountain, I walked out on my own two feet. Alive. Right into the city that was about to save my life.
