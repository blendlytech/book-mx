I want to tell you I had the whole thing beat, because that is exactly what I believed, riding shotgun in Jo's car with Mexicali falling away behind us and three hours of open desert running out ahead.

Glenda had done her time. She'd checked herself in, so she could check herself out — her own signature, her own discretion, free to walk whenever she decided she was ready — and looking back on it now, it wasn't near long enough. Not for her and not for me. But you don't know that at the time. At the time I had a clean phone floor running itself behind me, a replacement trained and seated, an Airbnb run out to its last paid day, and a sober woman in the back seat that I'd finally, after three years, gotten to hold in my own two arms. I had done every single thing I'd come down to that country to do, and then I'd done the one thing I hadn't even known to come for, which was get myself clean. The math all came out my way. I had it beat.

Glenda didn't think so.

She was nervous, and she has never once in her life been any good at hiding what she's feeling, so she didn't try. She told me straight out she was scared. I heard it and I filed it under *new sobriety, big change, of course she's rattled* and I drove right on past it, the way I drove past most things a person tried to hand me for free. She was the one reading the road right. I was the one who had it beat. Remember which of us was which.

---

Here is how we ended up pointed at the coast in the first place, and I need you to sit in it with me a second, because if you've been with me since the Orleans you are going to feel the floor tilt.

Glenda had a friend down in Puerto Peñasco — a man named Temo, bilingual, sharp as a tack, one of those people everybody likes inside of a minute. And Temo was *killing it.* Doing better than well. At a beachfront resort called Laguna Shores. Selling timeshares.

Selling timeshares.

I had spent six years of my one life on a telephone putting strangers into the exact chairs Temo's whole operation lived and died on — running the play I showed you back at Nate's bar. Never say the word *timeshare.* Never warm a soul up for a presentation, because warming them up admits a presentation is coming. You get them in the chair *cold* — no name on it, guard all the way down — because a person cannot brace against a pitch he doesn't know exists, and nobody says no to a thing nobody will admit is being sold. Omit, never lie. I'd run it ten thousand times down a phone line and I was, if I'm honest, one of the better men alive at it.

And now here I was, fresh out of one Mexican cage and aimed straight at a beach town to run the very same con — except this time on my feet, in the flesh, in broad daylight. Six years I'd been the disembodied voice on the wire that put people in the chairs. Now I was going to be the man standing directly in front of those chairs, looking the mark dead in the eye while I walked them through a thing I would never once name to their face. Same play. Same concealment. Same omit-never-lie. I'd just never had to do it with my own actual face before.

Because Temo had made us an offer that sounded like found money. A month, rent-free, in a unit right there at the resort, and a free breakfast or a free lunch for every tour we walked a couple through. Live on the sand for nothing, eat for nothing, and do the one thing on God's earth I already knew how to do better than I knew how to do anything else.

We thought it was perfect. Of course we did.

And I looked the part, for once in a long while. I was up to a hundred and seventy pounds by then — thirty on me since I'd dripped into my father's kitchen a buck-forty skeleton, every ounce of it put back sober — and it sat on me right. Better than I'd looked in years. I looked clean. I looked like a man who slept at night and ate his breakfast. There is a particular feeling, if you've ever been down to the studs and climbed back, to buttoning a pressed dress shirt over a body that's quit trying to die on you, stepping into the slacks and the dress shoes, catching yourself in the mirror and finding somebody in there you halfway recognized and halfway respected. I looked the part because for the first time in a long time I damn near was it.

And there was a circle quietly closing in it, too, one I didn't say out loud to a soul. I'd started my whole sales life face-to-face — a kid on a car lot, reading a man across the hood of a trade-in, years before I ever put on a headset. The phones came after. So lacing up those dress shoes to walk a resort floor wasn't only a new beginning, it was the *oldest* one I had, the very first room I'd ever learned to work come all the way back around to meet me. I was excited. God help me, I was genuinely excited.

---

So we checked Glenda out, and we loaded up into her sister Jo's car — Jo, who'd run me across the border in the first place, now running us toward the water — and the three of us set out for a town not one of us had ever laid eyes on. Three hours of desert. And somewhere out in the middle of all that nothing it landed on me, clean and quiet, what we actually had for the first time.

No curfew. No cup. No lady waiting by a door to lean in and smell my breath, no lab cup with my name on a piece of tape, nobody's nose to blow into when we got wherever we were going. For two solid months every single night of my life had ended at a cage door at 8:30 with a kiss and a chemical test, and now the door was just gone. The whole apparatus of it, gone. It was the first stretch of real, unwatched freedom Glenda and I had ever had together on this earth — three years of knowing each other, two months of cups, and now nobody on God's green ground keeping tabs on either one of us but ourselves.

A man who'd been paying closer attention would have been terrified of precisely that. I was thrilled.

---

And we had a plan. That's the part that still gets a hand around my throat when I think on it, because it was a *good* one, a real one, the most ordinary beautiful plan two people ever made.

America — Glenda's girl, eleven years old now — had been with Glenda's mother the entire time Glenda was getting clean, safe and looked after and waiting. The plan was Rose would keep her through the end of the school year and then bring her out to Peñasco, and the three of us would start the whole thing fresh by the ocean. A unit, then a place of our own. A school for the kid. Mornings on the beach. The two drunks who'd loved each other through a screen for three years, sober now, with the daughter and the salt air and a town nobody had ever watched us fail in. We had a plan, and we believed every word of it, and I would like the record to show that for once we were not lying — not to each other, not even to ourselves.

---

Rose knew the road we were on better than the both of us put together.

A lifetime ago she'd sold meth on the wrong side of the line and gone down hard for it — eight years in a federal prison in Arizona, and the day they finally let her out they walked her straight to the border and put her on the far side of it, deported on the spot, better than fifteen years back now. She had watched every reel of this picture before. She didn't lecture me. Before we pulled out she just asked me, plain and quiet, the way only somebody who's already paid in full can ask a thing — to take care of her daughter, and not to let her drink.

I gave her my best promise. Neither of us would touch it. And understand me: I meant it, all the way down.

The promise had one hole in it, and Rose and I both knew the exact size and shape of the hole, and neither of us said its name out loud. Sober was the vow. Weed was never in the vow. We'd be high inside the week, the both of us, and Rose knew that too and it didn't trouble her one bit — pot had never once been the thing that broke either Glenda or me; the bottle was the killer in our family, always had been. The weed was just weed. It happens to be wildly illegal down there, the kind of illegal that can cost a person their whole freedom the way I'd just spent a chapter showing you — anywhere in Mexico.

Anywhere but Peñasco. In Peñasco it wouldn't cost us a thing.

That's what we drove toward. The free unit, the open road, the kid coming after school let out, the perfect deal, the first air either of us had ever breathed without a watcher in it. I had it beat.

I want you to hold on to that — *I had it beat* — and carry it with you into what comes next.
