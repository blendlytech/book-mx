# Chapter 47 — Always a Tuesday
It was another two weeks before Omar surfaced.

I'd about given up on him coming at all — figured I was just another body on a bunk to him now, another strung-out story he'd heard a thousand versions of. Then on a Tuesday, it's always a Tuesday, a staffer told me Omar wanted me in his office, and I walked over there thinking finally, rehearsing my pitch the way I'd rehearsed a thousand pitches. A few weeks in one of his apartments. I'd make it right. I'd have money coming. I had it all laid out and timed, the ask buried three sentences deep where it wouldn't spook him, because that is what I do, and I had done it walking into every room I ever walked into.  I had been rehearsing this for when I finally got the chance to deliver it.

I opened the door and damn near fell the fuck over.

Glenda was sitting in a chair directly across from him.

Glenda. In a chair. Breathing. Whole. Looking right at me.

She came up out of that seat and we crashed into each other and I lost it entirely. I want to be honest that it wasn't relief. Relief has a shape to it, relief is a thing that comes down. This was my whole chest coming apart in a stranger's office. I had buried this woman. I had grieved her in a ditch in the desert with men pissing ten feet from my head. I'd stood in front of an empty call center and laid her in the ground in my mind and made my peace with it, and I had walked four days out of the mountains carrying that, and here she was, solid and warm and crying into my neck, and not one single thing in my head knew what to do with it.

Omar got up and went out and shut the door behind him. I didn't notice him do it. He told me later he stood in the hall a good ten minutes.

You're *dead*, I said into her hair. I didn't have a better word. Where did you go. How — what happened. How did you get out.

The questions came out of me in a pile, tripping over each other, not one of them finishing. She just held on tighter and said the only thing she said for a while.

*I'm sorry. I'm so sorry.*

And right there, fast as the joy had come, something came up behind it — because *sorry* is a word you say when you did a thing, and a dead woman doesn't do a thing to be sorry for.

What the fuck, I said, and I pulled back to look at her. You just *left* me up there? You were here this whole time? Your mom, your sister — I reached out to them. More than once. They told me they didn't know where you were. Nobody knew where you were. I thought you were *dead*, Glenda. I walked four days through the desert thinking you were in the ground. I *grieved* you. Do you understand that? And — Christ — did you know Zaul's here? He's *here*, he's been here the whole —

*Sit down, baby.*

She took both my hands and pulled me down into the chair beside her, and she looked at me with those eyes I'd buried and never thought I'd see open again.

*Let me explain what happened.*

---

You remember the nice couple, she said. The ones who hauled the feed in. Who started bringing food for Clayita.

Of course I do, I said. I'll remember them the rest of my life.

Well. I confided in them.

And then she told me how she got off that mountain, and it was so simple it made me sick.

She'd worked the one angle nobody else up there could work, because she had the language and she had the face that belonged and she had the one man on that hill whose guard came all the way down around her. She got Omar drunk. Our Omar. The mountain one, the one whose name I will never be able to hear clean again — got him good and drunk on a morning, and while he was under, that couple put her in the bed of their truck under a tarp and drove her the whole way to Mexicali. Flat on her back over the wheel well for the first half of it, holding her breath through every checkpoint and every slow turn, listening to men she couldn't see ask her driver questions in the dark.

She'd run a month before I did.

A month. She'd been off that mountain and down in this city, breathing, for a month before I ever put a broken piece of wood to the screw in that window — and in that month, and in all the weeks after it, she had thought exactly what I'd thought.

I thought you were dead too, baby, she said. I really did.

---

And then she told me the rest of it, and the rest of it is the part I've had the hardest time carrying, because there's nobody to be angry at in it.

My mom, my sister — they never told me you reached out.

She said it flat. Not defending them, not accusing them. Just the fact,

She'd tried my family the same as I'd tried hers. Thrown messages into the same silence I'd been throwing them into, from the other side of it. Different names, different accounts, the same dark. And out of every soul she reached for, exactly one answered her.

Zach.

Did he tell you I reached out, she asked.

No, I said. He never said a word.

I sat with that a second. My nephew — the last believer, the kid who'd sent me the last sixty dollars in his pocket on the word of an uncle telling a story nobody could be blamed for not believing — had been holding both ends of it and never knew he was holding both ends of it.

The only person I got through to was Brandy, I said. And that was a week ago. When they gave me the laptop back.

*Your* laptop, she said, and there was the ghost of the old smile on her, the first thing that had come across her face that wasn't grief. You mean my sister's laptop. You still have that thing?

Yeah, I said. I still have it.

She laughed. Actually laughed — the first real one in what felt like a year, and it came up out of her cracked and rusty like something that had been sitting in a garage.

Only you, she said. Only you would walk down off a cartel mountain and through the desert for four days and still have Jo's laptop in the bag.

It was actually seven total, I had to correct her, and it came out of my mouth before I could stop it. I can't help it.

---

I don't have a wise thing to say about it. I've tried to find one and there isn't one.

Two people spent five months in a hollowed-out bus four feet from each other. Then they spent two months in the same city, twenty minutes apart, and each one of them was standing at a funeral for the other. Every message we sent went out into the same silence from opposite ends of it and not one of them ever found the person on the far side. Nobody did anything cruel. Nobody lied on purpose that I know of. It was just families and phones and fear and a couple of people not passing a thing along, and that was enough. That was all it took.

I never did get a straight answer about why her mother and her sister told me they didn't know where she was. I asked, once, later, and got something soft back, and I let it go. I've decided I don't need it. Those women had a daughter and a sister come home half dead off a mountain and then a gringo asking questions on a phone from a country he wasn't supposed to be in, and I can build about four versions of that in my head where nobody is a villain. I'd rather carry four decent versions than go digging for one bad one.

That's a new thing for me, incidentally. Leaving a rock unturned. I'd put it down to growth if I hadn't only just learned to do it at forty-some years old, on a folding chair, with everything already broken.

---

There was one more thing, and I'd been carrying it since Michigan.

I'd never asked her about it. Not in three years of a screen, not in two years of a country, not in five months of a bus with black paint on the windows.  I to this day have no idea why it was important other than I thought I'd never get the answer for it when I thought she was dead and I always wanted to ask it.  I just never thought it to be the right time and regretted not asking when I had the chance.  It sounds stupid now writing it, but I asked it.  I was just so broken at the time and it just came out like an after school special.  The ending of a movie you wouldn't believe.

What was wrong, I said. Back then. You'd get on that camera at night and your eyes would fill up and you'd look off somewhere and I'd ask you what was wrong, and you wouldn't say.  You would cry looking at me. I knew the answer. I always knew the answer.

She didn't say anything.

Every night, I said. For months. I asked you a hundred times and you never once told me. So tell me now. We're past everything. Tell me.

She looked at the corner of Omar's desk for a long while.

I was watching you die, she said.

And there it was. The plainest sentence anybody has said to me in my life, and I'd waited two years to hear it, and it took her four seconds.

She said she'd sit there in that kitchen in Mexicali with the bottle where the bottle always was and the lit rectangle propped up in front of her, and she'd watch a man two thousand miles off get a little smaller every week. She said by November she wasn't looking at a man anymore. She said she'd already started thinking of me in the past tense and hating herself for it, and that when I'd ask her what was wrong the true answer was *you're going to be dead before I ever get to touch you*, and there is no way to put that sentence into a phone at eleven at night to a man who's already got a glass in his hand.

So she didn't. She looked off and said nothing and I reached for the glass, and we did that for months.

Then she said the part I didn't expect and didn't ask her selfishly for.

That's why I quit, she said.

I asked her to say it again. I actually did that. I sat there and made her say it again.  Understand, I was looking at a dead woman I'd had so many questions for. So many questions I'd wished I'd asked laying there in that rehab center thinking I'd never have the answers. Now she was right there in front of me.

She said she'd checked herself into this building — *this* building, the one we were sitting in, two years and one whole life ago — because she'd decided somebody was going to have to be standing up when I got there. That she couldn't do a thing about me from where she was. That the only piece of it that was hers to move was her, so she moved that. 

It wasn't that profound or well put together. I did that for the book, but it was the jist of it and it killed me.

I have thought about that more than almost anything else in this book.

Because I built my entire crossing on her getting sober. It's right there on the first page. She put the bottle down and that was the crack the rest of it poured through — that's how I've told it my whole life, the miracle that turned a three-year drunken phone-fling into a reason to sell off the last of what I owned and go. I flew two thousand miles on the strength of that woman getting clean.

And I never once asked her why she did it.

Not one time. Two years. I took the miracle and I ran it as leverage on myself — *she did it, so I can do it, and I'll do it in the gravity of the place without ever taking a bed* — and I never turned around and asked the person who did the thing what it cost her or what it was for.

She got sober because she was watching me die.

And I came down there and put a Tecate in her hand on a back road in Peñasco, and eight months after that she was doing a cartel's cocaine out of a green-sealed bag in a room in Rosarito, and five months after that we were both getting beaten in the dirt in front of each other by men in good boots.

I said something to that effect. I don't remember it exactly.  I'm sure it was profound in my mind.. Whatever it was, it was long, and it had a lot of *I'm sorry* in it, and somewhere in the middle of it I heard my own voice doing the thing it does — building, shaping, working the room, even now, even here, even at this.

She let me get about halfway.

Then she did that flat one-note thing through her nose that she does when a man says something stupid with his whole chest.

Don't, she said. I picked it back up. Nobody held my mouth open.

And that was the end of that. That's the whole of what she'd give me. She'd carried a two-year answer for me and handed it across and then flatly refused to let me turn it into a thing about myself, which is the single most useful thing anybody has ever done for me and she did it in nine words.

---

Then Omar came back in. The good one. The one I'd keep.

You two have been through a lot, he said. I think you should stay here a while. Nobody is going to mess with you in this place. You're safe here.

I don't feel safe here, I don't feel safe anywhere Omar.  Do you have somewhere else I can go?  Do you have somewhere you can send us?  She looked at me and shook her head and I knew the answer.  I knew the answer long before Omar told me.

He turned to Glenda. You should check in too. The women's side. He looked back at me, then at both of us together. You both need some serious counseling after all this — serious. I'll find you an English-speaking one, Clay. I'll make sure of it.

I couldn't say no to Omar. I want to tell you I weighed it, that I sat there like a man with options and chose the wise one. The truth is I had nowhere else on earth to go, and a good man was holding a door open, and for once in my life I was tired enough and beaten enough and grateful enough to walk through it without first working out where the angle was.

I've got a room with air conditioning, Omar said. It'll be just you and Freddie. Freddie built my house — he does all the maintenance and the add-ons around here. He's gone seven to five during the week and he's hardly ever around on weekends. He just stays here to keep himself in check, keep himself off the stuff. You can work out of that room. Get better. Get help. He nodded at Glenda. You too.

That's a pretty big deal in a place like this, because in general population you are housed with 30 or so people in a room with no air conditioning.  Waiting for a family memeber to sign a letter that lets you go home.  This was a blessing.  I couldn't say no.  

We both agreed. Six months. I signed on the line — out date sometime in October.

And I sat there in that folding chair and felt the whole shape of it land on me at once. The rehab I'd come into this country to make a buck off of. The building I'd flown down to *use*. The back room I'd run my little con out of, ten duct-taped Dells and a floor full of sober people I'd figured to rent by the hour. That exact place was now the thing that was going to save my life, and the woman I'd come down here to be a hero for was checking into the women's side twenty yards away, and neither one of us had a dollar or a document or a plan between us.

You couldn't write it. I've said that before in these pages and I keep running into it, which is the whole problem with telling a true story: the parts nobody would believe are the parts you can't take out.  It may not be a love story, but it's a human one.

What Glenda and I were going to be now, I couldn't have told you. We'd both been in the ground as far as the other one knew. You don't come back up out of that and pick a thing up where you set it down. We'd see. I was done pretending I knew things I didn't.

---

I'd worry about the ID later I didn't have. I'd worry about all of it later.

That night I lay in a bed with air conditioning running over me, in a building I'd flown across a continent to swindle, four weeks off a mountain, with the woman I'd buried asleep on the other side of a courtyard.

And I want to tell you that was the end of the bad news.

But they gave me my laptop back that same week. And when I finally got my TextNow working again, there was a message sitting in it from Ace that had been waiting the better part of a month.
