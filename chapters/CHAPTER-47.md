# Chapter 47 — The Last Man Standing

Ace was the last man standing, and neither one of us knew it yet when he sat down to type.

He'd had months of it dammed up behind him and no soft way to let it out. He'd been carrying it alone, which is the part I keep coming back to. So he did the only thing a man can do with a load like that when the person it belongs to has dropped off the face of the earth: he wrote it all down in one message and sent it into the dark, having no idea whether it would ever reach anyone. He assumed I was dead as well.

It sat in that app the better part of a month while I was in detox and then in a bunkroom with no way to reach a soul.

I read the whole thing standing up.

***

Ritchie was dead.

Fentanyl. Four months back.

I did the arithmetic before I'd finished the sentence, because I always do the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is always the worst part. Four months back put it in January. And the last thing I had from Ritchie in this world was a text that said: *Happy New Year.*

He sent that, and then inside of a few weeks he was gone.

I never answered it. I was on a mountain, locked in a bus with black paint on the windows when it came in. I didn't have a phone, and there wasn't a damn thing I could have done about it — I know that, I've told myself that, I'll go on telling myself that until the day I die. It doesn't help. The message is still sitting in that app. It'll be sitting there when I'm gone. Two words and an exclamation point from a man who is never going to send another.

You have to understand what Ritchie was to me. He was my shadow in Vegas and I was his. He picked me up every single morning in that silver Lexus — spotless, always spotless — wearing Versace shades, a Rolex, a Yankees cap, a plain tee, and cargo shorts. We'd go run our casino free-play before either one of us did a lick of work, because that was the routine before the job. Whatever came out of a machine that day got split straight down the middle, no discussion, no counting, because that's just what it was between us.

I've said all that before, but I need to say it again. I still want to say more.

He had a daughter. She was twelve when I first met him, and I got to watch that girl grow all the way up — twelve to near-grown, smart and funny and genuinely kind, the unmistakable center of that man's entire life. She's about eighteen now.

Somebody had to go tell her. Somebody had to sit in a room I've never seen and break that girl's heart, and I wasn't there for one second of it.

He'd argue politics with me until the paint peeled off the wall and relish every single minute of it. He was dead certain about everything he believed, and so was I about entirely different things. Neither one of us ever moved the other an inch, and it never cost us a single morning.

I don't have anything wiser than that. He was my best friend in that city, and he died of the poison that's killing everybody. I found out four months late, staring at a laptop screen.

***

Silesh got into the meth.

Ace said you wouldn't have recognized him. It carved him all the way down to a rail.

I want to say who that man was before I tell the rest, because the rest will sit heavy on top of him otherwise, and he doesn't deserve to be buried under it. Silesh was the smoothest human being I have ever stood next to. Somali, born and raised, a ladies' man, a shameless self-promoter, and funnier than anybody in that entire crew, including me. I'll tell you what he was worth on a sales floor: if Silesh took over your live transfer, you bought yourself a clean forty-five minutes. That’s not an exaggeration. Forty-five minutes of *“sweetie”* this and *“doll”* that, and you could walk away to gamble, smoke, or sleep on an office floor, and come back to a closed deal. He'd sit there reeling in a mark with a glass of water beside him that he never touched. I teased that man without an ounce of mercy for years, and he loved every second of it, because he was the finest sport God ever made.

One evening, the highway patrol pulled him over on a desert highway outside Vegas. He sat in his car through a standoff that stretched on for hours. When the hours ran out, he put a gun to his own head, right there behind the wheel.

That was September.

I have gone back and checked where I was in September, and I wish to God I hadn't. That was the month I was sleeping on the sand in San Diego, hauling crushed aluminum cans to a recycling center to scrape together the price of a bar of soap and a disposable razor, just so I could clean up enough to walk south across the border. 

Which means there is a specific night in that month — some exact evening I could name if anyone ever handed me the police report — where I was trudging twenty-three miles down a pitch-black coastal highway in a dirty black t-shirt, wallowing in self-pity and half-hoping something out there would finish me off. And three hundred miles northeast of that road, Silesh was sitting in a stopped car with sirens flashing, having already made up his mind.

Both of us alone on a dark highway. One of us kept walking.

There is no lesson in that. I've looked for one.

***

And John-J.

Brain aneurysm, a month ago. On his floor mat.

John-J was in his sixties, our lovable old stray, and hands down the worst salesman God ever sat in front of a telephone. That's not me being cruel — that was the standing joke, he was completely in on it, and he'd have told you the exact same thing with a grin. He pitched bad joke after bad joke into dead silence, cheerful as a man could be, and he had the biggest heart of any of us by a mile. He was the only single guy in the crew. He rented a bare room for four hundred bucks a month, and there was no bed in it. Never was. He slept on a foam mat on the linoleum for years and never complained once.

He died on that mat, in that empty room, alone, last month.

Here is the memory I can't put down, the one I've turned over in my head a hundred times:

The last time I saw John-J was the best night our crew ever had. The Presidential Suite on the top floor of the Orleans in Vegas. Floor-to-ceiling panoramic windows overlooking the Strip, a jacuzzi bubbling full of people, a wet bar, and our loud, wild family howling until the sun broke over the valley. Every single one of us climbed into that hot tub except John-J. At dawn, when we all drifted over to the glass to watch the morning light hit the desert, someone went looking for him and found him passed out cold on the carpet outside the bathroom.

We laughed. God, how we laughed. We stood over that man on the floor at six in the morning, roasting him and giving him hell. It was hilarious, and he woke up laughing right along with us.

Four years later, he laid down on a floor in a room with no furniture, and nobody found him for days. Nobody laughed. Nobody was standing over him at all.

Same man. Same floor, essentially. The only difference between those two mornings was whether there was anybody in the room who loved him.

***

Out of the five of us — me, Ritchie, Ace, John-J, and Silesh — the found family that took in a broke, wanted, ruined fugitive and called him a brother without ever once asking what sins drove him to their door, there is only Ace and me left.

That's it. That's all that remains of that table.

And it was Ace who had to sit down and type it out. The last man standing, forced to carry the ledger and deliver three death notices all at once to a friend who had vanished nine months earlier and might easily have been dead in a ditch himself.

I wasn't there for a single one of them. That is the boulder sitting on my chest that will not budge. I missed all three. While my brothers were dropping one by one three hundred miles to the north, I was up on a cartel compound weighing bags of cocaine onto a folding table for men who turned a dog loose on me for entertainment. I didn't know. And there wasn't a single thing I could have done to save them even if I had.

I've tried using that excuse to soften the blow. It doesn't soften anything. It's factual, and it doesn't help at all, so I've stopped expecting it to.

***

I found all of this out in the very same week I discovered Zaul was alive and Glenda wasn't dead.

Think about the sheer whiplash of that. I've had months to process it, and it still refuses to sit flat. Inside of seven days, every single person I had buried in my mind sat up and started breathing, and every single person I had left alive went into the dirt. 

The entire ledger flipped upside down. 

Zaul, whom I had mourned at an abandoned call center, was standing in a hallway in a grey t-shirt, alive and apologizing. Glenda, whom I had wept for in a desert gully while cartel guys pissed ten feet from my head, was sitting in a folding chair, laughing about her sister's laptop. And Ritchie, Silesh, and John-J — who were all healthy and laughing the last time I saw them — were gone. All three. Buried by strangers while I was learning how to keep my eyes glued to the dirt.

I sat in a plastic chair inside that Mexicali clinic and tried to hold all of that in two hands, and I couldn't. I still can't. I've had counseling since, talked through it in English with a therapist paid to listen, and I haven't made it make any more sense than it does right now.

This world is completely insane. I have no profound wisdom to drape over it. It's just cold and crazy. It never checks with you first, and while you're busy running for your life, it keeps right on deciding who gets to stay and who has to go.

***

There is an older version of me that would have walked straight out the front gate and drowned this in booze. I want that on the record, because I don't get to write a chapter about three dead friends and pretend I was suddenly noble.

The old instinct was screaming at me, offering the same rotten solution it has offered my entire life: *Three of your brothers are dead, you missed the end, you can't fix it, and the pain is too big to carry — so bury it in the cellar and go pour a drink.* Swallow it down, shut down the feelings, numb out. That procedure has governed every disaster of my adult life ever since I was a seven-year-old boy standing in a living room holding a fistful of my sister's hair.

Except I was four weeks off a cartel mountain, locked inside a fenced compound with barred gates, and there wasn't a drop of alcohol within miles.

That is the only reason I stayed sober. I want to be brutally honest about that. It wasn't moral fortitude. It wasn't spiritual growth. It wasn't a single virtue I had earned. It was a chain-link fence, a urine screen every Monday, a good man who kept the door locked, and the sheer dumb luck of finally washing up in the one place on earth where my old self couldn't find a drink.

Sometimes you don't get better right away. Sometimes you just end up somewhere they won't let you get any worse, and you stand there long enough for the difference to start meaning something.
