# Chapter 17
Two months. Two entire months on that beach, and we did not touch a drop. Not one. People don't believe that part when I tell it, but it is God's own truth. We turned down every party we got asked to, and on that strip you got asked constantly. We kept to our own selves. We weren't killing it on the floor — nobody was; I'll tell you right now that timeshare is about the single hardest thing a human being can be asked to sell, and I have sold some hard things — but we didn't have to be killing it, because the solar money was still landing out of Mexicali like clockwork, never a nickel under five grand a month, and the sun came up over the water every morning like it was doing it on purpose. July of 2024. The weather on that beach was so good it felt rigged. We had each other, sober, in the daylight, and a little money coming in, and for the first stretch of my grown life I wasn't running from a thing or running toward one either. I was just there. It was the closest to peace I'd been since I was a boy.

---

We even made friends. There was a fella we called Colombia — on account of that being precisely where he was from and the two of us not being any too clever about nicknames.

Colombia didn't speak a lick of English, not one word of it, so everything I know about the man I know secondhand from the people who could talk to him, and every last one of them told me the identical thing: smooth as oil, a ladies' man of the first order, a born charmer. I had to take it on faith. What I had firsthand was the driving. Colombia picked us up every single morning and carried us where we needed to go, and the man drove a hundred miles an hour everywhere he went — through town, past the resorts, down roads that were more crater than road — swerving around the potholes and the other cars like the whole thing was a video game he'd already beaten once.

I thought, more than one time, sitting in that car: *this is it. This is how we die.* The dumb, sideways, foreign-road way it finally happens. And I quit putting my seatbelt on. I want to be honest about why, because this is the kind of honest the book is for — it wasn't machismo. I left the belt off because some flat, tired part of me had already done the math and decided that if we were going to wreck, and we obviously were, I did not want to be the one who lived through it. Let it be clean. That was the shape my mind was still in, underneath all that beach-peace, and it took me years afterward to turn around and see the thing sitting there in the seat next to me the whole time.

Somehow the wreck never came. The one I was so sure of just never arrived.

---

What arrived instead was a Tecate store.

Colombia was forever trying to get us out — come drink, come to the party, *vámonos* — and for two months we'd told him no every time and meant it. We held that line about as long as two people like us were ever going to hold it. And then, somewhere in the third month, for no reason and for every reason at once, we stopped holding it. We were walking home and we passed a little store that sold Tecate, and the temperature outside was the exact right temperature, and our mood was the exact right mood, and Glenda and I just looked at each other.

That was all it took. One look. No argument, no big discussion, no working up to it. It was on, and we both knew it was on.

I went in. I came out with two tall boys of Tecate Red, and we took the back way home so we could drink them as we walked. And Lord, they were good. I'm not going to lie to you and say they turned to ash in my mouth, because that is not how it goes and you already know it isn't. They tasted like the best thing I'd had in months, and we felt amazing, the two of us, warm and loose and laughing down a back road in Mexico with a cold can sweating in my hand.

It should have ended right there. Two beers on a walk. It never does.

Two tall boys became a twelve-pack. The twelve-pack found a bottle of vodka. The vodka, somewhere down in the small hours, turned up a bag of coke — it always knows a guy, the vodka does — and the bag of coke handed me the single worst hangover of my entire natural life, and understand that I have built a life out of bad hangovers. We really, truly did it that time. Took the whole thing down to the studs in one night, like we'd been saving up the whole two months for it.

We didn't do it again the next day. Or the day after that. We let it sit for weeks, and when we did pick a drink back up it was a tall boy, maybe two, and then we'd set it down. We'd just stop. I did not want another hangover like that one — and this was the miracle of it, this was the thing I had never once in forty-some years on this earth been able to do — I didn't get one. I could have a beer and put the next one back in the cooler. I could drink on a Tuesday and wake up Wednesday like a human being, my next three days still in my pocket instead of bled out on a bathroom tile. I was, at long and everlasting last, drinking like a normal person.

You've read this whole book; you know what my father had that I never did — the dial where I'd only ever been issued a switch. And here I was, on a beach in Mexico, clean off the hard stuff and sipping one slow beer like I'd been born knowing how, and I believed — God help me, I believed it straight down to the floor of myself — that the beach had finally handed me that dial. That I had been cured of the one thing that does not get cured. It was great.

---

We each scratched out a couple of timeshare sales, nothing that mattered, nothing that was going to cover a light bill, and after the second month we just quit. Walked off the floor and became full-time beach bums, the pair of us — a few beers, a couple of joints, good food, the water. Never overdoing it. Living off the Mexicali money and the sun.

America came down in September.

She hadn't wanted to leave Mexicali — that's where her whole life was, her friends, her school — and she'd begged her mother to let her finish out the summer up there, and Glenda let her. So we had the whole of August to ourselves, and then the first week of September the girl came down to start school in Peñasco, and it was a good September. I'll say that plain. But I have to be straight about America and me.

We never clicked. Not really. She spoke good English, so it wasn't that — it was that she was meeting *me*, the actual flesh-and-blood me, after years of knowing me only as a man inside her mother's phone, a face in a lit box, and the box and the man just never quite lined up for her. We had our moments. But we never hugged, not one single time the whole way through. It was always a high five. I'd open my arms somewhere down in my chest and she'd put up a flat hand, and we'd slap it, and that was as close as she would let me come. What she *would* do — every time, without fail — was take my side. Her mother and I would get into it, the way we did, and that girl would land on my side of the fight every time, like she'd quietly appointed herself my attorney. I think she just needed time to learn she could trust me. We didn't end up getting all the time we needed.

Her own father wanted nothing to do with her. Glenda told me one night, quiet, that the one thing America had ever really wanted was a father — just a father figure, somebody to *be* that for her. And I wanted to be it. God, I wanted to be it. Understand that I'd had a two-year-old daughter of my own once, a lifetime and a whole country ago, and I'd stood there and watched her get carried out of my life for good — and here was a kid at my table who needed the exact one thing I had never gotten to give a soul. I'd have traded my right arm to be that man for her. We were getting there, too. Slow. A high five at a time.

And for once, we got more time at it than I was used to getting at anything good — because the good run, the real one, held on longer than anything good in my life ever had.

America moved in with us for keeps, and the three of us — four, whenever Rose came around — settled into a thing that looked, from the outside and even from the inside, like an honest-to-God family. The money kept right on coming out of Mexicali, clockwork, never a nickel short, straight through the fall and on toward Christmas. I had a woman and a kid and a paycheck and a patch of beach, and I was sober enough most days to actually feel all four of them, drinking like the normal man I had finally and gloriously talked myself into believing I'd become. I want to mark this stretch for you plain, because there is not much of it in this whole book and even while I stood in it I knew to hold still: for those few months at the tail end of 2024, I was happy. Flat-out, undeserving, borrowed happy. And it held.

And then Rose said the one word that would load the four of us into a car and carry us a thousand miles down into the most dangerous country I have ever set foot in.

*Home.*

Rose and Glenda were Sinaloa women, born and raised, and Rose still had a brother down there in the old place. What she wanted — now that her daughter was clean, her granddaughter was near, and the whole patched-together clan of us was finally standing in one country at one time — was to take us all home for the holidays. The whole of December. Likely January hung on the back of it. Home to Sinaloa.

I said yes the way I said yes to everything in those years: fast, grinning, and without the first clue what I was agreeing to. I had no passport. I was a gringo carrying two warrants out of the States and not one scrap of paper in Mexico, and I had just signed on to be driven a thousand miles down into the one stretch of that whole country whose name even people who know nothing else about Mexico know to say quietly.

We loaded up the car.
