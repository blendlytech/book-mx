# Chapter 48 — The Last Man Standing
Ace was the last man standing, and neither one of us knew it yet when he sat down to type.

He'd had months of it dammed up behind him and no soft way to let it out, and he'd been carrying it alone, which is the part I keep coming back to. So he did the only thing a man can do with a load like that when the person it belongs to has dropped off the face of the earth. He wrote it all down in one message and sent it into the dark and had no way of knowing whether it would ever land on anybody.  He assumed me dead as well.

It sat in that app the better part of a month while I was in detox and then in a bunkroom with no way to reach a soul.

I read the whole thing standing up.

---

Ritchie was dead.

Fentanyl. Four months back.

I did the arithmetic before I'd finished the sentence, because I always do the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is the worst part. Four months back put it in January. And the last thing I have from Ritchie in this world is a text that says *Happy New Year.*

So he sent that, and then inside of a few weeks he was gone.

I never answered it. I was on a mountain in a bus with black paint on the windows when it came in, and I didn't have a phone, and there wasn't a thing I could have done about it — I know that, I've told myself that, I'll go on telling myself that. It doesn't touch it. The message is still sitting in that app. It'll be sitting there when I'm dead. Two words and an exclamation point from a man who is not going to send me another one.

You have to understand what Ritchie was. He was my shadow in that city and I was his. He picked me up every single morning in that silver Lexus, spotless, always spotless, the Versace shades and the Rolex and the Yankees cap and a plain tee and cargo shorts, and we'd go run the free play before either one of us did a lick of work, because that was the job before the job. And whatever came out of a machine that day got split straight down the middle, no discussion, no counting, because that's what it was between us.  I've said all that, but I wanted to say it again.  

I still want to say more.

He had a daughter. She was twelve when I met him and I got to watch that girl come all the way up — twelve to near-grown, smart and funny and genuinely kind, and the unmistakable love of that man's whole life. She's about eighteen now.

Somebody had to go tell her. Somebody did that, four months ago, in a room I've never been in, and I wasn't there for one second of it.

He'd argue politics with me until the paint came off the wall and enjoy every minute of it. He was dead certain about everything he believed and so was I, about different things, and neither one of us ever moved the other an inch, and it never cost us a single morning.

I don't have anything wiser than that. He was my best friend in that city and he died of the thing that's killing everybody, and I found out four months late off a screen.

---

Silesh got into the meth.

Ace said you wouldn't have known him. Took him all the way down to a rail.

I want to say who that man was before I say the rest, because the rest is going to sit on top of him otherwise and he doesn't deserve to be flattened under it. Silesh was the smoothest human being I have ever stood next to. Somali, born and raised, a ladies' man and a shameless self-promoter and funnier than anybody in that crew including me, and I'll tell you what he was worth on a floor: if Silesh took over your live call, you had a clean forty-five minutes. That's not a figure of speech. Forty-five minutes, *sweetie* this and *doll* that, and you could go gamble or smoke or lie down in an empty office and come back and the deal was closed. He'd sit there working a mark with a glass of water beside him and never once touch it, and I teased that man without an ounce of mercy for years and he loved every second of it, because he was the finest sport God ever built.

One evening the police stopped him out on a Vegas highway. He sat in his car through a standoff that ran for hours. When the hours were up he put a gun to his own head, right there behind the wheel.

That was September.

I have gone back and looked at where I was in September, and I wish I hadn't. That's the month I was sleeping on the sand in San Diego and hauling cans to a recycling center, and then panhandling up the price of a bar of soap and a razor so I could clean myself up enough to walk south across the busiest border in the country. Which means there is some particular evening in there — some specific night I could probably name if anybody ever handed me the date — where I was walking twenty-three miles down a black coast road in a stolen-looking black t-shirt, feeling sorry for myself and half hoping something out there would find me, and about three hundred miles northeast of that road, Silesh had already been sitting in a car for hours and had already made up his mind.

Both of us alone on a highway at night. One of us kept walking.

There is no lesson in that. I've looked for one.

---

And John-J.

Brain aneurysm, last month. On the mat.

John-J was sixty-something and he was our old stray and he was the worst salesman God ever sat down at a telephone. That is not me being unkind — that was the standing joke and he was in on it and he'd have told you the same thing himself. He laid out joke after joke into the usual merciful silence, undeterred as a man can be, and he had the biggest heart of any of us by a distance that wasn't close. He was the only single one in that whole crew. He rented a room in that city for four hundred a month and there was no bed in it. Never was. He slept on a mat on the floor for years and never once complained about it in my hearing.

He died on that mat, in that room, alone, last month.

And here is the thing I cannot put down, and I've turned it over about a hundred times.

The last time I saw John-J was the best night any of us ever had. The Presidential Suite at the Orleans, top floor, panoramic windows, a jacuzzi full of people and a wet bar and the whole loud confederation of us howling until the sun came up over the valley. Every last one of us got in that hot tub except John-J. And at dawn, when we all found our way over to those windows to watch the light come up, somebody went looking for him and found him laid out cold on the floor in front of the bathroom.

We laughed. God, we laughed. We stood over that man on the carpet at six in the morning and gave him hell about it, and it was funny, and he'd have said it was funny.

And then four years later he laid down on a floor in a room with no bed and nobody found him for I don't know how long, and nobody laughed, and nobody was standing over him at all.

That's the same man. That's the same floor, more or less. The whole difference between those two mornings is whether there was anybody in the room.

---

Out of the five of us — me, Ritchie, Ace, John-J, and Silesh, the found family that took in a wanted, broke, untouchable man and called him one of their own without ever once asking what he'd done to end up on their doorstep — there's Ace, and there's me.

That's it. That's what's left.

And it was Ace who had to write it out. The lone one left, sitting down to be the one who tells it, three times over, to a man who'd gone silent nine months before and might well be dead himself for all he knew.

I wasn't there for a single one of them. That's the thing that sits on my chest and won't move. I missed all three. I was up on a mountain counting cartel dope onto a table for the men who set a dog on me for sport, and about three hundred miles north my brothers were going down one at a time, and I didn't know, and there is not one thing I could have done if I had.

I've tried to make that last clause help. It doesn't help. It's true and it does not help even slightly, and I've quit expecting it to.

---

I found all of this out in the same handful of days I learned Zaul was alive and Glenda wasn't dead.

Think about the shape of that for a second, because I've had months to and I still can't get it flat. Inside of about a week, every single person I had buried in my head sat up and started breathing, and every single person I'd left breathing went in the ground. The whole ledger reversed itself at once. Zaul, who I'd mourned at an empty call center — alive, in a rehab tee, apologizing. Glenda, who I'd grieved in a ditch in the desert while men urinated ten feet from my head — alive, in a folding chair, laughing about her sister's laptop. And Ritchie and Silesh and John-J, who were all of them alive and well and going about their business the last time I had any information at all — gone. All three. Months gone. Buried by strangers while I was on a mountain being taught not to look a man in the eye.

I sat in a plastic chair in a Mexicali rehab and tried to hold every piece of that in two hands and I couldn't. I still can't. I've had counseling since and I've talked about it in English to a man paid to listen and I have not gotten it any flatter than I've got it right here.

This world is crazy. I don't have anything wiser to lay over the top of it. It's just plain crazy, and it does not check with you first, and the whole time you're gone it keeps right on making its decisions about the people you love.

---

There's a version of me that would have gone straight out and drunk on this. I want that on the record too, because I don't get to write a chapter about three dead friends and leave myself out of it looking solemn.

The old arithmetic was sitting right there, the same as it has sat there my entire life, and it was making its case: three men are dead and you missed all of it and there is nothing you can do and here is a feeling too big to hold, so put it in the cellar and go in to dinner. Take it, close the ledger, eat. That procedure has run every catastrophe of my adult life since I was seven years old in a living room with a handful of my sister's hair.

I was four weeks off a mountain and locked inside a building with a fence around it and nothing to drink within a quarter mile.

Which is the only reason I didn't. I want to be exactly that honest about it. It wasn't strength and it wasn't growth and it wasn't one single thing I'd earned. It was a fence, and a piss test on Mondays, and a good man who'd held a door open, and me having finally arrived in the one place on earth where the old procedure couldn't be run.

Sometimes you don't get better. Sometimes you just get somewhere they won't let you get worse, and you stand there long enough for the difference to start meaning something.
