# Chapter 48 — Zach

I've had a lot of time to reflect since sitting in this Mexican rehab. It's funny — I guess I saved the best chapter for last. I'm not saying this to be dramatic; I'm saying it because it's true. It's also funny, and it's also going to make me cry. It's about my nephew, Zach, and it's about the best thing that ever happened to me. It took me a long time to get here, but I'm finally here. Family is hard for me to talk about. It's hard for me to talk about anything real. But Zach is different.

Everybody in that family said the same thing about Zach, his whole life and mine:

*“He's little Clay.”*

They said it about his face, which was fair — the kid came out looking like me and never stopped, to the point where relatives who hadn't seen either of us in years would get us confused in photographs.

And then, somewhere along the way, they started saying it the other way:

*“You're going to end up like your uncle Clay.”*

His mother said that to him. My mother said it. My father said it. My own sister Niki, who has bailed me out of more holes than any human being alive, said it to her son right in her own kitchen. She wasn't being cruel and she wasn't wrong to be afraid. She’d watched my train wreck her entire life. She had the data.

So the boy grew up with my face, my habits, and my name used as a cautionary tale in his own home.

He is not me. I need that stamped on the page before anything else in this chapter. He is not me and he never was, and everybody who said it was looking at the wrong things.

He's smarter than I am. Not by a little. And he is honest in a way I have never once managed for twenty-four consecutive hours in forty-six years on this earth.

***

Let me tell you what kind of honest, because it's the funniest thing in this book and it's also the entire point of the chapter.

Zach told his mother about his first sexual experience, and plenty more thereafter. He told her about a lot of other things too — far too many to recount in this book, every one of them leaving me completely floored.

He told her. Voluntarily. He walked right into the room and reported it, the way a kid reports a grade on a report card.

Now, I want to walk you through what my own brain does with that, because the two reactions explain the entire chasm between him and me.

My first reaction is pure delight. It is, flat out, one of the funniest things I have ever heard. Looney tunes. The kid was out of his mind.

Then the second reaction hits about a half-second later, and it isn't funny at all. It took me twenty years to understand it.

He wasn't being reckless. He wasn't bragging or showing off. There was zero calculation in it of any kind, which is exactly why it was unthinkable to a man like me. At that age, with that same mother, I would have run four separate defensive plays before the kitchen door even swung shut: *What does she know? What can she actually prove? What is the absolute smallest piece of truth I can throw at her to make her go away?* That wasn't a conscious strategy; that was just the machine. It kicked on and ran before I even walked into the room.

Zach didn't have the machine. He had a human being in front of him and no wall between them, so the truth just came out.

That is not a defect. I spent decades writing it off as one — *the kid just can't get out of his own way* — and I said it affectionately and I said it often. But what I actually meant, if I had been man enough to admit it, was: *the kid has no exits, and I have never once in my life stood in a room without measuring the back door.*

***

Here's the other thing, and it's the lesson he taught me:

If there was trouble, Zach walked straight at it.

Something would go sideways — a job, a girl, money, a screw-up he'd made — and he went and handled it. That day. He’d jump in the truck. He’d make the call. He’d walk right into the room where the ugly conversation was waiting and have the damn conversation.

I go to my room, shut the door, and pray the problem magically disappears.

I mean that literally, and I mean it as the summary of my entire adult life. I have never in my life walked straight at a hard thing. I go sideways. I take the detour. I sit with the mess and construct a sanitized version that's easier to swallow, and then I get in a car and haul that story somewhere else. I have crossed international borders just to dodge conversations that Zach would have settled before lunch.

He's twenty-some years younger than me, he was born with that spine, and I have never managed to acquire it. I’d say I admire it, but "admire" is too soft and safe. It's the only trait anyone in my family ever possessed that I actually envied, and I never once told him so until right now.

He never used it as a weapon against me. Not once. Not in a lifetime, not in a private moment, and never sideways at a holiday dinner table with the whole family listening.

He went right on defending me.

That's the mystery about Zach I still can't fully fathom. The family would get wound up about me at a table — and understand, they had every right to; mostly they weren't even being mean, they were just exhausted — and Zach would go to bat. Every single time. *“He's doing really well now. He's got a great job. He's close to his friends. He's got a little family out there.”*

He would say it into a room full of people who had all long since done the math. It fell on deaf ears every single time, and the next time it came up, he’d step right back up to the plate. I only ever found out because he’d tell me later, still genuinely offended on my behalf.

He had a version of me in his head that he built when he was a little boy, and he desperately wanted it to be real. I know that. And what I've had to make peace with is that there came a point — I don't know the year, I don't know the day, and I won't insult him by guessing — when he realized it wasn't true. When he had access to the exact same rap sheet everybody else at that table had.

And he kept going to bat anyway.

That’s not simple belief. Belief is easy. That is something far heavier, and I don't have a word for it. Knowing full well who I was, he chose to stay right where he had always stood.

***

I called him my rock for years before I finally understood why he was able to be one.

It’s this, and I've only just figured it out:

Zach is the one person on this earth I have never had to lie to.

I want to be precise here, because I've spent this entire book being merciless about my own dishonesty. I'm sure I've shaded a detail or dodged an admission I wasn't ready to face. But I never had to put on an act for him, because I never had to fear being judged. I could tell that kid the raw, ugly truth of wherever I was — the benders, the empty bank accounts, the jail cells, the disastrous relationships, the sheer wreckage of it all — and I could lay it out flat without spinning it. 

There was no angle to work with him. There was nothing to hustle out of him. He wasn't a mark, he wasn't a bank, and he wasn't an audience to charm. He was just a kid who loved me unconditionally, so the salesman inside me shut up and stayed off. I knew there was no condemnation on the other end of the line — just a conversation with someone who loved me and wanted to hear about my life. He's the only person I've ever had where I didn't feel the need to be more or less than what I was. I could just be me. I don't know if he ever realized what a gift that was.

Ten years of that. One completely clean ledger.

He has a son now.

Recently. And I wasn't there. That is a sentence I am going to be carrying for a very long time.

He met Peyton — and I actually got to watch that unfold, which is more than I can say for most things in my family. She is a genuinely wonderful woman who has endured a staggering amount of Zach's chaos and stayed right by his side. Because he has plenty of chaos. He is honest as the daylight is long, and he still routinely shoots himself in the foot. Those two things aren't contradictory; they’re the exact same trait, and it’s why she loves him, and why I love him.

I don't know her well enough to capture her on paper, and I want to be honest about why: it wasn't a scheduling conflict. I simply wasn't around. I can give you a photographic, sensory description of a dark alley in Anchorage where I got jumped, but I couldn't tell you three basic facts about the woman my nephew loves. For the entire span of years when normal people build family ties, I was in a motel, or a casino, or a barn in Michigan, or a bus with blacked-out windows on a Mexican hill.

What I do know is that she holds the heart of the one person on this planet who has shown me more grace than anyone else. That earns her a permanent place in my soul whether I ever get to know her properly or not. She is good for him, and that’s more than enough for me. Welcome to the family, kid.

There is a baby boy in South Carolina right now, and I have never held him.

***

I would give my right arm for him. I would chop it off today without a second thought if it helped him.

But I can't be in his life right now. Not from here, and not as the man I currently am. Here is the lesson it took a razor-wire fence, a plastic chair, and two therapy sessions a week to finally drill into my skull:

I can't hold that baby until I put myself back together. The only thing I have ever actually given Zach is an example, and he copied every single one I set. He copied my expressions. He copied my mouth. He took his first drink the very week he turned twenty-one, right in his mother's house, because Uncle Clay was sitting there and being Uncle Clay was the standard he was chasing.

The only thing I can give that boy now is a version of myself that is actually worth copying.

That's it. That's the grand revelation — the least original thought a man has ever had, arriving at age forty-six, inside a Mexican rehab, about twenty-five years too late to do Zach any good. I am nowhere near that man yet, but that is the work. I know Zach will hold me to it, but he won't disown me if I stumble. He will keep a seat for me at his table, and the only person disappointed will be me. That’s what real love looks like. I've never been that kind of man, but I'm trying. I plan on meeting my great-nephew in a few years.

Zach was my Clayita long before there ever was a Clayita.

I've thought about that constantly in this clinic. A half-wild creature that decided I was his person without taking a vote on it. Who followed me everywhere for reasons I couldn't comprehend, who ate whatever scraps I had to offer and stayed loyal through the worst of it.

I found that once in a stray kitten on a cartel mountain when I was down to nothing, and it kept me alive, so I wrote an entire chapter about her.

I’d been getting that exact same love from a boy in Charleston for twenty-five years, and I never once had the sense to write it down while he was keeping me breathing. 

That is how I will remember him. That is the truest thing in this whole book.
