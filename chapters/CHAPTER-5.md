The schedule alone should have told me what I'd signed onto. Two three-hour shifts — noon to three, then five to eight — with a two-hour break carved out of the middle of the day like somebody's idea of a reward. To a working man that's a split shift, a nuisance, dead time you can't do anything with. To me it was the most beautiful roster anybody had ever handed me. Two hours, dead center of the day, to walk down to the store, grab a sandwich and a couple of tall boys, smoke a joint on the stroll back, and ease into my second shift loose and warm and grinning into the headset. They were paying me to work six hours and drink through the seam in the middle of them. I thought I'd died and gone to heaven. I should've recognized the address.

And the sun never went down. It was the back half of June, a few days short of the solstice, and Anchorage was drowning in light twenty-four hours a day — three in the morning lit up like six in the evening, the whole city running on no sleep and not knowing it. I was on Alaska time, calling California homeowners an hour ahead of me, so the minute I picked up at noon their afternoon was already moving. Nothing lined up. Not the clock, not the light, not the work, not the drinking. I just rode the whole crooked thing and called it freedom.

Because I was killing it. Right out of the gate.

They called us closers, and I never once stopped finding that funny. In Vegas a closer closed — ran the card, took the money, sent a man home with a package and a verification recording around his neck. Up here I wasn't closing a dime. I was closing an appointment. That was the entire job: talk a stranger into sitting down with a solar rep and agreeing to a soft credit pull — a "credit pass," we called it, like it was a backstage laminate and not his FICO score — and for every one I booked with that pass attached, they paid me a hundred dollars. I never brought a single dollar into the building. I wasn't the man who took the money. I was just the man who built the doorway somebody else would someday walk a sale through. The opportunity. That was me. I provided the opportunity.

And the hardest part of it should have been impossible. Twenty minutes. That's all the rope I got. Some opener the homeowner had never laid eyes on would warm-transfer them across a border to a stranger named Clay, and inside of twenty minutes I had to get that stranger comfortable enough to hand me the last four of their Social Security number and their date of birth so we could run the pass. Sit with that a second. Think how hard your own mother would be to talk out of those two numbers, cold, over the phone, by a man she'd known for the length of a sitcom. That was the whole degree of difficulty. And I did it eight times in two days.

Eight appointments, a credit pass clipped to every one. The next closer in the room booked three. Three hundred dollars my first day, five hundred my second — eight hundred dollars in forty-eight hours, paid out in cash at the end of each shift, and I hadn't told one lie to earn a nickel of it. That was the part that kept catching in my throat. After six years in the Vegas rooms, this was easy as breathing, and clean. Nobody was getting steered into the basement of Circus Circus. Nobody was getting a Restaurant.com card called dining. I was setting a free appointment for a real product with a real company, and a stranger was thanking me for it, and I was getting rich. I had no idea, sitting there those first two nights, how rich that number was about to get.

The openers were all in Mexico, every last one of them, and they were paid like it — a fraction of what the operation handed me for the exact same call. There were only two US-based closers in the whole machine, and I was the one who could close. The openers earned a bonus on top of their wage for every transfer that turned into a booked appointment — twenty-five dollars a head, I think it was — which meant I wasn't just a coworker to them. I was money. I was their commission walking around on two legs. And God, you should have seen them light up. My WhatsApp would go off like a slot machine every time I booked one of their transfers — a flood of it, all caps and exclamation points and little *gracias* from people I'd never lay eyes on, celebrating me from two thousand miles south like I'd just hit the royal for the whole room. They wanted me on their phones. Some of them, if a transfer landed on the other closer, would disconnect the closer and dial back through until the line dropped onto me. Fought over me. Disconnected a working closer just to get me.

King of the castle. Again. That old warm feeling of being the most wanted man in the building — and me too high on it to notice that the warmth was coming up the wire from Mexico, and that being wanted had never once in my life turned out to be the same thing as being safe.

The whole thing was brand new, and it ran like it. Door-to-door appointment setting had died in the COVID years — nobody was knocking on strangers' doors anymore — so the operation had graduated to the phones, and this was only their second phone campaign ever. The first one they'd run with all-Mexican closers and it hadn't hit; the numbers were soft, the deals didn't stick. So they'd made the call to spend real American dollars on real American voices, and that was the entire reason a man like me had a seat at all. Manny was out of Vegas — that's how he knew the mini-vacation rooms even existed, how he knew the precise words to drop into a job ad to summon a creature like me up out of the snow. Manny. The one who posted it. The one who'd interviewed me over video and hired me on the spot. He'd reached into the same desert I'd crawled out of and pulled my exact type to the top of the world, and neither of us thought a thing of it. Birds of a feather, recognizing the feather.

The first time I saw her was on a group video training call. Twenty-some little boxes stacked on a screen, half of them dark, openers logging in from kitchens and back bedrooms all over Mexico, Manny droning through the new script — and one of those lit squares was Glenda. She was two years younger than me and beautiful. I'll be straight about it, because she'd want me to be: that was the entire content of my first thought. Not *the best opener in the building.* Not *careful with that one.* Just — beautiful, and me filing it away with everything else I was sure I already had a handle on. I looked at her the way you look at a thing you don't yet know is going to cost you everything. Which is to say I barely looked at all.

Then I heard her. The very first appointment I ever closed up there came across as a transfer from her — my third call on the job — and the first thing I learned about Glenda's voice was that she was drunk. I could hear it straight through the line: loose, low, a half-step too warm with a homeowner she'd known for all of ninety seconds, wasted at her desk in the middle of a workday and somehow better at the job for it. She'd warmed that Californian so soft and so certain the deal was half-closed before it ever reached me. I caught what she lobbed and put it away clean, my first of the eight, feeling like a natural — and somewhere under all that easy warmth I clicked the voice to the face from the screen and felt something turn over that had nothing to do with the appointment. I knew right then I had to meet her. It wasn't love. I didn't expect to fall in love with anybody. I just wanted to see her naked. That was the whole size of it that first day, the third call of a brand-new job.

That was Glenda. She worked out of a house in Mexico; I worked out of the States — soon enough out of a hole in the wall in Alaska — and between us ran a corporate phone system built to move strangers from her mouth to my ear. We were about to use it for something it was never made for. Best opener in the building, best closer in the building, both of us a little drunk and both of us dead sure we were running the machine instead of the other way around. I figured we'd make each other a lot of money. I was right about that part — and I have never in my life been so right about a small thing and so stone-blind to the enormous one standing directly behind it. What we'd end up with wasn't a romance so much as two drunks falling for each other through a phone system, one transferred call at a time, very good at exactly one thing together: feeding each other's worst habits.

She didn't have to fake a word to get to me. Remember that. Hold onto it. Everything that came after, she meant.

Saturday came in like a coronation. The word came down the WhatsApp before I was even off the mattress: double commissions, all day. Every credit pass worth two hundred dollars instead of one. *Holy shit.* After the week I'd just had, I sat there running the math on a pot I hadn't won yet, and the number was obscene. I was going to make bank. Real, stupid bank. There was just one loose thread left in the whole gleaming picture — the Airbnb was up Monday, and I had to be out.

And I hadn't accounted for what this town cost. Nobody warns you that Alaska in high summer runs double — solstice money, everything marked up for the few bright weeks people actually want to be there, hotels worst of all. I priced a few and felt the floor tilt. Then I found a hostel near downtown for forty dollars a night, and I booked it for two weeks sight unseen — the same brilliant due diligence that had already landed me in the most dangerous neighborhood in the city.

Forty dollars, it turned out, bought you a pod. Japanese style — and I am not stretching this for the page — a hole in the wall you climbed up into. You could not stand inside it. It had a bed, and a little shelf of a desk jutting out from the wall that you could work at if you sat on the mattress with your laptop, headset on, calling California homeowners out of a slot the size of a coffin with a reading light screwed into it. There was a quiet day room with a real table I could use when the building emptied out. And I looked at the listing photos of that hole in the wall — a man who'd just made eight hundred dollars in two days and pissed some of it away already on bars and a dispensary and a city that cost double — and I thought: *perfect.*

That's the word I used. Perfect. I reserved it for the full two weeks, set check-in for Monday, and closed the laptop. Saturday morning, the light already flat and bright as noon, and me standing in that Airbnb feeling like I'd solved my whole life before breakfast. Housing handled. The biggest payday I'd ever seen a few hours out. King of the castle, crown and all. I've told you what my luck really was — nothing but the house letting me keep playing. That morning was the morning the house decided to collect.

Before my first shift I set out for breakfast.

There's a Denny's about a twenty-minute walk from the Airbnb, same bad side of town, and I made the trip on foot the way I'd made every trip that week — loose and unbothered, a dead man strolling and grinning at the scenery. Big breakfast. Coffee. On the way back I stocked up for the day: a joint, a few tall boys, a bag of snacks to ride out both shifts. Then I started home through my own neighborhood, arms full of goodies, the joint lit, the sun up, two hundred dollars a pass waiting on me at noon.

That's the last thing I've got. The joint. The walk. The math.

Next thing in my head is the rose bush.

I came to in it — flat on my back in somebody's landscaping, thorns hooked into my arms, my shirt torn half off me, my face wet and stinging where I'd gone down into the branches. It took me a long moment to understand I was awake, and another to understand I was empty. I mean *empty.* The Airbnb key, gone. My wallet — my whole life was in that wallet — gone. My phone. My cash. Every beer and every snack and the joint. Everything I'd carried out of that house and everything I'd carried into that state, stripped off me while I was somewhere else inside my own skull, and not one frame of it left in my memory. No face. No hands. Nothing.

It was still before noon. I was maybe three blocks from the Airbnb. And I was about as alone as a man gets.

Here's the full inventory of what I had left: nothing. No phone to call a soul. No wallet, no ID, no way on God's earth to prove I was a man who belonged anywhere, least of all in the rented townhome up the block. I didn't know a single neighbor by name. I had no number for the owner — I'd learn later, off somebody on the street, that the owner lived down in California, which was about the most fitting detail in the whole disaster — and nobody on that block had it either. It was strictly an Airbnb. A lockbox and a stranger's house and not one human being within three thousand miles who could let me in.

So I spent the next three hours trying to break into my own life.

There was a window I could see standing open — an upstairs bedroom, cracked for air. A neighbor lent me a ladder, and I need you to picture this thing: one of those little wooden folding jobs, a step stool with ambitions, and me balanced on the very top of it still a good five feet shy of the sill, swaying, reaching up at an open window like it owed me money. So I tried to jump it. Stood on that top step and threw myself at the ledge and came down hard enough to damn near snap my ankle. No water. No food. Nothing in me but a hangover, a head wound, and the slow cold understanding that my debit card — a thousand-some dollars still on it, the last money I had in this world — was out there in the wild in somebody else's hand, bleeding out by the minute, and there was not one thing I could do about it.

Because everything that could save me was on the wrong side of that door. My laptop. My whole job. If I could just get inside, I could open the dialer on my PC and at least kill the card before it emptied. That was the entire prize now. Not the double commissions. Not two hundred a pass. Not the king-of-the-castle week. Just: get in, cancel the card, drink a glass of water.

So I worked it like a man losing his mind in slow motion. Sit on the step for twenty minutes. Get up, walk the house, try a window, try the door, claw at the lock with nothing in my hands, fail, sit back down. Wait on a car, a neighbor, anybody with a phone or a name or the owner's number. Nobody came. Nobody had it. And the sun didn't move, because the sun never moved up there — just hung in the sky and watched me with the rest of the block.

By the time it got past six I'd stopped thinking straight, if I ever started. And I made the single dumbest decision in a life that's mostly a monument to them. I was going to put my elbow through a window.

I picked the thickest one in the place, naturally. Drove my elbow into it, and it did not politely crack — it went off like a shotgun, the whole street ringing with the sound, and in the half-second after I understood I'd just announced myself to every soul in the most heavily policed poor neighborhood in Anchorage. I didn't care. I was through the frame in seconds, opening my arms up on the glass on the way in, and I dropped down into my own rented kitchen bleeding from about nine new places and could not have been more relieved.

First thing I did was pour a glass of water. Stood at the sink and drank it down like water was the thing that had been wrong all day. It couldn't have been five minutes. I was still standing there — glass in my hand, blood running down to my knuckles — when I looked up at the window over the sink, the one I hadn't broken.

There was a cop on the other side of it. Looking right at me.

So I did the only thing a man with my history does when the law turns up on the far side of the glass. I waved. Polite as church. Gave him the little half-smile you hand a cop when you've already decided, against every piece of evidence stacked up around you, that this is going to go just fine. *Sorry — I got jumped. Robbed. They took everything, I had no way in, no way to reach the owner.* I said it through the window like it was an explanation, like it was a thing that would land.

And somewhere in the middle of saying it I caught my own reflection in the glass next to his face and understood exactly what he was looking at. A man bleeding out of nine places. Shirt half torn off his back, thorns still hooked in his arms, face shredded, filthy, swaying, standing in a stranger's kitchen he'd just put an elbow through the window to get into. I didn't look like the renter. I looked like the precise creature this whole neighborhood gets warned about — the homeless guy who'd break into an empty house for a glass of water. And I want that on the record: I was, at that exact moment, holding the glass of water.

"Okay," he said, easy, reasonable. "Can you open the front door for me and step outside so we can talk?"

Sure. Of course. I'd open the door and I'd explain it and he'd understand, because I have spent my entire life dead certain that the next room is the one where I finally get to explain. I walked to that door working out which words to lead with.

There was no conversation. I opened it and I was spun, shoved, told to put my hands behind my back, and folded into the back of an Alaskan cop car before I'd gotten one syllable of the speech out. Cuffed and stuffed. The only mercy the night saw fit to hand me — and I take my mercies wherever they fall — was that this one didn't reek of Joop.

Then I sat. Two hours in that backseat while the longest day of the year refused to end outside the windows. Every so often a cop would crack the door, ask me exactly one question, and shut it again before I could turn the answer into a story. *You got the owner's number?* No. Door closes. *You know the owner's name?* No. Door closes. *Any ID in the house?* No — a laptop, I can pull up a receipt if you let me get to my laptop. Door closes. Each one a door, an actual door, swinging shut on me one more time, on a day that had been nothing but locked doors since the rose bush.

Out past the glass there were four cruisers now, maybe more, every light bar running, painting the block red and blue under a sun too high to let the colors mean anything. Two of the cops were carrying what looked to me like AK-47s. For me. For the bleeding man who'd wanted a glass of water. They stood in a knot out in the street and talked, and I couldn't catch a word of it, and I sat there in my cuffs running the same broken arithmetic I'd run my whole life. *Surely.* Surely once they sorted it out they'd laugh. Surely a man can't go to jail for breaking into his own Airbnb. Surely. The best closer in the building, the unmovable mark, the man who'd seen the wire from both ends — and I sat there genuinely certain I couldn't be closed by four cop cars and a felony. A closer doesn't get closed. I'd staked my whole life on that one.

One of them peeled off the group and came to my window. He had good news and bad news, he said. He didn't extend me the courtesy of choosing the order.

Bad news: I was going to jail.

*Fuck my life.*

Good news: they'd reached the owner — down in California, naturally, three thousand miles from the door I'd just bled all over — and the owner had confirmed I was the renter. So they weren't going to charge me with breaking and entering. Just destruction of property. The owner wanted to press charges. And I was not allowed back on the property.

Sit with the shape of that. I'd been jumped and robbed and stripped of every last thing I owned in broad daylight, spent the longest afternoon of my life clawing to get back into a house I was paying for, and finally bled my way in for a glass of tap water — and the verdict on all of it was that I was the criminal. I'd told you what my luck really was. Nothing but the house letting me keep playing. This was the morning it reached across the table and raked the whole pot back.

They booked me into county, put me in a cell, and told me I'd see a judge on Monday. Okay. Great. I lay down on that bunk and felt the old machine spin right back up: *this won't be too bad.* Surely the judge would take one look and see straight through it and cut me loose. Surely the owner had somebody nearby with a key — they'd let me in, I'd grab the suitcase and the laptop, I'd get hold of work and lay the whole insane story out and pray to God I still had a job to lay it out to. I had the place rented for two weeks. I had time. I had a plan, lying there on a steel bunk in Anchorage with not one thing left to my name — the exact same sermon I'd preached myself the weekend my job got erased. *It's a glitch, I'll straighten it out Monday.* Same unshakable faith that the next morning was finally going to break my way. I never learned.

Monday morning came — the weekend swallowed whole on a steel bunk, Sunday a dead day with no court and nothing to do but wait on it — and the judge was a face on a TV screen, and I want to be precise about how this went: I did not get to say one single word. Not one. She read the case off a page, postponed the whole thing to Wednesday, and the screen went dark. And the jail, it turned out, was still scared out of its mind of COVID — scared enough that we weren't let out of our cells to eat, to walk, to do anything at all. You ate where you slept and you stayed there.

So now I really might be finished. Three more days of work gone, maybe four, and that was *if* they actually turned me loose Wednesday. How could this be happening? It isn't a question. There's no answer down at the bottom of it. It's just the sound a man makes on the inside while the floor keeps dropping out from under him.

Monday and Tuesday were the two longest days of my life — the longest days of the year, the sun nailed to the sky over the whole state, spent in a two-man box where you couldn't tell day from night and it wouldn't have changed a thing. My cellmate, by every measure but one a perfectly normal guy — we'd talk, he made sense — was a serial masturbator who'd roll over with no warning and no shame and go to work three feet away, indifferent to whether I watched. Too stupid to invent. But that was the exact texture of my life — wall to wall with this kind of insane shit, and me the one common denominator in every room it ever happened in.

Wednesday the judge had the owner on the phone, live. The owner would be in town Saturday to come let me into the house. *Saturday.* I lost it, as much as you can lose it at a face on a screen — that's unacceptable, my whole life is in that house, I need the laptop, I need to work, there is no version of this where I sit here until Saturday. Didn't move her an inch. And then she did me one better: a protective order. I was not to go anywhere near the property without a police escort. Barred by force of law from the home that contained everything I owned...again. I'd been here before — a court order locking me out of a house I had paid for.  New state, new stranger holding the key, same fool on the wrong side of the door. The geographic cure, working exactly as designed.

*Fuck my life. Again.*

I'd be released the next morning, they told me, or the afternoon. And I lay there and thought: released to *what?* No money. No debit card — that one was already out in the wild, bleeding down to zero. No ID, and no way on earth to replace an ID with no ID, that perfect little snake of a loop. No way to so much as prove who I was long enough to climb into the forty-dollar Japanese pod I had already paid for — a full week of it now, paid up front, every night of it spent in a cell instead. The one roof I had left in the world, and I couldn't get under it. Meantime I had free room and board, courtesy of Alaska's finest establishment. The jail.

After my cellmate finished his business, I asked him the only practical question I had left: what time do they cut a man loose around here? "All depends," he said. "Could be as early as two in the morning." And I lay there and prayed it wouldn't be. Two a.m. in Alaska is cold as death even in June, and I owned, at that point, a torn-up t-shirt and a pair of filthy shorts and nothing else. I was hoping with everything I had for a mid-afternoon checkout, someplace warm to walk out into. But there's no front desk in a place like that. Nobody to call. No request you get to put in. You go when the door says you go.

I drifted off at last, my celly — full of cum and bad news in equal measure — squeezing in one final go before lights out. And then the cell door cracked open like a gunshot, and a voice said:

"Mills. Go home."

I lay there a second in the dark.

I had no home.

It was two in the morning.
