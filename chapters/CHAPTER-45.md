# Chapter 45 — The Other Omar

Like I said, I came into that city with no plan. I knew how to get to exactly one place, and that was the rehab.

I still knew the owner. And here's a thing I have to tell you, because life doesn't care how clean your symbols are: his name was Omar. The same name as the man who'd locked me in a bus and stood by while I was beaten and run down by a dog for sport — that exact name belonged to one of the kindest men I have ever known, who lived in a house directly across the street from the rehab and was, in every way that counts, the other Omar's opposite in this world.

I'd been inside that house. I'd met his kids. He was the one who'd given us the okay to run our little call center out of his building with Zaul back at the start of all this, even though he wanted nothing to do with the operation itself. He also kept apartments around the city that he rented out to guys finishing their time in the program, and that was my whole play: get to Omar, and pitch him on letting me hole up in one of them for a few weeks.

So I walked the last of the way to his door and knocked.

You have to picture what I looked like. Four days in the desert. No water for the back half of it, no sleep for any of it, coked down to nothing, lips split and swollen, filthy to the bone. Not good. Not good in a way that would terrify a stranger.

His kid answered — two years older now than the last time I'd seen her and showing every day of it, somewhere around eleven, and I'm ashamed to say I couldn't pull her name up out of the wreckage in my head. These were kids used to seeing men in rough shape, just not at their front door. Inside the rehab, behind the chain-link, sure. Not standing on the welcome mat. I rasped out a question for her dad. She turned and yelled for him, and I thought, *thank God, he's home.*

Omar surfaced. A man in his forties like me, a kid at heart, the kind of guy who's always got a joke loaded and ready to fire. No joke now. He took one look at me and the color drained straight out of his face. 

*“What happened to you?”*

And I came completely apart on his doorstep.

"Omar," I said, shaking, "you wouldn't believe it, man. I was kidnapped by the cartel. I think Glenda's dead. I don't know what to do. I just walked four days out of Rosarito and I've got nowhere to go. I need help. I just need a safe place for a little while."

His eyes went wide as saucers. I was sobbing by then, full on, the whole weight of the last six months finally catching up to me on a concrete doorstep in Mexicali. 

*“It's okay, man,”* he said, soft, the way you talk to an injured animal. *“Come on. Let's get you cleaned up. Get you a shower, get you something to eat, get you some sleep. Then we'll talk.”*

He walked me across the street. I was past arguing. I was past everything. "Okay, Omar. Thank you."

***

*“You have to sign this before you go in,”* he told me in the intake office, almost apologetic. *“It's a legal thing. Don't worry about any of it — I'll be back in the morning and we'll figure it out.”*

I signed.

Again. The exact same kind of paper I'd signed walking into this same world two years earlier — the admission form that turns a human being into an intake number with the scratch of a pen. I'd come down to Mexico the first time to *use* a facility like this, to run my hustle out the back room. Now here I was signing my way in for real, with everything I owned crammed into a dirty book bag and a dead woman's name echoing in my skull.

First thing they do is the urine screen. I popped hot, obviously. Coke and weed — it was the only fuel that had kept my feet moving across forty miles of sand. And then, searching my pockets, they found the baggie of weed I'd swiped off Omar's desk on the mountain and completely, genuinely forgotten was in my jacket.

Just like that, the good Omar stopped believing a single word. 

Why would he? A skeletal, strung-out gringo turns up at your family's front door with a wild, rambling tale about cartel kidnappings, desert escapes, and dead girlfriends, and then he fails a drug test and you pull baggies out of his clothes. There's a much simpler explanation that fits every single one of those facts, and it's the one anyone running a rehab would land on: he’s not an escapee. He’s just a junkie who hit rock bottom and spun a Hollywood yarn to cover a binge. 

I don't blame Omar for seeing it that way. In his shoes, I’d have thought the exact same thing.

They stripped me, hosed me down, and locked me into the detox ward for two weeks. No Omar. No laptop. No contact with the outside world. And still — after everything, after all the miles and blood — not ten words of Spanish in my vocabulary.

***

When they walked me back toward the detox bunks, we passed it.

The call center. Dark now. Dead quiet. Ten plywood, duct-taped desks gathering dust in the dim light — the exact room where the entire chain of events was forged. The deal, the reason I crossed the border, the very first link in a chain that dragged me all the way to a black-painted bus on a godforsaken mountain two hours west.

*That's where it all started,* I thought, stopping in the hallway to stare through the glass. I finally let myself do the math I'd been running from for months.

If Zaul hadn't gotten flashy. If he hadn't flashed cash, bought that BMW, and paraded a gold chain around town. None of this happens. I'm still on a quiet beach in Puerto Peñasco. America is still going to school with her little friends. We still have that patched-together family, the three of us — the closest thing to real peace I had ever managed to steal. Maybe Rose still has her car wreck, but if she does, we drive up together, drop off cash to fix it, and drive right back home. Because the money never dries up. Because the man running the machine never vanishes into thin air.

Standing there outside the dark office, a wave of pure fury washed over me. And then, right behind it, came a heavy, hollow sadness. Sad for Zaul, who I was certain was rotting in an unmarked grave somewhere just like Glenda. 

And when I got down to the bone of it, I knew I couldn't put the blame on him. Not honestly. Zaul vanishing was just the match dropped on the floor. I’m the one who built the entire house out of dry kindling and soaked it in gasoline. Every single choice after he disappeared belonged to me. The drinking, the coke, the desperate holes in Rosarito, the mounting debt, the lies — every misstep that walked me right into the hands of that cartel was mine. I could have made a hundred different choices. I don't know how things would have ended, but I know they were *my* choices to make. That’s enough to keep a man honest about whose wreckage he's standing in.

***

Two weeks in the detox tank, sweating out the mountain, and then they moved me into general population. Back on the wrong side of the cage.

Two more weeks of that — a thin mattress on a steel bunk in a hall packed with hundreds of men trying to claw their way back from the edge of the same cliff. I recognized faces from the old days. Plenty of them spoke English. And every last one of them welcomed me in with open arms, the way men in those rooms always do: no questions, no judgment, take an empty bunk, you're with us now.

I didn't share my story with a single soul. I let them think I was just another drunk who took a bad tumble, and left it there. Some things you don't dump onto a crowded room. Some things you can barely bring yourself to put on paper.

***

Except I have to back up, because the real shockwave hit while I was still locked in detox.

One afternoon, guards were marching our row down the corridor toward the dining hall when I caught sight of someone near the kitchen line.

Zaul. 

Big as life, standing right there on two good legs.

My jaw literally dropped. I had buried this man in my mind months ago. I had grieved him, cursed him, forgiven him, and laid him in a ditch beside Glenda — and here he was, flesh and blood, wearing a grey rehab t-shirt and wiping off a tray. 

*“Zaul!”* 

The name ripped out of my throat before I could stop it. His head whipped around, his eyes popped wide, and he rushed across the tile and threw both arms around my shoulders in a bear hug.

I hugged him back. And in that exact same heartbeat, every muscle in my arm wanted to drive a fist straight through his nose.

"What the hell *happened* to you?" I asked.

Turns out my suspicions were only half right. There *had* been dangerous people hunting him down — that part was legitimate — but they never laid a finger on him. What took Zaul down was Zaul. He’d spiraled into a black hole of high-stakes gambling and heavy drugs, fled to Mexico City, and spent six straight months running wild until the federales finally threw him in a cage. 

And then Omar — my Omar, the good one across the street — had driven all the way down, pulled whatever strings he had to pry him out of that Mexican jail, hauled him back to Mexicali, and checked him into his own clinic to dry out. Zaul had been living as a patient in this building for four months.

He started apologizing immediately, throwing out excuses and regrets as fast as he could talk.

"You have no idea," I told him, staring at his clean-shaven face. "You have no earthly idea the hell you dropped me into when you disappeared."

*“Hey, listen, brother,”* he whispered, a flash of that old, slick grin breaking through the institutional haircut, *“we can still do this. We can get the call center fired back up. Start it fresh.”*

"Good luck," I said quietly, "finding another solar company in the States willing to run paper the way they did. That was lightning in a bottle, Zaul. But... yeah. We'll see."

In my head, the answer was already set in stone: *Not a chance in hell. Not with you, not in this room, not ever again.* But when a ghost hugs you, you don't lay all your cards on the table.

Then Zaul's grin faded, and he looked at me closely for the first time. 

*“So what about you, man? What happened? Why are you back in here?”*

"It's a long story," I said, looking toward the heavy steel doors. "Maybe I'll tell you someday. Right now, I just need to see Omar."
